# Full Script — Beowulf: The Hall, the Mere, and the Dragon

**Owner-controlled.** Production roles never edit this file. No compensation
number, generation note, or critic suggestion is ever written into it.

**How to read this document.** Each page is written as a scene first. The spec
under it is three lines and no more: mode (in the header), location, and why
the reader turns the page. Panel notes are staging, not metadata. **If the
metadata for a page is longer than the page's dramatized content, the story is
not being written.** Chronicle pages (Mode 4) add two non-rendering lines:
the reader's **Question** and the **Sources**.

**Text discipline.** Every string between quotation marks in a speaker,
`PROSE FIELD`, `CHRONICLE FIELD`, `LABEL`, `CAPTION`, `SOUND`, or
printed-object line renders exactly as written. Nothing else in this document
renders. `tools/script_audit.py` and `tools/verify.py` parse this format, so
keep it exact.

# MOVEMENT I — THE HALL THAT SANG · pp 1–7

---

## PAGE 1 · *spectacle*

**Location:** the fen below Heorot, night.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 100%.** A wide black fen under a cold rising moon:
pools of dark water, reeds, mist lying in long strips. In the foreground,
low among the reeds, something enormous is crouched — only the curve of a
huge stooped grey-green back, a long arm resting in the water, and two eyes
glowing like coals, turned away from us toward the horizon. Far off, on a
hill in the top third of the page, a great hall blazes with light from its
open doors, its gold roof catching the firelight. Thin threads of light and
the shapes of music drift from it across the fen. Everything is blue-black
except that distant gold and the two coals.

> **TITLE CARD** *(top, over the night sky, above the hall)*
> "BEOWULF
>
> The Hall, the Mere, and the Dragon"

> **CAPTION** *(lower left, over dark water)*
> "In the fens of Denmark, where nobody went after dark, lived something that hated the sound of music."

**Why you turn the page:** something in the dark is listening to that hall, and it hates what it hears.

---

## PAGE 2 · *dramatic*

**Location:** Heorot, night, a feast.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** Inside Heorot by firelight: a long hall with a
fire burning down the middle, carved pillars, shields hung on the walls,
benches crowded with feasting warriors. At the far end on his high seat,
KING HROTHGAR — tall, very thin, a long flowing white beard to his waist, a
gold circlet, a crimson robe — raises his arms to the hall. Beside him
stands QUEEN WEALHTHEOW, tall, auburn braids under a white veil, a green
gown, holding a great gold cup in both hands. On a low stool at the king's
feet sits UNFERTH, lean and dark with a short pointed black beard, in black
and silver. Behind the king's shoulder, an old bald warrior with a grey
braided beard (unnamed) laughs.

> **CAPTION** *(top left, over the dark rafters)*
> "King Hrothgar of the Danes had built the greatest hall in the world. He named it Heorot."
>
> **HROTHGAR:** "Drink, all of you! Tonight Heorot belongs to everyone in it. Wealhtheow, my queen, the first cup is yours to give."
>
> **WEALHTHEOW:** "Then I give it to the king who built this hall."

**Panel 2 — 25%.** Unferth, on his stool on the right, rises halfway and
lifts his own cup to the hall with a showman's flourish, pleased with
himself.

> **CAPTION** *(top of the panel)*
> "At the king's feet sat Unferth, his spokesman, who always had the cleverest thing to say."
>
> **UNFERTH:** "A hall this fine deserves a fine speech, my lord. Luckily, I've written one."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Two beats in one strip. Left: the king's poet, an old
man with a small harp, singing by the fire. Right: outside the open doors,
in the blackness beyond the light, two coal-red eyes, low down, watching.

> **CAPTION** *(over the poet)*
> "The king's poet sang of how the world was made."
>
> **CAPTION** *(over the darkness outside)*
> "Out in the dark, something heard every note."

**Why you turn the page:** the happiest hall in the world doesn't know it is being watched.

---

## PAGE 3 · *illustrated prose*

**Location:** Heorot, the same night, after the feast.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 70%.** The great double doors of Heorot, barred with
iron, bursting inward in a storm of splinters. The long hearth-fire is
blown flat. Across the doorway falls an enormous shadow, man-shaped and
wrong: too tall, arms too long, hands ending in hooks. We see only the
shadow and the coal-red eyes in the doorway; the sleeping warriors on the
benches are dark shapes under blankets, nobody yet awake. No one is harmed
in the picture.

> **PROSE FIELD 1** *(top of the page, over the dark rafters)*
> "That night, when the feast was over and the warriors lay asleep on the benches, it came up out of the fen. Later, people gave it a name: Grendel.
>
> The doors of Heorot were barred with iron. Grendel touched them with one claw, and they burst inward like kindling. The fire in the long hearth bent flat. He was across the hall before the first man woke."

**Why you turn the page:** the monster is inside the hall with the sleeping men.

---

## PAGE 4 · *dramatic*

**Location:** Heorot, the morning after.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** Heorot in grey morning light: benches
overturned, blankets trailing, a gold cup lying on its side on the floor, the
doors hanging off their hinges. In the doorway, huge clawed footprints in the
mud lead away down the hill toward the fen. HROTHGAR stands in the middle of
it, leaning on his staff, his face grey. WEALHTHEOW beside him. UNFERTH on
the right, sword already drawn. The old bald warrior with the grey braid
stands behind the king, one hand gripping Hrothgar's shoulder.

> **CAPTION** *(top left)*
> "In the morning, thirty men were gone from Heorot. None of them was ever seen again."
>
> **CAPTION** *(beside the old bald warrior)*
> "Beside the king stood his oldest friend, who had fought next to him in every battle of his life."
>
> **HROTHGAR:** "Thirty of my men. Thirty! And I slept through it, in my own house."

**Panel 2 — 25%.** Unferth steps forward, on the right, sword held high.

> **UNFERTH:** "Then tonight nobody sleeps, my lord. I'll fill this hall with every sword in Denmark. Let it come back and meet them."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Close on Wealhtheow, on the left, and Hrothgar, on the
right, looking at the clawed tracks.

> **WEALHTHEOW:** "And if swords can't stop it?"
>
> **HROTHGAR:** "Then I built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into."

**Why you turn the page:** the Danes are going to fight it with swords, and the queen doubts that swords will be enough.

## PAGE 5 · *illustrated prose*

**Location:** Heorot and the Danes' village, over twelve winters.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 60%.** Heorot at night under snow, its gold roof
white, its doors shut, not one light in it. Below the hill, a scatter of
small houses with barred shutters, a thin glow in each. The moon over the
fen.

**Panel 2 — 40%.** By day, inside the empty hall: HROTHGAR alone on his
high seat, gaunter and older, his beard longer, staring at the cold hearth.

> **PROSE FIELD 1** *(top of the page, over the snowy sky)*
> "Unferth filled the hall with swords. Grendel came anyway. Whoever stayed in Heorot after dark was gone by morning, swords and all. Soon nobody stayed.
>
> For twelve long winters the greatest hall in the world stood empty every night. The Danes slept in little houses far from it and barred their doors. Old King Hrothgar sat alone in his high seat by day, and grew older, and waited for someone, anyone, to help.
>
> Sailors carried the story across the sea."

**Why you turn the page:** the story is crossing the sea, looking for someone.

## PAGE 6 · *dramatic*

**Location:** the Geats' harbour, across the sea.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** A harbour of turf-roofed houses and beached
longships under a grey sky. On the dock, a pair of sailors (unnamed) tell
their story to a crowd, one making claws of his hands. Sitting on an
upturned boat, listening, is BEOWULF, nineteen: very tall and broad but
still rangy, a broad open clean-shaven face, a crooked nose, a notch scar
through his left eyebrow, long tawny-gold hair tied back at the nape, a
deep sea-blue cloak. His grin is gone. He is very still.

> **CAPTION** *(top left, over the sky)*
> "In the land of the Geats, across the sea, the sailors' story reached a young man named Beowulf."
>
> **CAPTION** *(below it)*
> "When he was a boy, the whole country had laughed at him: too big, too slow, always asleep by the fire. Nobody expected anything of Beowulf."

**Panel 2 — 25%.** Beowulf stands up, on the left, frowning, to the sailors
on the right.

> **BEOWULF:** "King Hrothgar? Years ago that king saved my father's life. He paid a debt for him that nobody else would pay."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Beowulf jumps up onto the upturned boat, grinning now,
one fist raised, to a crowd of young Geat warriors around him.

> **BEOWULF:** "Now it's my turn. I'm going to Denmark to fight his monster. Who's coming with me?"
>
> **CAPTION** *(bottom right, over the raised hands of the crowd)*
> "Fourteen of them said yes."

**Why you turn the page:** the boy everyone laughed at is going to fight the thing no army could stop.

## PAGE 7 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Who are the Danes and the Geats, and where in the world is all this happening?

**Sources:** *Beowulf* (Cotton Vitellius A.xv) for Danes, Geats and the sea crossing; Lejre excavations (Tom Christensen, Roskilde Museum, 1986–2004; the hall at Fredshøj, about 48 m long, 2004–09); the *Chronicon Lethrense* (about 1170) on Lejre as the royal seat of Hrothgar's family, the Skjöldungs; Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* (731) I.15 on the Angles, Saxons and Jutes coming to Britain from these coasts.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 70%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum
with a ruled margin. The dominant image is a hand-drawn ink-and-watercolour
map of the lands around the North Sea and the Baltic about the year 500:
the Danish islands and peninsula, the coast of southern Sweden across the
water, the North Sea, and the eastern coast of England at the left edge.
A dotted sea-road joins the Geats' coast to the Danish island of Zealand. A
tiny drawing of a long hall marks Lejre. A red-and-lapis interlace
initial with a touch of gold begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(left of the map, on plain vellum)*
> "Who were the Danes and the Geats? Look at the map. About fifteen hundred years ago, the Danes lived on the islands and coasts of what is now Denmark. The Geats lived across the water, in what is now southern Sweden. Between them lay the sea, and the sea was their road."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below the map, on plain vellum)*
> "In old Danish stories, King Hrothgar's family ruled from a place called Lejre. There, archaeologists have found the post-holes of great wooden halls, one almost fifty metres long. And the old poem this story comes from was made far away, in England, by people whose ancestors had sailed there from these very coasts."

> **LABEL** *(on the map, over Denmark)*
> "Danes"

> **LABEL** *(on the map, over southern Sweden)*
> "Geats"

> **LABEL** *(on the map, beside the hall drawing)*
> "Lejre"

> **LABEL** *(on the map, at the left edge)*
> "England"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows where Heorot stood and how far Beowulf has to sail, and the next page puts him on the sea.

## PAGE 14 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** What was a hall like Heorot really like, and why did everybody sleep in it?

**Sources:** *Beowulf* 64–85 (the building of Heorot), 1232–1250 (warriors sleeping on the benches with shields above their heads), 35 and 352 (*bēaga brytta*, "ring-giver"); hall excavations at Lejre and Gudme (Denmark) and the reconstructed halls at West Stow and Cheddar (England); mead, Old English *medu*, a drink fermented from honey.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 70%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. The dominant image is an ink-and-watercolour cutaway of a
great timber hall, its roof lifted away: a long hearth down the middle with
smoke rising through a hole in the roof, benches along both walls, shields
and spears hung above them, a high seat at the far end, great double doors.
Tiny figures feast at one end; at the other, the same benches are covered
with straw mattresses. A red-and-lapis interlace initial with a touch of
gold begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(above the cutaway, on plain vellum)*
> "What was a hall like Heorot? For the people of the North, a great hall was the heart of the kingdom. A fire burned down the middle of the floor. Warriors feasted on the benches, drank mead made from honey, and at night pulled out straw mattresses and slept right there, with their shields on the wall above them."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below the cutaway, on plain vellum)*
> "The king was called the ring-giver. He handed out gold rings, and every warrior who took one promised to fight for him. So a hall was the one warm, bright place in a dark country, full of music, gifts and promises. That is why a monster breaking in at night was the most frightening story anyone could tell."

> **LABEL** *(on the cutaway, at the fire)*
> "the long hearth"

> **LABEL** *(on the cutaway, at the far end)*
> "the high seat"

> **LABEL** *(on the cutaway, over the mattresses)*
> "benches by day, beds by night"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows what a cup and a promise mean in a hall — and the queen is coming down the hall with the cup.

## PAGE 36 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Why did Unferth's sword have a name, as if it were a person?

> "a pattern-welded blade"

> **LABEL** *(beside the helmet)*
> "the Sutton Hoo helmet, about 625"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows what a named sword meant — and the story is about to jump fifty years.

## PAGE 42 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Why does a dragon sleep on a pile of gold inside an old grave?

**Sources:** *Beowulf* 875–900 (the scop's song of Sigemund and the dragon), 2231–2277 (the hoard laid in the barrow); *Völsunga saga* (13th century), Sigurd and Fafnir; the Sutton Hoo ship burial (excavated 1939, Mound 1, about 625); the Staffordshire Hoard (found 2009 by Terry Herbert; about 4,600 items and fragments, mostly gold and silver war-gear).

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. The dominant image is an ink-and-watercolour cutaway of a
grassy grave mound on a headland, its inside opened like a museum drawing:
a stone chamber heaped with gold — cups, a helmet, a shield boss, rings — and
curled around it, drawn in thin ink like a figure in an old manuscript, a
small serpent-dragon. A red-and-lapis interlace initial with a touch of gold
begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(above the cutaway, on plain vellum)*
> "Why does a dragon sleep on gold? In the old stories of the North, dragons guarded treasure hidden in the graves of people who died long ago, and only a hero could win it. The poet at Heorot sang of one: Sigemund. In later stories it was his son, Sigurd, who killed the dragon Fafnir."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below the cutaway, on plain vellum)*
> "And people really did bury treasure with their kings. In 1939, under a mound at Sutton Hoo in England, diggers found the shape of a whole ship packed with a king's gold. In 2009, a man with a metal detector found thousands of pieces of gold war-gear in a field in Staffordshire."

> **LABEL** *(on the mound)*
> "a barrow"

> **LABEL** *(at the chamber)*
> "the treasure chamber"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows what the old king is walking toward — a hoard in a grave, and the thing that guards it.

## PAGE 57 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** How do we still know this story, after more than a thousand years?

**Sources:** British Library, Cotton MS Vitellius A.xv (the Nowell Codex, copied by two scribes, dated about 975–1025); the Ashburnham House fire, 23 October 1731; G. J. Thorkelin's transcripts (1787) and first edition (1815); J. R. R. Tolkien, "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics" (1936) and *The Hobbit* (1937), chapter 12, Bilbo's stolen cup; Tolkien, *Letters*, no. 25 (1938), on *Beowulf* as one of his "most valued sources".

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. The dominant image is a museum study in ink and watercolour
of an open page of the Beowulf manuscript: dense dark lines of old English
handwriting, the edges of the page browned, scorched and crumbled away in
places. A red-and-lapis interlace initial with a touch of gold begins the
first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(above the manuscript, on plain vellum)*
> "How do we still know Beowulf's story? Because of one book. Every word of the poem comes from a single copy, written out by two scribes in England about a thousand years ago."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(beside the manuscript, on plain vellum)*
> "In 1731, the library where it was kept caught fire. The book was saved, but its edges were scorched, and over the years crumbs broke away, taking some words with them forever. You can see it today in the British Library in London."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 3** *(below the manuscript, on plain vellum)*
> "A professor named J. R. R. Tolkien loved this poem all his life. In his book The Hobbit, a thief creeps into a sleeping dragon's hoard and steals a golden cup. Sound familiar?"

> **LABEL** *(beside the manuscript)*
> "the Beowulf manuscript, about 1000"

**Why you turn the page:** the book is over, and the reader knows the story came to them through fire too.
