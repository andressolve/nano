# Volume Mandate — Beowulf: The Hall, the Mere, and the Dragon

The craft authority for this book is the workspace method in `../../method/`,
starting with `00-CRAFT-MANDATE.md`. This file carries only what is specific to
this volume. **It relaxes no method rule.** If a future editor adds a clause
that lowers a number, widens a tolerance, or softens a gate, that clause must
name the rule it relaxes and why, in the clause itself.

## The adaptation stance — say it first

> This volume is an adaptation. Fidelity to the source is not a goal, not a
> tiebreaker, and not a defense. Every element earns its place by what it does
> for this reader in this book, or it is cut.

The poem is a thousand years old, survives in one scorched manuscript, and
spends half its length on feuds between families the reader will never meet.
Those go. What stays is what has kept it alive: a hall full of song, a thing
in the dark that hates the song, a young man nobody rated, and an old king who
walks out alone to meet a dragon.

## Why this book has Chronicle pages

The readers' request carried from King Arthur Vol I (2026-10-12): a story that
is not boring, that also tells them what was going on — the world, the
history, the myth. King Arthur proved the form. Beowulf is the oldest long
poem in English, and almost everything around it is a real object you can
still see: the great halls at Lejre, the Sutton Hoo helmet and lyre, the
Staffordshire Hoard, and the one burned manuscript in the British Library.
The context is unusually concrete and unusually wonderful.

## The Chronicle — voice and register

**Voice.** Carried unchanged from King Arthur Vol I. An unseen narrator who
talks straight to the reader in the present tense, like the best museum guide
alive: curious, a little conspiratorial, specific. Names, dates, places,
numbers, real objects. **Context, not verdict** (owner, 2026-10-13): it opens
the world around the story — how people lived, feasted, fought and sang; what
a mead-hall, a named sword, a dragon's hoard meant to the first listeners;
where the poem came from and the things you can still go and look at — and
never spends a page arguing whether Beowulf really lived or dragons really
flew. At most one light clause of that kind, never a page's point and never
its opening. It is never a character in the story and never in the story's
voice. It never says "it is important to know."

**Register.** Each Chronicle page is **a page from a chronicle**, in the
register King Arthur Vol I proved: warm ivory vellum, a ruled margin,
iron-gall brown ink, one illuminated initial — here in the Northern style,
red and lapis **interlace knotwork** with a touch of gold, the kind drawn in
English monasteries about the time the poem was copied — and one dominant
image in ink and watercolour wash: a map, a real artifact painted as a museum
study, a cutaway, a scroll timeline. No painted full-bleed scene, no story
character. The reader must know at a glance they have stepped out of the
story.

**The run of questions** (each answered by one Chronicle page, each raised in
words by the pages before it): Who were the Danes and the Geats? · What was a
mead-hall? · Who sang this story? · Why did a sword have a name? · Why does a
dragon sleep on gold? · How did this story survive a thousand years?

## Volume constraints

- **Deaths are real and never drawn.** Grendel carries men away; the dragon
  burns; Beowulf dies. No blood, wound, body or eating is drawn, and the poem's
  gore (Grendel devouring a sleeper, the head on the cliff, Grendel's head
  carried home) is cut. A man taken by Grendel is shown as an empty place, a
  dropped cup, a shadow at a door. The cost is shown in the faces of the
  living. (Carried from *Three Kingdoms* and *King Arthur*.)
- **Grendel's claw may hang in the hall.** It is the proof of the victory and
  the most famous image in the poem. It is drawn as a monster's limb — a long
  dark scaled arm ending in hooked claws, hung high under the gable, its
  shoulder end lost in shadow. No blood, no torn flesh.
- **The monsters never speak.** Grendel, his mother and the dragon have no
  balloons and no captions in their voice. They are terrible because they
  cannot be argued with.
- **Beowulf's death is shown with dignity.** Closed eyes, Wiglaf's hand on his,
  the helmet set down. No wound drawn, no venom drawn.
- **Names cost.** Eight people have names. The coast-guard, the herald, the
  fourteen companions, the swimming rival, Hrothgar's sons, his old friend,
  Beowulf's uncle the king, the thief and the eleven men are unnamed, unlocked
  and have no balloons.

## The specific failure this volume must avoid

**The strongman, and the epilogue.**

The tempting book is a monster-of-the-week brawler: a slab of muscle with a
grin punches three monsters, and the dragon at the end is a sad coda for an
old man. That Beowulf has no inside, and the poem's real power — youth and
age, the boast and the reckoning — is the engine written out.

In this book young Beowulf is **nineteen, hungry, and a little too loud**: a
young man who was laughed at as a boy and has never forgotten it, who boasts
because he needs the world to say his name, who is genuinely funny and
genuinely brave and does not hear Hrothgar's warning because he is young. A
page where young Beowulf is serene or modest is a script-gate blocker. Old
Beowulf is **seventy, tired, and still too proud to ask for help** — and the
book is not finished with him until he needs it. The dragon movement gets the
same pages, the same dialogue and the same care as Grendel; it is the
climax, not an appendix.

The second failure is the one King Arthur named: **context leaking into
scenes.** No character explains Danish kingship, mead-halls, pattern-welded
swords or burial customs. Story pages contain only what a character's
immediate situation needs; every bit of history waits for its Chronicle page.
A character explaining history is a script-gate finding.
