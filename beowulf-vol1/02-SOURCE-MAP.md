# Source Map and Cut List

**A source map with an empty cut list is not finished.** Cutting comes first:
before combining, reordering, or inventing, ask what can simply go.

Line numbers follow the standard editions (Klaeber); translations consulted
are Gummere (1910) and Clark Hall (1901), both public domain.

## The cut list — named, unapologetic, famous material included

### Whole sequences cut

- **Scyld Scefing's ship funeral** (1–52), the poem's famous opening. A
  beautiful image about a king the reader will never see again; the book opens
  on the thing in the fen instead. (The ship-burial idea returns on Chronicle
  5, where Sutton Hoo makes it real.)
- **The Danish genealogy** (53–63) and every other genealogy.
- **The Finnsburh song** (1063–1159) — a whole tragedy sung at the feast.
- **Heremod, the bad king** (901–915, 1709–1722), Hrothgar's cautionary
  example; the warning is given straight, in Hrothgar's own words.
- **The Sigemund song** as a story-within-the-story: reduced to one line — the
  poet sings "the old song of the hero who killed a dragon". Sigemund is named
  only on Chronicle 5.
- **Thryth and Offa** (1931–1962).
- **The homecoming to Hygelac** (1888–2199): the report, Freawaru and the
  Heathobards, the gifts. One prose line covers the return.
- **The Swedish wars**, Ongentheow, Ravenswood, Onela and Eadgils, Hygelac's
  Frisian raid in detail, Beowulf's swim home with thirty suits of armour
  (2354–2396, 2425–2509, 2910–3007).
- **The "last survivor" who buried the hoard** (2231–2270): reduced to one
  prose sentence about who left the gold.
- **The curse on the gold** (3051–3075): the gold is buried again, useless; no
  curse.
- **The messenger's prophecy of the Geats' ruin** (2900–3027).
- **Grendel's descent from Cain** (102–114): cut from the story. The
  Christian poet and his pagan heroes may get a clause on a Chronicle page.
- **Grendel devouring Hondscioh** (739–745), **Aeschere's head on the cliff**
  (1417–1421), **Grendel's head carried to Heorot** (1635–1650): cut.

### Characters cut

- Hygelac (as a named character) · Hygd · Wulfgar · the coast-guard (as a
  speaker) · Aeschere (as a named character) · Breca (as a named character) ·
  Hondscioh · Hrothulf · Hrethric and Hrothmund (as named characters) ·
  Freawaru · Ecgtheow (as a character) · Hrethel · Weohstan · Eofor, Wulf ·
  Ongentheow · Onela · Eadgils · Heardred · the last survivor · the messenger.

### Characters merged

- **Hygelac + Heardred + every Geatish king between → "his uncle, the king"**,
  who falls in a war across the sea in one prose sentence, so the Geats ask
  Beowulf to rule.
- **Wulfgar + the coast-guard → silent Danish guards** who lower a spear and
  point the way.
- **The ten cowards + Beowulf's whole retinue → "eleven men"**, one crowd, no
  faces, no balloons.

### Why the biggest cut pays for the volume

Cutting every feud, genealogy and sung digression means the book has exactly
two kingdoms, one king on each side of the sea, and no names a reader has to
hold that do not walk on stage. It deletes the poem's single biggest barrier
to a first-time reader — the sense that the story keeps stopping to talk about
somebody else — and frees the pages for what the poem does best and these
readers came for: three fights, one rivalry, one warning and one young man who
stays.

## Protected claims

- **On Chronicle pages only, the facts.** Every claim traceable to a named
  source on the page's `**Sources:**` line. Dates with "about" where they are
  uncertain. Legend called legend, archaeology called archaeology — but no page
  is spent adjudicating whether the poem happened (context, not verdict).
- The six Chronicle pages are not optional and are not the first cut if the
  book runs long.

## Sensitive material and its treatment

- **Death:** real and constant in the poem; never drawn (see the mandate).
  Beowulf's own death is the book's last great scene and is given its room.
- **Grendel's raids:** men are *carried off*; the reader sees the empty place.
  No eating.
- **The claw:** hung in the hall, drawn as a monster's limb (see the mandate).
- **Religion:** the poem's Christian poet keeps thanking God. The characters
  may say "thank God" where natural; the book neither preaches nor erases.
- **The cowards:** judged by Wiglaf in the poem's own terms — they keep their
  lives and lose their names — without cruelty.
