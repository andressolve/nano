# Volume Architecture — 56 pages

The North in the age of legend, about 500 AD as the poem imagines it: the
Danish island of Zealand with Hrothgar's hall, and across the sea the coast of
the Geats. A few months in Movements I–V; then a jump of fifty years; then a
few days. Five places matter: the fen and its black lake, Heorot, the sea
between, Beowulf's own hall, and the dragon's barrow on the headland.

Story pages are numbered with the book; the six Chronicle pages (C) sit at the
chapter breaks and are part of the page count.

---

## I — THE HALL THAT SANG · pp 1–6

**Emotional entry:** dread. **Exit:** a ship's prow, and a young man grinning
at the sea.

Night in a fen. Something huge in the reeds lifts its head: far off on a hill
a hall blazes with light, and a harp is playing. Inside, old King Hrothgar of
the Danes has built Heorot, the greatest hall in the world; his queen
Wealhtheow carries the cup down the benches, his sharp-tongued spokesman
Unferth sits at his feet, and the king's poet sings how the world was made.
At the edge of the light, the thing listens and hates every note. One night
the doors burst inward. In the morning: benches overturned, a dropped cup,
thirty places empty, and huge clawed tracks going down into the fen. Unferth
swears the king's champions will guard the hall tonight. They try, night
after night; twelve winters pass; the greatest hall in the world stands empty
every night, and Hrothgar grows old waiting. Across the sea, in the land of
the Geats, sailors tell the story on the docks — and a big young man named
Beowulf, whom the whole country laughed at when he was a boy, the one who
slept by the fire, hears it. King Hrothgar once paid the blood-price that
saved Beowulf's father. Beowulf chooses fourteen companions: *Who's coming
with me?*

**The turn:** a monster empties the greatest hall in the world for twelve
years; across the sea, the young man nobody rated decides to go.
**Page-turn out:** he has never crossed the sea, and he has promised to fight
something no army could.

**Chronicle 1 · p 7 — "Who were the Danes and the Geats?"** A map of the
North about 500 AD: the Danes on the island of Zealand, the Geats on the
coast of what is now southern Sweden, the stretch of sea between them, and
across the North Sea the land the poem was written in, England, where the
great-grandchildren of people from these coasts settled. At Lejre on Zealand
archaeologists found the posts of great halls, one nearly fifty metres long
— the kind of hall the poem calls Heorot. Why the English, centuries later,
still sang about the old country.

## II — THE STRANGER WHO BOASTED · pp 8–13 (+ the cup and the vigil, pp 15–16, after Chronicle 2)

**Emotional entry:** swagger. **Exit:** a young man lying awake in the dark
with no sword.

The ship runs before the wind to Denmark's white cliffs. Danish guards lower
their spears at the strangers, read them, and point the way up to the
gold-roofed hall. Hrothgar remembers the boy's father: *I paid your father's
debt when no one else would.* Beowulf: *Then let me pay it back.* And since
Grendel fights without a sword, so will he. Unferth, jealous of anyone the
king smiles at, asks the hall whether this is the same Beowulf who lost a
swimming race at sea and nearly drowned showing off. Beowulf tells the true
story — seven nights in a winter sea in armour, sea-beasts dragging him
down, nine of them killed by morning — and then the sting: *if you were half
as fierce as your words, Unferth, Grendel would never have dared come back.*
The hall roars; Unferth sits in silence. Queen Wealhtheow carries the cup to
the stranger and thanks God someone has come; Beowulf vows in front of
everyone that he will win or die in this hall. At nightfall Hrothgar does
what he has never done in twelve years: gives the hall to another man for
the night. The Danes go. The Geats lie down. Beowulf takes off his mail,
hands away his sword, and lies awake.

**The turn:** the boast is made in front of everyone; there is no way back.
**Page-turn out:** something is crossing the moor.

**Chronicle 2 · p 14 — "What was a mead-hall?"** (moved at script round 3 to the scene break after the flyting, so the vigil runs straight into the fight) A cutaway of a great hall:
the long hearth down the middle, the benches that became beds, the high seat,
the doors; mead brewed from honey; the lord called the "ring-giver" because
he gave gold for loyalty, and the gold made a promise; the hall as the one
warm, safe, lit place in a dark country — which is exactly why a monster
attacking it was the most frightening story anyone could tell.

## III — GRENDEL · pp 15–22

**Emotional entry:** terror. **Exit:** a hall full of sleeping people for the
first time in twelve years.

Grendel crosses the moor in the mist and the door gives at a touch of his
claw. He takes one sleeping Geat before anyone wakes — and reaches for the
next sleeper, who is Beowulf, awake, who catches his arm. Grendel has never
felt a grip like it, and for the first time in his life he is afraid. The
hall shakes; benches fly; the Geats' swords bounce off his hide as if it were
stone. Beowulf will not let go. Grendel tears himself free — and leaves his
arm behind — and flees howling into the fen. Morning: the claw hung high
under the gable, the Danes following the tracks to a black lake that churns.
Hrothgar embraces Beowulf: *From now on I'll love you like a son.* Unferth
stands under the claw and, for once, says nothing. That night the feast: a
gold banner, a helmet, mail, a sword, eight horses; the poet sings the new
song of Beowulf and the old song of the hero who killed a dragon; Wealhtheow
gives him the great gold neck-ring and asks him to be kind to her small sons.
The Danes sleep in Heorot again.

**The turn:** the monster is beaten by a young man's bare hands, and the hall
sings again.
**Page-turn out:** something else is coming up out of the lake.

**Chronicle 3 · p 23 — "Who sang this story?"** The poet of the hall — a
*scop* — with his harp: the six-stringed lyre found in the Sutton Hoo ship
burial, painted as a museum study. Songs like this were sung aloud from
memory, the beat carried by words that start with the same sound. And the
riddle-names the singers used, called kennings: the sea is the
*whale-road*, the body the *bone-house*, a sword the *battle-light*.

## IV — THE MOTHER IN THE MERE · pp 24–32

**Emotional entry:** shock. **Exit:** a young man coming up out of black water
holding a melted sword's hilt.

Night, and something comes up out of the lake: Grendel's mother, older and
stronger, for her son's arm. She takes Hrothgar's oldest friend and the claw
and is gone before swords are drawn. At dawn, Hrothgar, broken: *my friend is
dead, and there are two of them.* Beowulf: *It's better to go after him than
to cry for him. I'll go.* The ride to the mere: a place so frightening a
hunted stag would rather die on the bank than jump in. At the water's edge
Unferth steps forward and offers his own sword, Hrunting, which has never
failed anyone who held it — and Beowulf, taking it, asks that if he doesn't
come back his own sword go to Unferth. He dives. All day he sinks; she seizes
him at the bottom and drags him into her hall under the water, where a strange
fire burns. Hrunting strikes her and does not bite — the first time it has
ever failed. She is on top of him with a knife; his mail holds. On the wall
he sees a sword made by giants, too heavy for any other man; he swings it,
and she falls. Light floods the cave, and the giant's blade melts away like
ice in spring until only the hilt is left in his hand. On the bank the Danes
have given up and gone home. Only the fourteen Geats are still waiting.

**The turn:** he goes where nobody could go, and the sword that fails him is
the sword of the man who mocked him.
**Page-turn out:** what does Beowulf do with Unferth's failed sword?

## V — THE OLD KING'S WARNING · pp 33–34

**Emotional entry:** honour. **Exit:** a ship heading home with a hero on it
nobody will ever laugh at again.

In Heorot Beowulf hands Hrunting back to Unferth: *a good sword* — not a word
of blame. Unferth, who expected to be shamed, can't speak. Hrothgar turns the
giant's hilt in his hands and gives Beowulf the warning he will need fifty
years from now: *don't let pride grow in you; your strength is for a little
while; then sickness, or the sword, or fire, or old age will come for you.*
Beowulf, nineteen and unbeatable, nods and doesn't really hear it. At the
shore the old king weeps and kisses him, knowing they will not meet again.
Beowulf sails home with a ship full of gold — and gives it away.

**The turn:** the hero who has won everything is given the one warning he
cannot hear yet.
**Page-turn out:** fifty years.

**Chronicle 4 · p 35 — "Why did a sword have a name?"** A sword in this world
took weeks to make: iron rods twisted and hammered together so the blade
showed patterns like flowing water. It was passed from father to son, and it
had a name — Hrunting, Naegling — like a person. Painted as museum studies:
a pattern-welded blade, and the helmet from the Sutton Hoo ship burial, with
little boars over its eyes, like the boar-helmets the poem describes on the
Geats.

## VI — FIFTY WINTERS · pp 36–40

**Emotional entry:** age. **Exit:** an old king with an iron shield, walking
out of the burned town at dawn.

Fifty winters. Beowulf's uncle, the king, fell in a war across the sea; the
Geats asked Beowulf to rule, and he kept them safe for fifty years. He is old
now, white-bearded, still enormous. On a headland by the sea, a runaway
hiding from a beating crawls into an ancient grave mound and finds a dragon
asleep on a mountain of gold. He takes one golden cup. The dragon wakes,
knows, and at nightfall comes out burning: villages, halls, and Beowulf's own
great hall go up in flame. In the ashes, the old king meets his young kinsman
Wiglaf, seventeen, who has never been in a real fight, and orders a shield
made all of iron, because wood will burn. *I'll go myself.* That night at the
forge Wiglaf asks him the question he has wanted to ask all his life: *were
you ever afraid?* — and Beowulf tells him about the boy everyone laughed at,
and about a young man who boasted in a hall across the sea. Wiglaf asks to
come. Beowulf lets him come with eleven men, to watch: *the dragon is mine.*

**The turn:** the old king decides to fight the dragon alone, as he fought
Grendel — the same pride Hrothgar warned him of, and the same courage.
**Page-turn out:** the barrow on the headland, and smoke rising from it.

**Chronicle 5 · p 41 — "Why does a dragon sleep on gold?"** In the old
northern stories, dragons guard treasure buried in the graves of the long
dead, and only a hero can take it from them — the poem's own singer
remembers one, the hero Sigemund, whom later storytellers turned into Sigurd,
the slayer of Fafnir. People really did bury kings with their gold: the ship
grave at Sutton Hoo, found in 1939 under a mound in Suffolk, and the
Staffordshire Hoard, thousands of pieces of gold war-gear found in a field
in 2009. A cutaway of a barrow with its treasure chamber, painted as a museum
drawing.

## VII — THE DRAGON · pp 42–55

**Emotional entry:** resolve. **Exit:** a mound on a cliff, seen from a ship
far out at sea.

On the headland the old king sits and remembers: the boy by the fire,
Grendel's arm, Hrothgar's warning — *fire, or the sword, or old age. He was
right. It's all three.* He tells his men to wait: *this isn't your fight.*
He shouts the dragon out, and it comes in a river of fire. His iron shield
holds, but his sword bites too little, and the fire wraps him. Ten of the
eleven run for the woods. Wiglaf turns on them: *we promised him, in his
hall, when he gave us these helmets* — and goes alone into the fire. His
wooden shield burns to nothing; he gets behind the old king's iron one.
Beowulf strikes with all his strength and his sword Naegling shatters. The
dragon's teeth close on the old king's neck. Wiglaf, his hand burned, strikes
low, into the belly; the fire dies; Beowulf draws his knife and cuts the
dragon in two. They killed it together. Beowulf sits against the barrow and
knows the poison is in him. He asks Wiglaf to bring out the treasure so he
can see what he bought for his people. Wiglaf, in the hoard: gold everywhere,
a golden banner glowing in the dark. Beowulf's last words: he gives Wiglaf
his gold collar, his helmet and his mail — *you're the last of our family*
— and asks for a mound on the headland so sailors will call it Beowulf's
Barrow. He dies. The ten come creeping back, and Wiglaf tells them what they
have lost: *you'll keep your lives. You've lost your names.* The pyre, and a
woman singing. The barrow raised on the cliff, the gold buried with him, as
useless as it ever was, and twelve riders circling it singing that he was the
kindest of kings and the most eager for fame. Last story page: years later, a
ship far out at sea, a sailor pointing at the mound on the cliff.

**The turn:** the hero who always fought alone wins his last fight only
because someone stayed — and leaves his name to the one who stayed.
**Page-turn out:** how did we ever hear about him?

**Chronicle 6 · p 56 — "How did this story survive a thousand years?"** One
book. Every word of *Beowulf* comes from a single manuscript copied by two
scribes about a thousand years ago. In 1731 the library holding it caught
fire; the book was saved, but its edges were scorched and crumbled, and some
words are gone forever. An Icelandic-Danish scholar copied it out in 1787;
it was first printed in 1815. Today it is in the British Library. And a
professor named J. R. R. Tolkien loved it all his life — and when he wrote a
thief stealing a cup from a sleeping dragon named Smaug, everyone who knew
*Beowulf* smiled. The page shows the scorched manuscript painted as a museum
study.

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
The first raid (p 3) and its morning (p 4) are separate. The arm torn off (p
19) and the claw in the morning (p 20) are separate. Hrothgar's grief (p 25)
gets its own page before the plan. Hrunting failing (p 29) and the giant's
sword (p 30) are separate. Hrunting returned (p 33) gets its own scene.
Beowulf's death (p 50) and the cowards' return (p 51) are separate; the pyre
(p 52) and the barrow (p 53) are separate.

**One dominant turn per page.** Every page in `07-PAGE-CONTRACT.md` names the
one thing that changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence
comes back here, not to the prompt.

**Chronicle placement.** Every Chronicle page follows a page that lands a
movement's turn and precedes a page that opens a new movement. None sits
between a turn and its consequence: there is no Chronicle between IV and V,
because Hrunting's failure is answered in Heorot. Chronicle 4 is the bridge
across the fifty years. None sits inside the dragon fight.
