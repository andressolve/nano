## 1. Cold read

A thing in the Danish fens hates the music coming from King Hrothgar's gold-roofed hall, Heorot. One night it bursts in and takes thirty men. Unferth, the king's smooth spokesman, fills the hall with swords, and that fails. For twelve winters the hall stands empty every night. Sailors carry the story across the sea to Beowulf, nineteen, whom his own people once laughed at as the slow boy by the fire. Hrothgar once saved Beowulf's father, so Beowulf sails with fourteen men to pay the debt.

In Heorot Beowulf boasts that he will fight Grendel bare-handed. Unferth mocks him over a swimming race he lost as a boy. Beowulf tells the true story (seven nights at sea, nine sea-monsters killed) and hands the insult back. The queen, Wealhtheow, tests him, and he vows to win or die.

That night Grendel takes one sleeping Geat before Beowulf can move, then grabs Beowulf and meets a grip he cannot break. He tears free and runs off to die, leaving his arm behind. Hrothgar adopts Beowulf in his heart. Beowulf grieves the man he lost. Wealhtheow asks him to be kind to her small sons.

That same night Grendel's mother takes back the arm and Hrothgar's oldest friend. Unferth, who stares at the black lake and will not go near it, lends Beowulf his sword Hrunting. Beowulf dives. The sword fails, he kills her with a giant's sword that melts, and he comes up holding its hilt to find only his thirteen Geats still waiting. He returns the failed sword to Unferth without blame. Hrothgar warns him against pride: sickness, the sword, fire, or old age will come for everyone. Beowulf hears none of it and sails home a hero.

Fifty years later, a runaway steals a cup from a sleeping dragon. The dragon burns the coast, starting with old King Beowulf's hall. His cousin's boy, Wiglaf, asks him at the forge whether he was ever afraid. "Every time." Too proud to need anyone, Beowulf goes out alone. The ten men he brought run. Wiglaf does not ("You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway."). Together they kill the dragon. Poisoned, Beowulf says, "Fifty years I never needed anyone, Wiglaf. Today I did, and you came." He leaves Wiglaf his ring and his name, and dies. Wiglaf shames the cowards and buries the gold with the king. Sailors still point at Beowulf's Barrow.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed:**
- **p16:** I was pulled out of the story at the moment of highest suspense in the first half (see Finding 1).
- **p37:** "Fifty winters went by", then two kings die, and then Beowulf reigns "for fifty years". I did the sum and it doesn't add up (Finding 2).
- **p53:** I turned back to p50 and p52. Beowulf said twice that the gold was for his people, and one page later Wiglaf buries it without a word about that wish (Finding 3).
- **p24:** "Beowulf was sleeping in a guest house that night" answers my question, but only after I'd asked it (nonblocking).
- **p12 against p6:** If the boy killed nine sea-monsters at fourteen, why did the whole country think he was slow? I worked out the answer myself (they believed he lost the race), and the script never says it (nonblocking).
- **p52:** "You're the last of our family now." Isn't Wiglaf's father alive? On p41 he says "My father said…" (nonblocking).
- **p14:** Wealhtheow's eyes go to "two small boys". On a first read I didn't know who they were until p22 (nonblocking).

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good script, and in places it is better than good. Movement by movement:

**I. The Hall That Sang (pp 1–7).** The opening image, a crouched thing turned toward a distant gold hall that "hated the sound of music", is a real hook. p4 has heat. Hrothgar's guilt ("And I slept through it, in my own house") and his bitter last line ("Then I built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into") are a king's voice, not a summary. Unferth's p2 line ("Luckily, I've written one") gives him a personality before the plot needs him. p6 earns its want: the debt, the laughed-at boy, "Who's coming with me?"

**II. The Stranger Who Boasted (pp 8–16).** The best-written movement in the first half. The flyting (pp 11–13) has true want-from on both sides: Unferth wants to shrink the man the king smiled at, and Beowulf wants the hall. It has subtext ("you've had a lot of mead"), and Beowulf's grin drops into plainness at the right moment. The attribution-strip test passes: Unferth, Beowulf, Wealhtheow and Hrothgar sound like four different people. Wealhtheow on p14 ("every one of them is gone") is a test, not a welcome. The one failure is structural: the Chronicle on p16 (Finding 1).

**III. Grendel (pp 17–23).** The fight is enacted on the page, not reported. Grendel's fear on p18 is a smart beat. p21's grief turn is real: an empty bench with a folded cloak, and Beowulf answering "love you like a son" by talking about the man he lost, which is subtext. It's slightly dutiful in wording ("But I wanted to bring all my men home"), but it lands. Unferth alone under the claw is good.

**IV. The Mother in the Mere (pp 24–32).** p27 is the best small beat in the book. Unferth stares at the water and won't go near it, then lends the sword. "Come back, Geat. I want my sword" is subtext a ten-year-old will feel without having it explained. The underwater spectacle works. p32's "Did you think I wasn't coming back?" is the young Beowulf the volume mandate asked for: loud, funny, alive.

**V. The Old King's Warning (pp 33–36).** p33 turns the rivalry around with grace. p34 plants the list that the climax pays off. "He nodded, and didn't hear a word" is exactly right for a nineteen-year-old.

**VI. Fifty Winters (pp 37–42).** The forge (p41) is the heart of the book. "Were you ever afraid?" / "Every time." / "Everyone heard me. After that, I couldn't run away." In one confession it changes what the boasting meant. Old Beowulf has appetite (pride, refusing help), so the dragon movement is not an epilogue.

**VII. The Dragon (pp 43–57).** It earns its climax. p44's "I'm too old to start now. Stay." p48's "I told you to stay on the hill!" / "…So am I. I came anyway." p50's "Old Hrothgar left nothing out" and "Today I did, and you came." The structure pays off cleanly: the warning, the fear and the pride each come back at the right page.

The one place where the heat drops out is p53. Wiglaf's burying of the gold contradicts the dying king's stated wish and never admits it. That throws away the most interesting choice in the last movement (Finding 3).

## 3. Mandatory findings (blockers)

### 1. p16 (Chronicle): placed between the setup and its consequence, at the book's tightest suspense

**The failure.** p15 ends with Beowulf lying unarmed in the dark, "He did not close his eyes", and its turn line is "Grendel always comes." The next page is a museum cutaway of a hall. Then p17 opens with Grendel on the moor. The page's own turn line admits the suspension: "he is on his way."

Craft mandate §3 says a Chronicle page goes "only at a chapter break … never between a turn and its consequence." The architecture names Movement II's turn as "the boast is made … there is no way back." The fight on pp 17–19 is that boast's consequence, and p15 is the held breath just before it. A Chronicle here is a commercial break inside a held breath.

**What the reader has to repair.** They have to re-enter the dread they built over pp 14–15 after a lecture-register page about straw mattresses and ring-givers.

**Revision.** Move the hall Chronicle to sit between p13 and p14.
- p13 lands a scene's turn: "Beowulf has won the hall", the benches pounding, Unferth silent.
- p14 opens a new scene: late afternoon, the cup ceremony.
- The question ("what was a hall like Heorot, and why did everybody sleep in it?") is fully raised by pp 2–13.
- The page's content (the ring-giver, the cup, promises made in the hall, benches turned into beds) directly primes Wealhtheow's cup and vow on p14 and the Geats lying down on p15.
- pp 14 → 15 → 17 then run unbroken: vow, night, Grendel.

Rewrite the Chronicle's turn line to match ("…and the queen is coming down the hall with the cup"). Move the Movement II/III boundary in `03-VOLUME-ARCHITECTURE.md` and renumber. If the owner prefers, an equally valid break is immediately after p5 (the hall abandoned, before the Geats). In that case the map Chronicle should not also sit only one page later.

### 2. p37 (illustrated prose): the fifty-year arithmetic contradicts itself

**The failure.** "Fifty winters went by. Beowulf's uncle, the king, was killed in a war across the sea, and then his son after him. The Geats came to Beowulf and asked him to be their king, and he was — for fifty years."

If fifty winters passed in total (and the ages match: 19 on p6, about 70 now), then two reigns came first and Beowulf cannot have ruled for fifty of those years. p44 and p50 lean on "fifty years" again, so the number matters.

**What the reader has to repair.** A careful ten-year-old does the sum and either assumes a mistake or stops trusting the timeline in the movement that most depends on time.

**Revision.** Keep "Fifty winters went by." Change the reign clause to "…asked him to be their king, and he was, for all the long years after. In all that time, no enemy dared to attack the Geats." p44's "For fifty years they've sung that I never needed anyone" stays true, because the songs began at Heorot. Don't touch p50's "Fifty years I never needed anyone."

### 3. p53 (dramatic): Wiglaf overrides the dying king's stated wish without acknowledging it

**The failure.** Beowulf says on p50, "I want to see what I've won for our people", and again on p52, "That's for our people. They'll need it when I'm gone." One page later Wiglaf says, "He won this gold for us, and we let him fight alone. Not one of us has earned a single ring of it. It goes into the ground with him." p55 then confirms the gold "lies there still."

Wiglaf is reversing his king's last request two pages after he died, and the script writes it as though there were no conflict. That loses the heat. The most interesting choice in the coda is Wiglaf deciding that the people his king wanted to provide for don't deserve it. But the reader isn't shown a choice, only an apparent contradiction. "We let him fight alone" is also unclear coming from the one person who didn't.

**What the reader has to repair.** "But Beowulf said the gold was for the people!" The reader has to invent Wiglaf's reasoning, or decide the script forgot p52.

**Revision.** Make it an enacted choice that names the wish it refuses. Panel 3 could read:

> **WIGLAF:** "He said this gold was for his people. His people ran. Not one of you has earned a single ring of it."
> **WIGLAF:** "It goes into the ground with him, where nobody who left him can ever touch it."

Optionally add one beat of cost: Wiglaf's hand on the gold ring at his neck, the one piece he keeps because it was given. Keep p55's "as Wiglaf said."

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **Mode balance.** Story pages split 22 dramatic / 12 illustrated prose / 17 spectacle, which is 43% / 24% / 33% against roughly 60 / 20 / 13. The volume mandate allows a monster epic to lean toward spectacle, and I found no spectacle page whose turn is a person's choice in conversation. The fight pages are action, and that's correct. This is noted, not blocked. If a page is ever converted, p29 panel 3 ("Then I'll do it with my hands!") and p45 are the nearest candidates.
2. **Spectacle text load.** The audit flags 31–39 words on pp 17, 18, 24, 31, 38, 39 and 49. Each caption carries causal information (p17 "too fast", p24 the guest house, p49 "together"). Keep the words; don't cut causal language. Just note that these pages are action sequences, not silences.
3. **p12 and p6: tie the wound to the race.** One clause would turn the laughing into a story rather than a premise, for example at the end of p12: "Nobody at home believed him. All anyone remembered was that Beowulf lost." It also makes p41's "everyone laughed at me" sharper.
4. **p24: the guest house arrives after the event.** Plant it on p22 instead ("…and Beowulf was given a guest house of his own"). Then p24's last line can be a reaction rather than an alibi.
5. **p14: the two small boys aren't identified.** Wealhtheow's look goes to children the reader can't place until p22. One clause would put her want on the page early: "…and every one of them is gone. My sons sleep in this hall too."
6. **p41 and p52: Wiglaf's father.** "You're the last of our family now" reads oddly if his father is alive. "My father, before he died, said our family has always stood by you" fixes it and adds weight to Wiglaf's reason.
7. **p40 panel 2.** "Tell the smith to make me a shield all of iron. Wood will burn. And find me that runaway." This is one balloon of orders. It's acceptable because it's a single line in old Beowulf's dry voice and it plants the shield. If it can carry character, so much the better (for example, "Wood will burn. I'm not ready to.").
8. **p23: harp or lyre.** The field says "a small harp" and the label says "the Sutton Hoo lyre." Make it "a small harp called a lyre" so the field and the label agree.
9. **p21.** "I'm only sorry he got away" sits slightly against p19's "ran howling into the dark to die". It works because Beowulf doesn't know Grendel died, but the reader might pause. Something like "I wanted to finish him here, in front of you" keeps the regret without the clash.
10. **Five-word audit, all six pass.**
    - "Looking for me?" (p18): one referent, forceful.
    - "Yes, my lord." (p34): answers complete advice, and the caption carries the meaning.
    - "I'll gather the army!" (p40): a complete thought.
    - "Every time." (p41): answers a complete question in the same panel.
    - "He needs us now!" and "Then I'll go by myself." (p47): each is uniquely determined by the line before it.
11. **Chronicle pages, other than p16.**
    - All six open on their question in words.
    - None debunks the story.
    - None carries a causal step. With all six removed, the story still passes the paraphrase test. "Whale-road" on p56 can be worked out from context, and p47's promise doesn't depend on p16.
    - pp 7, 23, 36, 42 and 57 sit at true breaks.
    - p57 follows the story's landed last turn, and "Sound familiar?" is the right register.
    - No claim I believe to be false.
12. **Architecture.** Every page has one mode, one turn line and one dominant panel, and none has more than two locations. Text fits the budget. p2 (about 97 words) is the heaviest dramatic page and is within the ~105 ceiling with protected balloon lanes.
13. **Voice and the volume's named failures.**
    - Young Beowulf is never serene or modest.
    - Old Beowulf is proud until p50.
    - The monsters never speak.
    - No character explains history.
    - Hrothgar's oldest friend obeys the no-balloon rule and still registers through pp 2, 4, 22 and 24.
