I read the full script cold, start to finish, before opening anything else. After that I read `method/00-CRAFT-MANDATE.md`, `method/02-SCRIPT-LOOP.md` §3, `qa/script/audit-round-05.txt`, `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md` and `00-VOLUME-MANDATE.md`. ## 1. Cold read

A thing in the fen hates the music from King Hrothgar's golden hall, Heorot. It comes up one night and thirty men vanish. For twelve winters the hall stands empty after dark, and the old king waits. Across the sea, a young Geat called Beowulf hears the sailors' story. He was laughed at as a slow, sleepy boy. Hrothgar once paid his father's debt, and Beowulf sails with fourteen friends to pay it back.

At Heorot he boasts he'll fight Grendel bare-handed. Unferth, the king's sharp-tongued spokesman, mocks him over a lost swimming race. Beowulf tells the true story (five nights, a storm, nine sea-monsters) and wins the hall. The queen brings him the cup and tests him with her eyes on her small sons. He swears that tonight he wins or dies.

Grendel takes one Geat before Beowulf can move. Then Beowulf grabs his arm, and Grendel tears free, leaving the arm behind. The claw hangs over the doors, the hall feasts, the queen gives Beowulf her gold ring, and the Danes sleep in Heorot again.

That night Grendel's mother comes, takes back the arm and takes the king's oldest friend. Beowulf, asleep in his guest house, walks in cheerful the next morning straight into Hrothgar's grief. That is one of the best moments in the book. Unferth, shamed, lends him the sword Hrunting. It fails under the lake. A giant's sword kills the mother and melts down to its hilt. Beowulf comes up to find only his Geats still waiting. He gives the "failed" sword back to Unferth with praise. Hrothgar warns him that sickness, the sword, fire or old age come for everyone, and Beowulf doesn't hear a word.

Fifty years later Beowulf is an old king. A runaway steals a cup from a sleeping dragon, and the dragon burns the king's own hall. Old Beowulf refuses an army. In the forge he tells his cousin's boy, Wiglaf, that he was afraid every time. He also explains the old boast: once everyone had heard him, he couldn't run. He walks down alone, too proud to need anyone. His ten men run. Wiglaf comes anyway. Together they kill the dragon, but Beowulf has been bitten. He says "Today I did, and you came," gives Wiglaf his ring and dies. Wiglaf shames the deserters. The Geats bury the gold with their king rather than keep what his life paid for. Sailors still point at Beowulf's Barrow. A last Chronicle page tells how the poem survived a fire, and how a cup stolen from a dragon reached *The Hobbit*.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p55, first sentence:** "He had wanted the gold for them." I had to pause to work out who "He" was and who "them" were. The only person drawn on the page is Wiglaf. This is Finding 1.
- **p44:** "It's all three, all at once." I paused for a beat to map "the sword." Nonblocking (O2).
- **p24, after the attack:** were Wealhtheow's sons asleep in the hall that night? The script never says. Nonblocking (O1).
- **p53:** Wiglaf clasps his ring while saying nobody should wear the gold. There was a small hitch in what the gesture means. Nonblocking (O3).

Everything else read straight through without a reread.

## 2. Is it alive?

**Yes. This is a good book, and in long stretches a moving one.**

- **Movement I (pp 1–7).** It opens on something listening in the dark, which is exactly right. Hrothgar's line "Then I built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into" is a king's despair in his own voice. Page 6 gives Beowulf his want in one breath: the debt, plus the boy everyone laughed at.
- **Movement II (pp 8–14).** The flyting (the exchange of insults and boasts) has real heat. Unferth wants to shrink the stranger in front of the king. Beowulf wants the hall to say his name. "Unferth, my friend, you've had a lot of mead" is subtext and comedy in one line. The swimming story flips the scene and the hall erupts. Young Beowulf is loud, funny and hungry, never serene. Appetite passes.
- **Movement III (pp 15–23).** The queen's cup scene now has its engine: she wants a promise for her sons, and she gets one with an object. "Looking for me?" is the line Francisco will quote. The grief over the lost Geat (the empty bench, the folded cloak) keeps the victory from being cheap. Unferth with nothing to say under the claw is a lovely silent beat.
- **Movement IV (pp 24–32).** "But Grendel had a mother. And she had lost her son" is the best caption in the book. Page 25 is the strongest dramatic page: "I hope it was a peaceful night?" / "Don't speak to me of peace." The Hrunting thread repays itself on p27 with "Come back, Geat. I want my sword." That is pure subtext, Unferth asking to be forgiven without asking. Four spectacle pages under the lake each carry a distinct turn, with no padding.
- **Movement V (pp 33–36).** Returning the sword is grace you can see; "for the second time in his life" pays off p21. The warning scene works because Beowulf doesn't listen ("Yes, my lord" plus the caption). That gives the scene its heat: an old man trying to reach a boy who is already elsewhere.
- **Movement VI (pp 37–42).** The forge page (p41) is the heart of the book. "Every time" and "After that, I couldn't run away" recast the young man's boasting as a way of trapping himself into courage, without anyone saying so. Old Beowulf's "No army" is pride. He does not come across as noble.
- **Movement VII (pp 43–57).** The climax gets the same care as Grendel, as the mandate demanded. The proud lines on p44 set up "Today I did, and you came" on p50, and it lands. "Sickness too, then. Old Hrothgar left nothing out" is grim, dry and in his voice. Wiglaf's "So am I. I came anyway" is the engine said by the right person.

**Presence tests:**
- **Attribution-strip:** passes. Each speaker can be told apart without the name:
  - Hrothgar is formal and sorrowful.
  - Unferth is polished and barbed.
  - Young Beowulf is jokey and then suddenly plain.
  - Old Beowulf is dry and short.
  - Wiglaf is earnest and too fast.
- **Aphorism:** the borderline lines are "Don't let pride grow in you" (p34) and "It's better to go after the one who killed him than to cry" (p25). Each belongs to its speaker and is attached to an action or to a refusal to hear, so they pass.
- **Logistics:** there is no logistics dialogue.
- **Enacted, not narrated:** passes; every big event is drawn.

What still needs fixing is narrow. The page that carries the book's final moral decision (to bury the gold) opens on a pronoun that points at the wrong visible person.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**1. p55, PROSE FIELD 1, opening sentence: "He had wanted the gold for them."**

- **The failure:** a pronoun with no antecedent opens the page, and the only visible candidate is wrong.
  - The page shows one named figure: Wiglaf, riding at the head of the twelve, wearing the helmet and the ring.
  - The rendered text just before it (p54's caption) has no singular "he."
  - "Them" points forward to "the Geats" in the next sentence.
- **What a first-time reader must repair:** read aloud, the listener asks "Who's he? Wiglaf?" Wiglaf is the one in the picture. But two pages earlier he said nobody should wear the gold, so "Wiglaf wanted the gold" contradicts what the reader just saw. The reader has to read on to "their king," then go back and reassign both pronouns.
- **Why it blocks:** this sentence carries the book's final moral decision. The dying king's wish is overridden, and the reader must understand exactly whose wish it was and why. That is anti-terse rule 4 (protect antecedents) and story test 1 (effortless first read). "Recoverable after the next clause" is a revision flag, not a pass.
- **Prescription:** name him and name them. For example: "Beowulf had wanted the gold for his people. But when the Geats heard how their king had died, they would not keep gold he had paid for with his life." The second "he" is then safe. Change no other causal language.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

- **O1. pp 22–25: the sons and the oath.** On p15 Beowulf swore the sons would sleep in their father's hall "tomorrow night." On p22 they did, and that same night the mother came. The script never draws the connection, and a reader will ask whether the boys were hurt. This is free heat. On p25 Hrothgar could add something like: "My sons slept in this hall last night because you swore they could. She came anyway." That gives Beowulf a personal stake in going to the lake. At minimum, show the two boys safe among the torchlit Danes in p24 panel 3.
- **O2. p44: "It's all three, all at once."** "Fire" (the dragon) and "old age" (him) are clear. "The sword" needs a beat to read as "the fight." This is the ledger's signature line and the echo of p34 matters, so I would keep it. Optionally gloss it: "The dragon's fire, a fight, and an old man. It's all three, all at once."
- **O3. p53, panel 3.** Wiglaf grips his ring while saying "I'm not sure any of us should." The gesture can read as "including this ring," and on p55 he is still wearing it. One clause would settle it: "This ring he gave me with his own hand. The rest — I'm not sure any of us should." Or drop the clasp from the staging.
- **O4. Mode balance.** Over the 51 story pages the split is 22 dramatic / 12 prose / 17 spectacle, about 43 / 24 / 33, against a target of roughly 60 / 20 / 13. The volume mandate acknowledges a lean toward spectacle in a monster epic. I found no spectacle page whose turn is a person's choice and should be converted, and the book does not read under-dramatized. I record it rather than block on it.
- **O5. p49 staging: "his hand burning."** Wiglaf's burned hand never appears again. Either drop it from the staging or carry it (a wrapped hand on p50 or p53). This is staging only.
- **O6. Wealhtheow's sons.** Beowulf's "I'll be their friend as long as I live" (p22) is never touched again. That is acceptable for this volume, but a half-clause in p37's prose tying the ring to that promise would give the old man's thumb on the ring an inside.
- **O7. Audit warnings.**
  - Spectacle pages carrying 31–38 words (pp 17, 18, 24, 31, 38, 39): each caption carries causal information, so none should be cut.
  - p57, the book ending on a Chronicle page: p56 lands the story's final image first, and the Chronicle page reads as a coda.
- **O8. Chronicle pages** (pp 7, 14, 23, 36, 42, 57) all pass:
  - each sits at a true break, and none is inside the climax run;
  - each opens on its question in words, and the story before it raised that question;
  - each carries one idea and one dominant image;
  - each offers context, not a verdict;
  - none carries a causal step the story needs, and none spoils a later turn.

  I believe every claim is accurate (Lejre, mead, *scop*, the Sutton Hoo lyre and helmet, pattern-welding, Sigemund and Sigurd, the Staffordshire Hoard, the 1731 fire, Tolkien's cup). With every Chronicle page removed, the story still passes the paraphrase test. p14's ring-giver promise deepens p47 but is not needed for it, because Wiglaf says the promise aloud.
- **O9. The six balloons of five words or fewer.** All pass:
  - "Looking for me?" (p18): the reach on the previous page settles it.
  - "Yes, my lord." (p34): intentional, and the caption seals it.
  - "I'll gather the army!" (p40): complete thought.
  - "Every time." (p41): direct answer to a complete question in the same panel.
  - "He needs us now!" and "Then I'll go by myself." (p47): one visible referent, forceful.
- **O10. Ledger.** Its page ranges are stale, as the brief says. For example, it has the yellow shield burning on p46 and Wiglaf's confidence "before p45," but the shield burns on p48. Reindex after the gate.
