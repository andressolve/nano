## 2. Cold read

A hall full of song stands on a hill in Denmark. Down in the fen, something hates the music. On the first night it comes up the hill, the iron doors burst in, and thirty men are never seen again. King Hrothgar slept through it in his own house. His spokesman Unferth promises swords. The queen, Wealhtheow, asks the right question: what if swords can't stop it? They can't. For twelve winters the hall stands empty every night.

Sailors carry the story across the sea to Beowulf. As a boy his own people laughed at him for being big, slow and always asleep by the fire. Hrothgar once paid his father's debt, so Beowulf has a debt to repay and a name to make. Fourteen friends go with him. In Heorot he boasts that he will fight Grendel bare-handed. Unferth mocks him over a lost boyhood swimming race. Beowulf tells the true story (nine sea-monsters, seven nights) and turns the insult back on Unferth: if you were half as fierce as your words, Grendel would never have come back.

The queen carries him the cup and lets him see her sons, who have never slept a night in their father's hall. Beowulf swears to win or die. Grendel is too fast and takes one Geat before Beowulf can move. Then Beowulf grips him, and Grendel tears free, leaving his arm. At the morning reunion Beowulf grieves for the man he lost, and Unferth has nothing to say. That night Grendel's mother takes back the arm and the king's oldest friend. Unferth lends Beowulf his famous sword, which fails under the lake. A giant's sword kills her and melts. The Danes give up waiting; only the thirteen Geats stay on the shore. Beowulf hands the failed sword back to Unferth with nothing but praise. Hrothgar warns him privately that sickness, the sword, fire or old age comes for everyone. Beowulf is nineteen and doesn't hear a word.

Fifty years pass. A runaway steals a cup, and a dragon burns the old king's hall. Beowulf is seventy, still too proud to accept an army, and he tells his young cousin Wiglaf that he was afraid every time. On the headland he remembers the warning, says it aloud, and walks down alone. His ten men run. Wiglaf runs the other way: "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway." The sword breaks, the dragon bites, and the two of them kill it together. Beowulf says he needed someone and Wiglaf came, gives him the ring and dies. Wiglaf strips the cowards of their names. The Geats bury their king with all the gold, and sailors still point at the barrow.

I could tell all of that back after one read without notes, and that holds with every Chronicle page removed.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p15, Beowulf's oath.** "Then I'll be the last man who ever has to swear it." I reread to find what "it" was. (Finding 1.)
- **p55, the burial.** "They would not keep gold he had paid for with his life." I went back to p52 ("That's for our people. They'll need it when I'm gone.") and p53 ("He won this gold for his people"). (Finding 2.)
- **Small hesitations, none blocking:**
  - p21, "I wanted to finish him here, in front of you." It took a beat to read as regret that Grendel got away.
  - p33, Unferth says "Everyone here knows it failed you." No one was shown telling the hall.
  - p25, "Don't cry for him." Hrothgar is not staged in tears.

## 3. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book with a real pulse, and in several places it is moving. Movement by movement:

**I (pp 1–7).** The opening image, the two coals in the fen turned toward a gold hall, is a promise kept. Hrothgar's "Then I built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into" is a king's despair in his own voice, not a summary of it. Wealhtheow's four-word doubt does more than Unferth's whole speech. Beowulf's entry has appetite: he goes still, then climbs onto the boat. Heat is present.

**II (pp 8–14).** The flyting is the best-built scene in the first half. Each speaker wants something from the other, right there in the room:
- Unferth wants the hall laughing at the stranger.
- Beowulf wants the hall itself.

There is real subtext: "Unferth, my friend, you've had a lot of mead" is an insult dressed as courtesy. The flashback page earns its place as an answer, not as backstory. If you strip the speaker names, you can still tell Unferth (polished, performing) from Beowulf (jokes, then sudden plainness).

**III (pp 15–23).** Wealhtheow's test on p15 is the right instinct. Her eyes go past him to her boys, and "My sons have never slept one night in their father's hall" is a mother asking without asking. Beowulf's reply misses it (Finding 1). The fight run (17–19) is enacted, not narrated, and Grendel being too fast is a real cost. p21 is the scene I'd show anyone: the king weeping with joy, the hero looking at the empty bench, and Unferth under the claw "for once" with nothing to say. p22's "be kind to my sons… their father is old" is understatement with a knife in it.

**IV (pp 24–32).** "But Grendel had a mother. And she had lost her son." is a superb turn. Beowulf walking in cheerful on p25 ("I hope it was a peaceful night?") into Hrothgar's "Don't speak to me of peace" is real dramatic irony. Unferth's hand-over on p27 is pure subtext: he stares at the water he won't enter, and "Come back, Geat. I want my sword" is love said as a joke. The four-page underwater run is spectacle doing its job. p32's "Did you think I wasn't coming back?" is exactly the nineteen-year-old the mandate wants.

**V (pp 33–36).** The returned sword is the grace note, and "for the second time in his life" pays off p21. The warning scene works because Beowulf *doesn't* listen. The caption "He nodded, and didn't hear a word" is the hinge of the whole book.

**VI (pp 37–42).** The old king in the ashes stays proud ("No army. I killed Grendel with my own two hands"). The forge scene is the emotional engine of the second half. "Every time." and "Everyone heard me. After that, I couldn't run away" give the boasting its wound at last.

**VII (pp 43–57).** The climax gets the dialogue and the care the mandate demanded. "It's all three, all at once" leaves out sickness, so the reader notices before Beowulf does. p48's exchange is the best line in the book. "Fifty years I never needed anyone, Wiglaf. Today I did, and you came." lands the engine. Wiglaf's judgment of the ten has heat.

The ending's moral beat (the gold) is where the book briefly forgets what it set up (Finding 2).

**Voices.** Hrothgar is formal and old-song. Unferth is barbed, then ashamed. Young Beowulf is loud, then plain. Old Beowulf is dry and proud. Wiglaf is earnest and too fast. All five pass the attribution-strip test.

**Registers.** There is no logistics dialogue. p40's "a shield all of iron. Wood burns." is an old soldier's practicality and stays in character. There is no aphorism dialogue. p25's "It's better to go after the one who killed him than to cry" is general in form, but it is Beowulf's appetite talking and is followed at once by "Show me the lake."

**Serenity.** Young Beowulf is never serene or modest.

## 4. Mandatory findings

**1. p15: Beowulf's oath points to nothing and steps around the queen's real request.**
- **The failure.** Wealhtheow says: "I thank God someone has come. But men have sworn great things in this hall before, Beowulf." / "My sons have never slept one night in their father's hall." Beowulf replies: "Then I'll be the last man who ever has to swear it, my lady. Tonight I win, or I die in this hall."
- **Read aloud.** "Then" follows from the sons line, but "it" reaches back past that line to "sworn great things" two balloons earlier. A listener asks "Swear *what*?" This is the vow the page turns on ("he has sworn it in front of everyone"), and its object is a pronoun with no referent nearby. That breaks rules 4 and 7 of §7: protect antecedents, and let promises carry their object.
- **Lost want.** Wealhtheow's want in this room, right now, is a promise for her boys. The revised line "My sons have never slept…" makes that her strongest beat, and Beowulf answers something else. The reply looks like it was written for an earlier version of her line.
- **What a first-time reader must repair:** a pronoun two balloons back, and a link from "sons" to "swear" that the script never makes.
- **Prescription.** Make the oath answer the sons and give "swear" a stated object. For example:
  > **BEOWULF:** "Then tomorrow night they will, my lady. I swear it here, in front of everyone: tonight I win, or I die in this hall."

  This keeps the cockiness, gives "it" its following clause, and lets her want land. If you keep the "last man" idea, name it: "the last man who ever has to swear to kill Grendel in this hall."

**2. pp 52–55: the Geats bury the gold against the king's dying wish, and the book never says it knows.**
- **The failure.** Beowulf asks to see the gold on p50 ("I want to see what I've won for our people before I die"). On p52 he says "Good. That's for our people. They'll need it when I'm gone." Those are nearly his last words. On p53 Wiglaf repeats "He won this gold for his people" and denies it to the ten. Then p55: "When the Geats heard how their king had died, they would not keep gold he had paid for with his life… Inside it they laid… all the dragon's gold."
- **What a first-time reader must repair.** Three pages in a row build "the gold is for his people," and then the people bury every piece. The prose gives a motive, but it never admits that the choice goes against what he asked. The reader is left to decide whether that is a deliberate act of love or the book forgetting its own setup. This reader stopped and flipped back.
- **Side effect.** Wiglaf's sentence on the ten ("Not one of you will ever wear a single ring of it") goes quietly hollow, because now nobody will.
- **Why it matters.** This is the last decision in the story, made by the people the king died for. It should feel chosen, not like a contradiction.
- **Prescription.** Name the wish and the refusal in one clause, so the burial reads as grief that outranks obedience. For example, open p55's prose with:
  > "He had wanted the gold for them. But when the Geats heard how their king had died, they would not keep gold he had paid for with his life."

  Optionally, end Wiglaf's p53 line with his own refusal ("Not one of you will ever wear a single ring of it. I'm not sure any of us should."). That would make p55 the people agreeing with him rather than overruling the king off-page. Do not cut the burial. It is the right ending, and it only needs to know what it is doing.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

**Five-word audit (all six pass):**
- p18 "Looking for me?": complete, one referent (the claw reaching for him), forceful.
- p34 "Yes, my lord.": answers a complete warning, and the caption makes the emptiness the point.
- p40 "I'll gather the army!": complete thought, refused in the next balloon.
- p41 "Every time.": a direct answer to a full question in the same panel.
- p47 "He needs us now!": the referent is unmistakable.
- p47 "Then I'll go by myself.": answers "Not one of them turned around."

**Mode balance.** Over the story pages the split is 43% dramatic, 24% prose and 33% spectacle, against the 60/20/13 target. The volume mandate expects a monster epic to lean toward spectacle. I looked for spectacle pages whose turn is a person's choice and found none worth converting: the spectacle pages are the fights, the raids and the hoard. Every choice in the book is dramatized. I am noting the gap, not blocking on it.

**Spectacle word counts** (pp 17, 18, 24, 31, 38, 39 at 31–38 words). Each caption carries a causal step or the turn: "He was too fast…", "She took back her son's arm, and Hrothgar's oldest friend…". None should be cut.

**Staging and continuity, worth fixing before packets:**
- **The claw's position.** p20 hangs the claw *outside*, "High above the great doors, under the carved gable." p21 stages Hrothgar and Unferth "under the claw." p24 puts it *inside*: "the Danes asleep on the benches under the hanging claw… a vast dark shape reaching up to the claw." Pick one place, most simply inside above the doors where the sleepers can see it, and stage all three pages to match.
- **p53, the ring.** Panel 1 has "the gold ring in his fist"; panel 3 has "the gold ring at his neck" with his hand closed around it. Either let him put it on between panels or keep it in the fist.
- **p25, Hrothgar's tears.** Beowulf says "Don't cry for him," so stage tears on Hrothgar in panel 2 ("His face is wrecked" doesn't promise them).
- **p33, how Unferth knows.** He knows the sword failed. One clause in panel 1, or a caption ("That evening Beowulf told the whole hall what had happened under the lake"), would show how he learned it. It would also strengthen the grace: Beowulf told the truth and still praised the sword.

**Lines worth a look:**
- **p21.** "I wanted to finish him here, in front of you" takes a beat to read as regret that Grendel escaped. "I wanted him to die here, in front of you, not run off into the dark" would make it instant without losing the pride.
- **p52.** Wiglaf has no line on the death page. His silence works. If one line is added, it should be his, not more narration.
- **p56.** "Whale-road" is a lovely callback to p23. With the Chronicle pages removed it still reads as "the sea," so it passes.

**Chronicle pages:**
- **Accuracy and tone.** All six are accurate, at true breaks, and opened by their rendered question. None debunks, lectures, spoils or carries a causal step.
- **Placement.** p23 sits in the right place: the false calm of "the Danes slept in Heorot," then the step outside, then "But Grendel had a mother."
- **p36.** The question is earned more by p27 and p33 than by the page directly before it, which is acceptable.
- **p36 wording.** "Just like the boar-helmets in the poem" is slightly loose: the poem's boars are crests, Sutton Hoo's are on the eyebrows. "Like the boar-helmets in the poem" would be exact.
- **p42.** It tells a reader *why* the dragon movement feels mythic without telling them what happens. That is good context, not verdict.

**Ledger.** The state ranges are stale against the script (young pp 6–34, old pp 36–55; Wiglaf's shield "burns on p 46"; when the ring passes to him). This is a docs-only fix and does not affect the script.
