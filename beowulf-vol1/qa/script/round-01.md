## 1. Cold read

Something huge crouches in a Danish fen at night. It hates the music coming from a golden hall on the hill. The hall is Heorot, and King Hrothgar built it. One night the creature, Grendel, breaks in. By morning thirty men are gone. Swords don't stop him, and for twelve winters the hall stands empty every night. Across the sea, a young Geat named Beowulf hears the story from sailors. As a boy everyone laughed at him. Hrothgar once paid a debt that saved Beowulf's father, so Beowulf takes fourteen men and sails to Denmark. In Heorot, the king's spokesman Unferth mocks him over a swimming race he lost as a boy. Beowulf tells the real story (nine sea-monsters killed), turns the insult back on Unferth, and wins over the hall. He swears to the queen that he will win or die, then lies down unarmed. Grendel comes and takes one sleeping Geat. Beowulf grabs his arm and won't let go, and Grendel tears free and runs off to die, leaving the arm behind. The arm is hung over the doors, and there is a feast, with gifts and a gold neck-ring from the queen. Then Grendel's mother comes out of the black lake, takes back the arm, and kills Hrothgar's oldest friend. Beowulf dives into the lake with Unferth's sword, Hrunting. The sword fails. He kills her with a giant's sword that melts down to its golden hilt. He comes up, hands Hrunting back to Unferth with nothing but praise, ignores Hrothgar's warning about pride and death, and sails home a hero.

Fifty years later he is the old king of the Geats. A runaway steals a cup from a dragon's hoard, and the dragon burns the coast, including Beowulf's hall. Beowulf has an iron shield made and tells young Wiglaf at the forge that he was afraid every time. He goes to fight alone. Ten of his men run; Wiglaf comes. Naegling breaks, the dragon bites Beowulf, and Wiglaf stabs it in the belly so Beowulf can finish it. Beowulf dies of the poison. He gives Wiglaf his ring and asks for a barrow. Wiglaf shames the cowards, and the Geats burn the king and bury him, with the gold, on the headland. Sailors still point at Beowulf's Barrow.

I could paraphrase it after one read. It is clear, it moves, and it frightened me in the right places.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed:**

- **p2 / p4:** Who are the queen and the man on the stool? Both speak. Neither is named until p5 (Unferth, in prose), p10 (Unferth, in a caption) and p14 (Wealhtheow).
- **p11 / p12:** Unferth says "Seven nights." The prose says "five nights side by side." I flipped back to check which was right.
- **p15 → p17:** Beowulf "did not close his eyes," yet Grendel takes a Geat "before anyone could wake." Why did a wide-awake Beowulf let his man be taken? Nobody mourns that man, and the count stays "fourteen Geats" for the rest of the book.
- **p24 / p25:** "Hrothgar's oldest friend." Which one was he? I had to guess the bald laugher from p2. I felt the king's grief, but not a loss of my own.
- **p40:** "No army. I'll go myself." Why? It's pride or protecting his men, and the script never decides which.
- **p43:** "the old king finally understands the warning." He understands death. He does not understand pride, because he does the proud thing straight away.
- **p47:** "ten of the eleven men" run, Wiglaf is separate from them, so who is the eleventh?
- **p52:** "You're the last of our family now." This is the first I hear that Wiglaf is family.
- **p52 → p55:** He dies to win gold "for our people. They'll need it." Then they bury it, "as useless to people as it ever was." Why?

## 2. Is it alive?

**Movement I (pp 1–7): the danger is alive, the people are not yet.** Page 1 is a great opening image. "Something that hated the sound of music" is a real hook, and p3's prose ("Grendel touched them with one claw, and they burst inward like kindling") has heat. The Danes, though, trade ceremonial lines. On p2 Hrothgar toasts, Wealhtheow honours him, and Unferth says "There's no hall like it anywhere under heaven," which anyone at the table could say. Nobody wants anything from anyone. Page 4 is better: Wealhtheow's "And if swords can't stop it?" has subtext. Hrothgar's "Then God help us all" is a stock phrase. Beowulf's entrance on p6 works: the laughed-at boy, the debt, "Who's coming with me?" I wanted the next page.

**Movement II (pp 8–16): the best stretch in the book.** The flyting with Unferth on pp 10–13 is properly alive. Unferth performs for the hall, and Beowulf is grinning "but his eyes aren't." Then come "Unferth, my friend, you've had a lot of mead" and "I was busy killing sea-monsters," and then the drop into plain menace: "If you were half as fierce as your words…" That is voice, appetite and heat all at once. Page 14 (the cup) is the weakest page here. Wealhtheow thanks him, and he repeats the vow he made on p13. Page 15's prose is lovely ("He did not close his eyes").

**Movement III (pp 17–23): fast, physical, fun, with one moral hole.** "Looking for me?" will make both boys shout. The arm in the doorway is the image of the book. But a Geat is taken while our hero is lying awake, and the book never comes back to it. On p21 Beowulf's only regret is that Grendel "got away." A nineteen-year-old who wants the world to say his name could be a little callous here. If so, the script has to show it on purpose, not by accident. The feast on p22 earns its subtext: "be kind to my sons. They are small, and their father is old."

**Movement IV (pp 24–32): strong.** "But Grendel had a mother. And she had lost her son." is a perfect turn. Beowulf's "I hope it was a peaceful night?" walking into the wreck is dramatic irony a child will feel in the stomach. Unferth's "Come back, Geat. I want my sword." is the best line of the book: love said as a joke. The dive, the failed sword and the melting blade all land. The emotional centre, the dead friend, is hollow because we never met him (Finding 2).

**Movement V (pp 33–36): good.** The return of Hrunting is generous and well staged, and the call-back ("for the second time in his life") works. Hrothgar's warning is speechy by design, and it's rescued by "Yes, my lord" / "didn't hear a word."

**Movement VI (pp 37–42): the forge scene (p41) is the best dramatic page in the second half.** "Were you ever afraid?" / "Every time." / "So I crossed the sea and boasted in a king's hall… After that, I couldn't run away." That is the boast-as-commitment theme, enacted, in the old man's own dry voice. It had me.

**Movement VII (pp 43–57): the spectacle lands and the relationship doesn't.** Wiglaf's stand on p47 is alive, and his p53 verdict ("You'll keep your lives. But you've lost your names.") is earned and devastating. But the volume mandate asks for an old king "still too proud to ask for help — and the book is not finished with him until he needs it." In this script he never needs it in a way anyone says or shows. The boy disobeys him and saves him, and the moment that should break the old man open passes in two captions ("Wiglaf struck low…", "They had killed the dragon together"). His last words are about gold, gear and a mound, not about the one who stayed. The engine of the book is "a hero's name is kept alive only by the one who stays beside him." It sits in the structure and is never felt between the two people. Then the ending buries the gold he died for, with no reason given. I felt the pyre and the sailors on p56. I did not feel the death as much as the forge scene had set me up to.

**Overall:** this is a good draft with a real voice, better than competent. The young Beowulf is never serene, and Unferth has a full arc in five pages. It does not yet pay off its own engine, and it has continuity holes a careful ten-year-old will catch. I return REVISE for the findings below, not for deadness.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**1. pp 2, 4, 5, 10, 14: two speaking principals go unnamed for pages.**
On p2 the caption names only Hrothgar and Heorot. Wealhtheow speaks on p2 and p4 but isn't named until the p14 caption. Unferth speaks on p2 and p4, is first named in p5's prose ("Unferth filled the hall with swords"), and is only identified as "the king's spokesman" on p10. On p2 his line ("And I'll say what everyone is thinking. There's no hall like it anywhere under heaven") is in no particular voice. It reads as generic flattery, not the clever, jealous man the flyting needs.
*Reader repair:* For four to twelve pages the reader holds "the queen" and "the man on the stool" with no names. On p5 they have to work out that "Unferth" is the sword-drawer from p4.
*Revision:* On p2, name both in the first exchange, through address or caption. For example, Hrothgar: "Wealhtheow, my queen — the first cup is yours to give." Add a short caption on Unferth: "At the king's feet sat Unferth, his spokesman, who always had the cleverest thing to say." Rewrite Unferth's p2 line so it is unmistakably his: sly, performing, a little jealous of the toast (for example, a compliment that is really about himself). On p4, have Hrothgar answer him by name ("Unferth, fill it, then.").

**2. pp 2, 4, 24, 25: Hrothgar's oldest friend dies without the reader ever meeting him.**
He appears only in stage directions ("an old bald warrior… laughs", "stands behind the king"). The p24 caption gives his relationship at the moment he is taken, and p25 asks us to share Hrothgar's grief ("He stood beside me in every battle of my life").
*Reader repair:* The reader has to scan back through crowd figures to work out who died, and the loss is felt only second-hand. The mandate's rule that "the cost is shown in the faces of the living" needs a face the reader knows.
*Revision (keeping the mandate's no-name, no-balloon rule for him):* Identify him in rendered text before the mother's raid. On p2 or p4 add a caption such as "Beside the king stood his oldest friend, who had fought next to him in every battle of his life." On p4 he grips the king's shoulder in the wreck. On p21 or p22, give him one warm, visible beat: he is the first Dane to clasp Beowulf's arm, or he raises the cup to him. Then p24's "Hrothgar's oldest friend" is recognition, not new information.

**3. pp 11–12: "Seven nights" against "five nights."**
Unferth: "Seven nights in the water, and you lost." The prose: "For five nights they swam side by side. Then a storm… By morning…"
*Reader repair:* I stopped and flipped back. A reader can't tell whether Unferth is exaggerating on purpose or the book made a mistake, and here the difference matters, because the page exists to set up "the true story."
*Revision:* Make the numbers agree (for example, "For five nights they swam side by side… on the seventh morning the waves carried him ashore"). Or, if the exaggeration is meant, have Beowulf correct it on p11 or p13 so the reader knows it is Unferth's lie.

**4. pp 15, 17, 21: Beowulf is awake, yet a Geat is taken "before anyone could wake," and nobody mourns him.**
Page 15 renders "He did not close his eyes." Page 17 renders "He snatched the first sleeping Geat before anyone could wake, and reached for the next." Page 21 has Beowulf say only "I'm only sorry he got away."
*Reader repair:* The text contradicts itself. Beyond that, the reader has to decide on their own whether the hero let a friend die, and the book never gives that death any aftermath. It is a consequential beat missing its aftermath, and the protagonist's reaction is missing too.
*Revision:* (a) Fix the logic on p17, for example: "He was too fast. Before Beowulf could move, Grendel had snatched the Geat on the nearest bench — and reached for the next." Or make the choice explicit: Beowulf keeps still to learn how the monster moves, and pays for it. (b) Give the dead man an aftermath on p21. Beowulf's regret should be a person, not a trophy: "I wanted to bring you all of him. And I wanted to bring all my men home." Or add a panel of the Geats round his empty bench. Grief can sit alongside the triumph, and it makes the grin cost something.

**5. pp 26, 32, 35 (from the setup on p8): the count is still "fourteen Geats" after one has died.**
Page 8 says "Fifteen men aboard." After p17, pp 26, 32 ("Only the fourteen Geats were still there", which is rendered) and 35 still count fourteen.
*Reader repair:* A child who counts will catch it, and it quietly erases the man from Finding 4.
*Revision:* After p17, say "thirteen" everywhere, rendered and staged. Or use "his Geats" in rendered text and draw thirteen. The first option is better, because the number itself mourns him.

**6. pp 40, 52: Wiglaf's kinship is first mentioned at the deathbed.**
Page 40: "You're Wiglaf. You were a baby the last time I looked." Page 52: "You're the last of our family now, Wiglaf." The ledger and architecture both call him the kinsman whose want is "to be worthy of his family's old king." None of that is on the page before p52.
*Reader repair:* The inheritance (ring, helmet, mail, "last of our family") arrives as new information at the most emotional moment. The reader has to absorb a relationship and a death on the same page, and Wiglaf's reason for standing in the fire ("he's my family") was never available to them.
*Revision:* Plant it on p40 in Beowulf's own voice: "You're Wiglaf — my cousin's boy. You were a baby the last time I looked." Then let Wiglaf's want show at the forge on p41, for example "My father said our family always stood by you." Page 52 then lands as fulfilment, not surprise.

**7. pp 40, 41, 43, 44: the climax decision has no clear motive, and the warning's "pride" half is never paid off.**
On p34 Hrothgar warns about two things: "Don't let pride grow in you" and that "sickness, or the sword, or fire, or old age" will come. Page 43 says "the old king finally understands the warning," and p44 delivers only the mortality half ("It's all three, all at once"). On p40 he refuses the army with no reason ("No army. I'll go myself."), and on p44 he sends them away with "It's mine, the way Grendel was mine." The book never tells the reader whether going alone is the pride Hrothgar warned against, or courage, or sparing his people. That is exactly the fork Movement V was built to set up.
*Reader repair:* The reader has to decide on their own what the old man's choice means. The p34 caption ("didn't hear a word") promises that the second half will show he finally heard it, and the book only half does.
*Revision:* Pick the meaning and put it in a person's mouth.
- If it is pride, which is the architecture's intent ("the same pride… and the same courage"), let Wiglaf push back on p40 or p44 ("You'd take an army for anyone else, my lord.") and give Beowulf an answer with subtext that half-admits it ("Fifty years they've sung that I never needed help. I'm too old to start.").
- Then pay it off in Finding 9, where he admits he needed the boy.
- Either way, give the p40 refusal a reason the reader can hear.

**8. pp 41, 43, 47, 53, 55: the eleven men don't add up.**
Page 41 renders "Come, then, with eleven men." Page 43 stages "eleven armed men and WIGLAF." Page 47 stages "ten of the eleven men are running… WIGLAF, alone." Page 53 has "The ten men." Page 55 has "Twelve riders."
*Reader repair:* One armed man vanishes. An artist following the staging will draw eleven plus Wiglaf, then ten running and one standing who never acts.
*Revision:* Make the staging consistent. Either all eleven run (pp 47 and 53 say "eleven") with Wiglaf the twelfth, or Wiglaf is one of the eleven (change p43 to "ten armed men and WIGLAF"). Then check that p55's twelve riders follow from whichever you choose.

**9. pp 47–52: the climax relationship is captioned, not enacted. The old king never needs or acknowledges the boy, and the requested conversions to dialogue were not made.**
- On p48 the only spoken line is "Get behind me, boy!"
- Page 49, where Wiglaf saves his king, is all caption ("Wiglaf struck low… The flames died"; "They had killed the dragon together"), with no dialogue at all.
- Pages 50–52 cover poison, treasure and bequests, and there is no line where Beowulf registers that the boy disobeyed him and came.
- The volume mandate says old Beowulf is "too proud to ask for help — and the book is not finished with him until he needs it."
- The engine says a hero's name is kept alive "only by the one who stays beside him."
- The architecture explicitly asked the script to convert the bite (now p49), the alarm (p24) and the woman's song (now p54) to voiced pages. None of them speaks.
- The result is 41 / 24 / 35 over the story pages against a roughly 60 / 20 / 13 target, and the shortfall is concentrated in exactly the pages where the turn is a person's choice.

*Reader repair:* The most important emotional turn of the volume, the proud old man saved by the boy he told to stay back, happens in narration. The reader has to infer what it means to both of them.
*Revision:*
- Make p48 or p49 dramatic. When Wiglaf arrives, Beowulf should see the disobedience ("I told you to stay." / "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I.").
- Make the save on p49 an exchange, not a caption, ideally keeping a picture-only panel for the bite.
- On p50 or p52, give Beowulf the line the whole book is waiting for, in his dry old voice: he needed someone after all, and the one who stayed is the one who will carry his name. For example: "Fifty years I never needed anyone. Today I did, and you came." Put it before the bequest, so the ring is a consequence and not a formality.
- Consider letting the p54 woman's song carry one rendered line of lament.
- Add a page if this needs one. Length is never a reason to compress.

**10. pp 52, 55: the gold is buried against the dying man's wish, and no reason is given.**
On p52 Beowulf says "That's for our people. They'll need it when I'm gone." On p55 the prose says "Inside it they laid the ashes of their king and all the dragon's gold, and the gold lies there still, as useless to people as it ever was."
*Reader repair:* The reader has to invent why the Geats ignored their king's last request. The narration then tells them what to feel about it ("useless"), which hides a missing causal step under a nice-sounding phrase.
*Revision:* Dramatize the choice. Give Wiglaf a line or a panel on p53 or p55 saying why. For example, after shaming the cowards: "He paid for this gold with his life. None of us is worth spending it. It goes into the ground with him." Then the burial is an act of honour the reader understands, and the narrator doesn't need to call it useless. Or keep the gold for the people, as Beowulf asked, and drop the line. Either way, the reader must not be left to solve it.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

- **p4.** The five-word audit: "Then God help us all." is complete and unambiguous, but it's a stock phrase anyone could say. A line in Hrothgar's own voice (slow, formal, a little sad) would make it his.
- **Five-word audit, the rest.** "Looking for me?" (p18), "Yes, my lord." (p34, anchored by the caption), "I'll gather the army!" and "No army. I'll go myself." (p40, clear, but the motive is missing; see Finding 7), "Every time." (p41, a complete answer to a complete question, excellent), "My lord—" and "Stay, Wiglaf." (p44), "He needs us now!" and "Then I'll go by myself." (p47), "Get behind me, boy!" (p48). All pass: complete or deliberate answers, one referent, and forceful rather than compressed.
- **p14.** The cup scene repeats the vow from p13 and has little heat. Wealhtheow's want (her sons) arrives only on p22. Let her eyes go to her small sons as she thanks him, or let her test him ("Men have sworn that in this hall before."), so the page has subtext.
- **p16.** The Chronicle sits on a legal chapter break, but it interrupts the book's sharpest early cliffhanger ("He did not close his eyes"). Its question is earned there, and its last line ("That is why a monster breaking in at night…") turns it into a suspense device, so I accept it. Still, judge on the proof whether it deflates.
- **pp 8 and 43 (and p17).** After a Chronicle page, the first story line opens with a pronoun ("They sailed at once"; "The runaway led them"). Re-anchor with names: "Beowulf and his Geats sailed at once."
- **p9.** The coast-guard confrontation is a challenge and a judgement, which is a person's choice. It could be dramatic rather than prose, which would also help the mode balance.
- **p27.** Unferth's change of heart reads through p21's silence, but one beat of him looking at the black water and not going would let the reader see the shame behind the gift (his secret want in the ledger).
- **pp 32–34.** Hrothgar's reaction to Beowulf coming back from the lake, after the Danes had given him up for dead, is skipped. One panel on p33 would do it.
- **pp 34, 43, 44, 50.** The warning lists four things, and Beowulf says "all three." Then the poison is the fourth, sickness. If that's deliberate, let it land on p50 ("Sickness too, Hrothgar. You left nothing out."). If not, keep the lists consistent.
- **p22.** Beowulf's promise to Wealhtheow's sons ("as long as I live") never returns. That's fine for this volume, but note it.
- **Spectacle text (audit warnings, pp 18, 24, 31, 38, 39, 49, 54).** 31–40 words each. Page 24's 40-word caption is the heaviest. "Beowulf was asleep in a guest house that night" could move to p25 as staging. Judge them as craft signals, not blockers.
- **p7.** Field 2 calls it "the poem" before the reader knows the story is a poem. One clause fixes it ("This story comes from an old poem, made far away in England…").
- **Non-rendering page-turn lines.** p22 ("someone sang the song about Beowulf"), p30 ("the giant's sword has fallen") and p35 ("the warning is in his pocket, unread") are muddled. They don't render, but the page contract will inherit them.
- **p13 staging.** "Hrothgar smiles for the first time in the book" contradicts p10's rendered "anyone the king smiled at." Change the stage note.
- **Chronicle pages generally.** All six open with their question, carry one shown idea, stay within budget, debunk nothing, spoil nothing, and carry no causal step. The story passes the paraphrase test without them. The factual claims I checked (Lejre hall, the Sutton Hoo lyre and helmet, pattern-welding, the Staffordshire Hoard, the 1731 Cotton fire, two scribes, the cup in *The Hobbit*) are accurate. Page 23's kenning "whale-road" pays off beautifully on p56. These pages are an asset.
