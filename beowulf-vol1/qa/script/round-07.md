# Script gate, round 7: Beowulf, Vol I (`books/beowulf-vol1/08-FULL-SCRIPT.md`)

## Cold read

I read pages 1–57 in order before opening any other project document.

**The story as I took it in.** Something in the fens hates the music coming from King Hrothgar's golden hall. It breaks in, takes thirty men, and keeps the hall empty for twelve winters. Across the sea, Beowulf hears the sailors' story. His people laughed at him as a boy, and Hrothgar once paid his father's debt. He sails with fourteen men and boasts that he'll fight bare-handed. Unferth mocks him over a lost swimming race, and Beowulf tops him with the true story: nine sea-monsters. The queen tests him with the cup ("My sons have never slept one night in their father's hall") and he swears it in front of everyone. Grendel takes one Geat before Beowulf can move. Beowulf then tears off the monster's arm, and the claw hangs over the doors. The Danes sleep in Heorot again. Grendel's mother takes the arm back and kills the king's oldest friend while Beowulf sleeps in his guest house. Hrothgar throws the oath back at him. Unferth, shamed, lends Beowulf his sword Hrunting. It fails under the lake, and a giant's sword kills her. Beowulf returns the failed sword with nothing but praise, and Unferth is silent for the second time in his life. Hrothgar warns him that sickness, the sword, fire or old age comes for everyone. Beowulf is nineteen and doesn't hear it.

Fifty winters later a runaway steals one cup and the dragon burns the old king's hall. Beowulf is still too proud to accept help ("they've sung that I never needed anyone"). He lets Wiglaf come only to watch. Ten men run; Wiglaf comes anyway, quoting Beowulf's own fear back at him. Together they kill it. Beowulf, poisoned, finishes Hrothgar's list ("Sickness too, then"), wants the gold for his people, and dies. Wiglaf shames the cowards and refuses gold "paid for with his life". The Geats bury it with their king, and sailors still name the barrow.

I could say all of that after one read, so the paraphrase test passes.

**Where I stopped, reread or guessed:**
- **p44, "It's all three, all at once."** I briefly counted: the fire is the dragon, old age is him, so what is the sword? I settled on "the fight". It was a half-second pause, and p50's "Sickness too, then" turns the list into a payoff, so I'm not raising it.
- **p56, "whale-road."** It resolves at once because of the p23 kenning and the picture of the ship. It is a pleasure, not a stumble.
- **p53.** In earlier rounds this is where the burial of the gold went against Beowulf's dying wish. I read it cold, watching for that failure, and did not stop. "The rest he paid for with his life. I don't think any of us should keep it" gives a reason a child feels straight away: you don't spend what your king died for. p55 then turns it from Wiglaf's private feeling into the Geats' choice ("But Wiglaf told them how their king had died…"). The refusal is now enacted, explained, and owned by the people.

I found no other stops.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book. In several places — p27, p33, p48, p50, p53 — it is better than good.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** The opening is economical and frightening. "Something that hated the sound of music" is a hook a reader can name. p4 has real heat:
- Unferth wants to look strong to the king.
- Wealhtheow's "And if swords can't stop it?" punctures that.
- Hrothgar's "Then I built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into" is a man blaming himself.

p6 gives Beowulf his appetite from the first balloon: "Now it's my turn."

**Movement II (pp 8–14).** The flyting is the most fun in the book. Unferth's voice is distinct: showy and sly ("Luckily, I've written one"; "Seven nights in the water, and you lost"). Beowulf's "Unferth, my friend, you've had a lot of mead" is the funny, cocky, too-loud nineteen-year-old the mandate demands. Then he goes "very still, very plain" for the real blow. The want-from on both sides is sharp: Unferth wants to shrink the stranger, and Beowulf wants the hall. Young Beowulf is never serene or modest.

**Movement III (pp 15–23).** Wealhtheow's cup scene has subtext. She thanks God, then says the hard thing with her eyes on her sons, and the oath answers her exact demand. The decision to let Grendel take a man before Beowulf can move is the best structural choice in the movement:
- It makes p21 grief rather than triumph ("I wanted to bring all my men home").
- It keeps "thirteen" honest for the rest of the book.

Unferth under the claw, "for once, had nothing to say," is a quiet reaction that sets up a later payoff.

**Movement IV (pp 24–32).** Four spectacle pages run underwater. They carry the action without narrating it, and Beowulf's only line ("Then I'll do it with my hands!") is pure character. The heat is on the land:
- p25: Hrothgar's "My sons slept in this hall last night because you swore they could. She came anyway." Beowulf answers with action, not apology, which is exactly his flaw and his virtue.
- p27: Unferth wants forgiveness without asking for it. "Come back, Geat. I want my sword." is the best line of subtext in the book.
- p32: "Did you think I wasn't coming back?" is the grin restored.

**Movement V (pp 33–36).** p33 pays Unferth off with generosity rather than a lecture. p34 is the one sermon in the book, and it is earned. It belongs only in Hrothgar's slow, sad voice, and it is dramatized by the listener not listening ("He nodded, and didn't hear a word"). That is enacted, not narrated.

**Movement VI (pp 37–42).** The jump in time lands. Old Beowulf's appetite has turned into pride ("No army. I killed Grendel with my own two hands"). The forge scene has the two finest small lines in the book: "Were you ever afraid?" / "Every time." Wiglaf wants to be allowed to come, and Beowulf wants to stay the man in the songs. That is the engine, played between two people at a bench.

**Movement VII (pp 43–57).** This is the climax and not an epilogue. The mandate's named failure is avoided. Each beat raises the heat:
- p44: "too old to start now. Stay."
- p47: Wiglaf alone against ten backs.
- p48: "I told you to stay on the hill!" / "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway." The protagonist's own words come back as the rescue.
- p50: "Fifty years I never needed anyone, Wiglaf. Today I did, and you came." It completes the arc in one sentence, in his voice.
- p53: Wiglaf's anger finally gives him a voice of his own ("You'll keep your lives. But you've lost your names.").
- p54–56: the grief is given room — a fire, a mound, a cliff seen from the sea.

The ending works: the name outlives the gold.

**Attribution-strip test.** It passes on every dramatic page I tried:
- Young Beowulf: jokes, then sudden plainness.
- Old Beowulf: dry and short.
- Hrothgar: formal and sorrowing.
- Wealhtheow: every sentence has a purpose.
- Unferth: a well-made sneer.
- Wiglaf: fast and earnest.

There is no logistics dialogue anywhere. The one practical instruction (p40, "Tell the smith to make me a shield all of iron. Wood burns.") is character, and it sets up the burning on p48.

## Checks performed (no blockers)

**Introductions and continuity.**
- Every named character is named in rendered text before the plot depends on them:
  - Hrothgar, Wealhtheow and Unferth on p2, Unferth again on p10.
  - Beowulf on p6, and Wiglaf on p40.
- Unnamed figures are anchored before they are needed: the oldest friend (p4, p22, p24), the sons (p15), the uncle (p27, p37) and the runaway (p38, p40, p43).
- The numbers hold: 14 + 1 men aboard; 13 Geats after p17; Wiglaf plus ten.
- Objects are tracked:
  - Hrunting is thrown on p29, picked up on p31, carried on p32 and returned on p33.
  - The ring passes from p22 to p37, then to Wiglaf on p52, and appears on pp 53–55.
  - The helmet passes on p52 and appears on p55.
  - Naegling breaks on p48 and lies on the gold on p53.
  - The yellow shield burns on p48.
- Time is consistent: twelve winters, seven nights, fifty winters, ten days.
- Every death has an aftermath: the Geat (p21), the friend (p25), the mother (p32), Beowulf (pp 53–56).

**Five-word audit (all pass).**
- p18 "Looking for me?" answers p17's reaching claw, with the grip visible.
- p34 "Yes, my lord." is deliberately hollow, and the caption tells us so.
- p40 "I'll gather the army!" is a complete thought.
- p41 "Every time." answers a complete question in the same panel.
- p47 "He needs us now!" has one possible referent, after "stand by him".
- p47 "Then I'll go by myself." is a complete thought.

**Architecture.**
- Every page has exactly one mode, one turn, and a marked dominant panel.
- No page has more than two locations; the two-location pages (9, 17, 20, 24, 35, 39) are transitions.
- Text sits within budget throughout:
  - The heaviest dramatic pages are p25 (about 100 words) and p2 (about 96), both inside the ~105 ceiling.
  - Prose pages run about 80–105 words.
  - Chronicle pages run about 100–120.

**Chronicle pages (7, 14, 23, 36, 42, 57).**
- Each sits at a scene break and opens its first field with its question in words.
- Each question is raised by the pages before it: "fens of Denmark" and "across the sea"; the sleepers on the benches; the king's poet; "Its name is Hrunting"; the dragon on the gold; the sailors still telling the story.
- Each carries one dominant image and gives context, not verdict. None debunks the story. The only hedge is "may mean 'shaper'", which is plain accuracy.
- None carries a causal step or spoils a later turn.
- With every Chronicle page removed, the story still paraphrases. p47's promise comes from Wiglaf's own mouth, not from p14.
- I found no claim I believe to be false.
- No story character explains history.

**Fidelity anxiety.** There is no functional crowd, and every character earns a place:
- The runaway is the dragon's trigger and the guide to the barrow.
- The oldest friend makes the mother's raid personal.
- The ten men exist so Wiglaf can be the one who stays.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **Mode balance.** The split is 22 dramatic, 12 prose and 17 spectacle, about 43/24/33 against the ~60/20/13 target. The volume mandate expects a monster epic to lean toward spectacle. Every spectacle page I checked is an event (a fight, an arrival, discovery, grief), not a person's choice sitting on the wrong mode. The form is intact.

2. **p25's text inventory is at the top of the budget.** It has about 100 words, with three Hrothgar balloons in one beat. This is not a script problem, but the page prompt should reserve clear balloon lanes for that beat.

3. **p22's promise is never closed.** Beowulf's "I'll be their friend as long as I live, my lady" is not touched again. One clause on p37 would close it, for example "He sent his ships to any friend who asked, and to Hrothgar's sons first of all." Optional.

4. **p44's "It's all three"** gives a brief pause over what "the sword" means. It is acceptable as written, because p50 turns the list into a payoff.

5. **p56's "whale-road"** leans pleasantly on p23 but is readable from the image alone. Keep it.

6. **The ledger needs updating.** Its page references (young/old ranges, "burns on p 46", "From p 50") are stale against the script, as the brief already notes. When it is reindexed:
   - Wiglaf's shield burns on p48.
   - The ring and helmet pass on p52.
