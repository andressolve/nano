# Script Gate — Beowulf: The Hall, the Mere, and the Dragon — Round 6

## Cold read

I read the whole script once, in order, before opening any other document.

**The story as a first-time reader would tell it.** A great hall in Denmark, Heorot, is full of song. Something in the fen hates the music. Grendel bursts in and carries off thirty men, and the hall stands empty every night for twelve winters. Across the sea, a Geat named Beowulf hears the sailors' story. He is nineteen, and his country laughed at him as a boy. King Hrothgar once paid his father's debt, so Beowulf sails with fourteen men to pay it back. At Heorot the king's spokesman, Unferth, mocks him for losing a swimming race. Beowulf tells the true story (five nights, a storm, nine sea-monsters) and wins the hall. The queen offers him the cup and says her sons have never slept a night in their father's hall. He swears they will tomorrow night.

Unarmed, he waits in the dark. Grendel takes one Geat before Beowulf can move, then grabs the wrong sleeper. Beowulf tears off his arm, and the claw is hung over the doors. That night there is a feast, a ring from the queen, and the Danes sleep in Heorot again while Beowulf sleeps in a guest house. Grendel's mother comes for the arm and takes the king's oldest friend. The king says the thing Beowulf feared most: his sons slept there because Beowulf swore they could. Unferth lends Beowulf his named sword. Beowulf dives into the black lake. The sword fails, he kills her with a giant's sword that melts, and he comes up to find only his Geats still waiting. He gives the failed sword back with grace, hears Hrothgar's warning about pride without listening, and goes home a hero.

Fifty years later he is king. A runaway steals a cup from a dragon's hoard, and the dragon burns the coast, the king's hall first. Too proud to take an army, old Beowulf brings Wiglaf and ten men to watch. The ten run. Wiglaf comes anyway. Together they kill the dragon, and Beowulf dies of the bite, giving Wiglaf his ring. Wiglaf shames the deserters. The Geats bury the king with all the gold, and sailors still point to Beowulf's Barrow.

I could tell that story after one read, so the paraphrase test passes. It also passes with the six Chronicle pages removed.

**Where I stopped or reread:**

- **p53, "The rest… I'm not sure any of us should."** Should what, and why? I had to wait two pages for p55 to tell me. This is Finding 1.
- **p24, staging "the hook under the gable empty."** This doesn't match p20, where the claw hangs inside, above the doors. Staging only (see observations).
- **p34, "the flood took every one of them."** I paused briefly on *which* flood (observation).
- **p44, "you'd send an army to help anyone else."** Nothing earlier shows this; I read past it (observation).

Nothing else stopped me.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book, and the dragon movement is the best thing in it. Movement by movement:

**I (pp 1–7).** The opening image does its job. The two coal eyes turned toward the gold hall make the monster's want ("silence; the hall gone") legible without a word. p4 has real heat in Hrothgar's shame: "And I slept through it, in my own house." Unferth's bravado ("I'll fill this hall with every sword in Denmark") pays off one page later in p5's flat "Grendel came anyway." Beowulf's want on p6 is double: the debt he names aloud, and the laughter the caption names for him. That is the right split between what he says and what drives him.

**II (pp 8–14).** The flyting is the most purely enjoyable stretch.
- **Want-from:** Unferth wants to shrink the stranger in front of the hall. Beowulf wants the hall on his side.
- **Subtext:** "Unferth, my friend, you've had a lot of mead" is an insult dressed as courtesy.
- **Voice:** It passes attribution-strip easily. Unferth speaks in well-made, sly sentences; Beowulf grins, then goes plain. "If you were half as fierce as your words…" lands because the grin drops first.

**III (pp 15–23).** p15 is the hinge of the whole first half, and it holds.
- **Wealhtheow:** her eyes go past the hero to her sons, and "My sons have never slept one night in their father's hall" is a test, not a thank-you.
- **The fight:** it is enacted, not narrated. "He was too fast" costs Beowulf a man.
- **The aftermath:** p21's folded cloak on the empty bench earns him the right to grin again. "I wanted him to die here in front of you, not run off into the dark" is exactly the hungry, slightly-too-loud nineteen-year-old the mandate asks for.

**IV (pp 24–32).** p25 is the hottest dramatic page in the first half. Beowulf walks in cheerful ("I hope it was a peaceful night?"), and Hrothgar turns the oath on him: "My sons slept in this hall last night because you swore they could. She came anyway." That one line connects the raid to Beowulf's own promise.

Unferth's lending of Hrunting is all subtext, and it works:
- He stares at the water without going near it.
- He makes the offer formal.
- He hides behind a joke: "Come back, Geat. I want my sword."

The underwater run is clean, enacted spectacle, and p32's "Did you think I wasn't coming back?" restores the grin at the right moment.

**V (pp 33–36).** p33 is a scene about generosity, staged as a mirror: the sword is held out across both palms, the way it was offered. Beowulf covering for Unferth's shame in public is real subtext. p34's "Beowulf was nineteen. He nodded, and didn't hear a word" is the book's best caption. It sets the trap the last movement springs.

**VI–VII (pp 37–56).** This is where the book stops being good and becomes moving. The mandate's warning that the dragon could become an epilogue has been fully answered.
- **Appetite:** old Beowulf's is pride, and it is spoken and dramatized: "No army. I killed Grendel with my own two hands," and "For fifty years they've sung that I never needed anyone… I'm too old to start now."
- **The boast reframed:** the forge scene (p41) turns the young man's boast into fear management: "Everyone heard me. After that, I couldn't run away." That makes p48's "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway" land like a blow.
- **Hrothgar's warning:** it returns exactly when it should. On p44 Beowulf says "all three, all at once," and on p50 "Sickness too, then. Old Hrothgar left nothing out."
- **The confession:** "Today I did, and you came" is the book's emotional peak, and it is earned.
- **Aftermath:** the death has it. Wiglaf's anger at the ten on p53 has heat, and "You'll keep your lives. But you've lost your names" is spoken from desire, not theme.

My one stop in this movement is the moral choice about the gold (Finding 1). It is the volume's final moral fork, and right now it rests on a half-finished sentence.

**Chronicles.** All six open with the question in words. All six give context rather than a verdict: none debunks or argues truth.
- **p42:** pays off the poet's dragon song from p22 (Sigemund).
- **p23:** plants "whale-road," which p56 reuses. That is the best kind of Chronicle-to-story echo.
- **p57's "Sound familiar?":** points back to the runaway's cup on p38, inside this book, so it does not depend on the reader knowing *The Hobbit*.
- **Accuracy:** I found no claim I believe is false. Lejre hall about 48 m; Sutton Hoo 1939; Staffordshire 2009 by metal detector; Ashburnham House 1731; two scribes; pattern welding; the boar-headed eyebrows of the Sutton Hoo helmet — all check.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p53, panel 3 (with p55): Wiglaf's refusal of the dying king's wish is a fragment that never says what it refuses or why.**

**The failure.** On p52, Beowulf's last instruction about the gold is "Good. That's for our people. They'll need it when I'm gone." On p53, the heir answers that wish in a balloon that ends without its verb, its object or its reason:

> "He gave me this one with his own hand. The rest… I'm not sure any of us should."

Read aloud, a listener asks: *should what?* The verb has to be borrowed from "wear a single ring of it," a sentence earlier. Round 6 inserted a new sentence ("He gave me this one…") between them, so the borrowing is now a reach. The reason is missing entirely.

Then p55 hands the decision to a different and vaguer agent: "when the Geats heard how their king had died, they would not keep gold he had paid for with his life." The decision has no named owner: Wiglaf half-says it, and "the Geats" do it.

**What the reader must repair.** On p53 they have to supply the missing "keep it." They also have to hold an unexplained hesitation from a boy who has just been given the king's last instruction, and on p55 they have to stitch his hesitation to the Geats' decision. This is the volume's final moral fork (§9 says it must be preserved). The reasoning shows up only on the page after next, and a different agent carries it out. That is "understandable after looking back," which this standard treats as a revision flag. It also breaks §7.7: a refusal should carry a subject, a meaningful verb and an object.

**Prescription.** Finish Wiglaf's thought on p53, with the object and the reason, in his own quick, earnest voice. For example:

> "He gave me this one with his own hand. The rest he paid for with his life. I don't think any of us should keep it."

Then let p55 show the Geats agreeing with him instead of reaching it alone. For example:

> "Beowulf had wanted the gold for his people. But Wiglaf told them how their king had died, and the Geats would not keep gold he had paid for with his life."

Both pages stay well inside budget. Keep the touch on the ring at his neck as the staging beat for "this one."

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p24, staging:** "the hook under the gable empty." p20 (and the round-5 change) puts the claw inside, high above the doors. Make p24's staging say "the place above the doors where the claw hung is empty," so the builder doesn't draw an outside gable.
2. **p34:** "And the flood took every one of them." The hilt shows the waves, but "the flood" assumes the reader knows which one. "A great flood took every one of them" costs one word.
3. **p44:** "you'd send an army to help anyone else." No earlier page shows Beowulf doing this. One clause in p37's prose would seed it ("he sent his ships to any friend who asked, and no enemy dared attack the Geats").
4. **p41:** "Come, then, and bring ten men. You can watch." That Beowulf wants witnesses for the song is good subtext and works as written. Make sure the page prompt stages the dry grin so "You can watch" reads as pride, not caution.
5. **p40:** How the old king survived his own hall burning is never touched. It's harmless, but p40's caption could be "In the morning, old Beowulf, who had fought the fire all night, stood in the ashes of his hall."
6. **Mode balance:** 22 dramatic, 12 prose, 17 spectacle over 51 story pages, or roughly 43 / 24 / 33. That leans heavily toward spectacle, which the volume mandate anticipates for a monster book. Every spectacle run (pp 17–19, 28–31, 45–49) is a fight that really is enacted, and every dramatic page carries a person's choice, so I don't count it as harm.
7. **Spectacle text (audit warnings, pp 17, 18, 24, 31, 38, 39; 31–38 words each):** Each one carries causal narration that silence couldn't. The page prompts should keep these captions in narrow top or bottom bands.
8. **Dramatic text near the ceiling:** p2 (about 95 words including captions) and p25 (about 98) are near the 105 limit, which applies only with large protected balloon lanes. Reserve the lanes in their prompts.
9. **Five-word audit (all six pass):**
   - p18 "Looking for me?" — the claw on the bench makes the referent unique.
   - p34 "Yes, my lord." — a deliberate empty answer, which the caption cashes in.
   - p40 "I'll gather the army!" — complete.
   - p41 "Every time." — a direct answer to a complete question in the same balloon pair.
   - p47 "He needs us now!" — "he" is unambiguous after the promise line.
   - p47 "Then I'll go by myself." — complete.
10. **p36 Chronicle:** Its question (Hrunting's name) was raised on p27 and p33, not by p35, so it's the least earned of the six. It still sits at the right break: the breath before the fifty-year jump. Acceptable.
11. **p57:** The book ends on a Chronicle page (audit warning). The story's last turn and final image land first on p56, and p57's question is raised directly by p56's "said: there is Beowulf's Barrow." Acceptable.

Findings: 1 (p53/p55, Wiglaf's unfinished refusal at the final moral fork).
