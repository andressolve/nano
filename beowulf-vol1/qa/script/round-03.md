## 1. Cold read

**The story as I took it in.** A monster in the Danish fens hates the music of King Hrothgar's great hall, Heorot. One night it breaks in, and thirty men are gone. Unferth, the king's clever spokesman, fills the hall with swords. It doesn't work, and for twelve winters nobody sleeps in Heorot. Sailors carry the story across the sea to Beowulf, a nineteen-year-old Geat whom everyone laughed at as a boy. Hrothgar once paid his father's debt, so Beowulf sails with fourteen men to pay it back. He boasts that he will fight Grendel bare-handed. Unferth mocks him over a swimming race he lost as a boy, and Beowulf tells the true story (nine sea-monsters) and wins the hall. The queen offers him the cup and tests him, and he swears to win or die.

That night Grendel takes one Geat before Beowulf can move. Then Beowulf grips Grendel's arm and tears it off, and Grendel runs off to die. The claw hangs over the door. Beowulf grieves the man he lost. There is a feast, the queen gives him a gold ring and asks him to be kind to her sons, and he sleeps in a guest house. That night Grendel's mother takes back the arm and the king's oldest friend. Beowulf dives into the black lake with Unferth's sword, Hrunting. The sword fails, and he kills her with a giant's sword that then melts. He comes back up, returns Hrunting to Unferth with no word of blame, hears Hrothgar's warning about pride and does not listen, and sails home famous.

Fifty years later he is king. A runaway steals a cup from a dragon's hoard, and the dragon burns the coast and Beowulf's own hall. Too proud to ask for help, the old king goes out alone. His ten men run. Wiglaf, his cousin's boy, comes anyway, and together they kill the dragon. Beowulf is poisoned. He tells Wiglaf "Today I did, and you came," gives him the ring, and dies. Wiglaf shames the deserters and buries the gold with the king. The barrow still stands over the sea.

**I could paraphrase every movement after one read.** The causal chain holds from page 1 to page 56.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed:**

- **p15.** "My sons sleep here too." I stopped. Page 5 told me nobody has slept in Heorot for twelve winters, and on p16 Hrothgar leads the Danes away before dark. Whose sons sleep where? (Finding 1)
- **p24 → p25.** The caption says "Beowulf, in his guest house, never heard a thing." The next line Beowulf speaks is "My lord? I heard shouting." I reread the caption to check. (Finding 2)
- **p25.** How does Hrothgar know it was Grendel's *mother*, and that she lives in the lake? I knew it from the p24 caption, so I didn't stall, but he never learns it on the page. (Nonblocking)
- **p28 / p32.** He "sank for most of a day," yet surfaces in the "late afternoon" of the day he dived. I noticed but kept going. (Nonblocking)
- **p53.** "He said this gold was for his people. His people ran." I stopped hard. Ten men ran. His people are the villages on a coast that p40 says is half burned, and p52 has just told us "They'll need it when I'm gone." I had to build a justification for the ending myself. (Finding 3)

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes. The script has real heat and distinct voices, and for most of its length I wanted the next page. The three findings below are line-level defects in a living script, not a dead register.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** A strong opening. Hrothgar's "Then I built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into" is the best line in the movement: bitter, in his own voice, and it hands the reader the stakes. Unferth arrives with a personality ("Luckily, I've written one"). Beowulf on p6 has appetite: the laughed-at boy, the debt, "Who's coming with me?"

**Movement II (pp 8–14).** The flyting is the most fun in the book. Unferth wants to shrink the stranger in front of the hall, and Beowulf wants the hall. "Unferth, my friend, you've had a lot of mead" is cocky, funny and unmistakably his. "Nobody at home believed him. All anyone remembered was that Beowulf lost" ties the boast to a wound without a word of explanation.

**Movement III (pp 15–23).** Wealhtheow's test is the right beat, with her eyes on her sons, but its key line is broken by a continuity error (Finding 1). The fight is clean and enacted. "Looking for me?" earns its grin. p21 is the best page in the first half: "I wanted to finish him here, in front of you" (vanity), then "But I wanted to bring all my men home" (real grief). One boy has two wants, and the reader feels the gap between them. Unferth "for once, had nothing to say" pays off nicely.

**Movement IV (pp 24–32).** The mother's raid is chilling because she takes someone we know by face. On p27 Unferth's gift turns a rival into a person: "Come back, Geat. I want my sword" is pure subtext, affection disguised as a sneer. The undersea fight is legible, and the melting blade is a wonder beat. Beowulf's "Did you think I wasn't coming back?" keeps his appetite alive after the victory.

**Movement V (pp 33–36).** p33 is lovely. Unferth wants not to be pitied, Beowulf wants to restore him in public, and "Then everyone here can hear me say it again" is generosity with an edge of showmanship. p34 is the engine of the whole book, and it works because Beowulf *doesn't* hear: "Yes, my lord." / "He nodded, and didn't hear a word." The subtext is right there on the page.

**Movement VI (pp 37–42).** The forge scene is the emotional hinge, and it lands. "Every time." / "After that, I couldn't run away" recasts the boast of the first half as fear, and that is the book's real idea, never stated as a moral. Wiglaf's want ("Let me come tomorrow") and Beowulf's grudging "You can watch" carry real heat.

**Movement VII (pp 43–57).** The climax gets the same care as Grendel did, which the mandate demands.
- "For fifty years they've sung that I never needed anyone, Wiglaf. I'm too old to start now" is pride with a crack in it.
- p48 "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway" is a payoff the book earned forty pages ago.
- p50 "Sickness too, then. Old Hrothgar left nothing out" is the best line in the script: dry, old, funny and devastating, and it completes the list from p34.

Then p53 wobbles. Wiglaf's anger is hot and correct, but the decision it drives contradicts the dying king's last wish on the page before, for a reason that blames people who weren't there (Finding 3). The funeral, the barrow and the sailors close the book with dignity.

**Mode balance.** Story pages split 22 dramatic / 12 prose / 17 spectacle, which is 43 / 24 / 33. That leans well toward spectacle. I checked every spectacle page for a turn that is really a person's choice and found none miscast; the fights are action. Nonblocking.

## 3. Mandatory findings

### 1. p15: Wealhtheow's line contradicts the book's own rule about the hall

- **Failure:** WEALHTHEOW: "...men have sworn great things in this hall before, Beowulf. My sons sleep here too."
- **What the reader has to repair:**
  - p5 establishes that the hall "stood empty every night" for twelve winters and that the Danes "slept in little houses far from it."
  - p16 has Hrothgar lead all the Danes away before dark.
  - p22 says "for the first time in twelve winters, the Danes slept in Heorot."
  - So her sons do *not* sleep here, and they won't tonight. The reader has to decide whether the queen is wrong, whether the boys are in danger tonight, or whether "sleep here" means something looser. This is the queen's only stake in the scene where she tests the hero, and its key line is false inside the story.
- **Revision:** keep the beat and make it true. For example: "My sons have never slept one night in their father's hall." Or: "My sons have never slept a night in this hall. I want them to." Either version turns the line into a want the reader can hold her to on p22, where they finally do.

### 2. p25: Beowulf's first line contradicts the caption that ends p24

- **Failure:** p24 ends with the caption "Beowulf, in his guest house, never heard a thing." The next page opens with BEOWULF: "My lord? I heard shouting. I hope it was a peaceful night?"
- **What the reader has to repair:**
  - The reader has to work out whether he heard or not. The intended answer is that he heard the morning commotion, not the raid, but that is a reconstruction.
  - Within the same balloon, "I heard shouting" followed by "I hope it was a peaceful night?" reads as sarcasm or obtuseness. That muddies the intended beat: a cheerful young man walking innocently into grief.
- **Revision:** drop "I heard shouting" and make the obliviousness clean. For example: "Good morning, my lord! They said you sent for me. I hope it was a peaceful night?" The staging already has him rubbing sleep from his eyes. Hrothgar's "Don't speak to me of peace" then lands on a true innocent.

### 3. p53 (with p52 and p55): Wiglaf's reason for burying the gold blames people who never ran, against the king's dying wish

- **Failure:**
  - On p52 Beowulf says: "Good. That's for our people. They'll need it when I'm gone."
  - On p53 Wiglaf says: "He said this gold was for his people. His people ran. Not one of you has earned a single ring of it. It goes into the ground with him, where nobody who left him can ever touch it."
  - p55: "as Wiglaf said, they laid ... all the dragon's gold" in the mound.
- **What the reader has to repair:**
  - Ten men ran. "His people" are the villagers whose coast p40 says is "half" burned, the ones who will "need it." Those people did nothing wrong.
  - As written, Wiglaf overrides the dead king's last wish one page later and denies a burned country its gold to punish ten deserters. The book never registers this as a cost, and p55 reports it with approval.
  - The reader has to invent a bridge ("the ten stand for everyone"), or decide the hero of the last movement is being unfair while the book seems not to notice. Either way they are solving the adaptation at the moment the ending should land.
  - Staging problem on the same page: panel 3's "the one piece he keeps, because it was given" contradicts p52, where Beowulf gives him the helmet and mail too, and p55, where Wiglaf wears the helmet.
- **Revision:** keep Wiglaf's rebuke of the ten exactly as hot as it is (panels 1–2 are excellent). Change the reason for the burial so it honours the king instead of punishing the innocent. Two workable routes:
  - **(a) The people choose it.** p53 panel 3: Wiglaf strips the deserters of any share ("Not one of you will ever wear a ring of it"). p55's prose then gives the decision to the Geats: "When the Geats heard how their king had died, they would not keep gold he had paid for with his life. They laid it all in the mound beside him, and it lies there still." The whole people gain agency, and Beowulf's "for our people" is honoured: they choose to give it back.
  - **(b) Wiglaf's reason is the price, not the deserters.** p53 panel 3: "He paid for this gold with his life. Nobody alive is worth that much. It goes into the ground with him." The rebuke to the ten stays a separate beat.

  For the staging, make the ring the one thing he *holds* (not the one thing he keeps), or drop that clause.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

- **p10.** Hrothgar greets Beowulf by name, but on p9 the prose gives the guard only "Geats, come to help King Hrothgar." Consider "Beowulf told him his name, and why he had come: ..." on p9.
- **p14 → p15.** The Chronicle sits between the flyting and the cup, in the same hall on the same day. p15's caption opens "Then Queen Wealhtheow...", which makes the Chronicle feel like it interrupts a continuous scene. Re-anchor p15 so it plainly opens a new scene: "Late that afternoon, Queen Wealhtheow carried the great gold cup..." The ring-giver and promises field on p14 sets up the oath on p15 well; it only needs a clean door.
- **p25.** Hrothgar knows it was Grendel's mother and that she lives in the lake, but no one on the page told him. One clause would source it: "Her tracks go down into that same black lake. The old people always said Grendel had a mother."
- **p28 / p32.** "He sank for most of a day" doesn't fit with surfacing in the "late afternoon" after a dawn start. Change it to "He sank for a long, long time" or have the Danes wait "until evening."
- **p30.** The caption explaining the giant's sword sits in panel 3 (the swing). It would read more naturally in panel 2, where he first sees the sword.
- **p49.** This is the wordiest spectacle page (39 words) at the book's climax. "The flames died" repeats what the picture shows. "The old king and the boy had killed the dragon together" states the meaning that p50's "Today I did, and you came" delivers better. Consider trimming to let p50 own the payoff. Keep "Wiglaf struck low, under the fire, into its belly" (it is causal) and "Beowulf drew his knife and finished it."
- **Spectacle text, pp 17, 18, 24, 31, 38, 39** (31–38 words each). Every caption is causal and should stay. If the builder needs space, split before trimming.
- **p53.** Panel 1 has the ring "in his fist" and panel 3 has it "at his neck." That's minor staging and is covered by Finding 3's revision.
- **p57.** The book ends on a Chronicle page. The story's last turn lands on p56, and the closing line ("came to them through fire too") earns the placement. Acceptable.
- **Five-word audit**, all pass:
  - p18 "Looking for me?" is a complete thought; the referent is the visible grip and reach.
  - p34 "Yes, my lord." is an intentional empty answer, and the caption makes the subtext explicit.
  - p40 "I'll gather the army!" is complete.
  - p41 "Every time." directly answers "Were you ever afraid?" in the same panel.
  - p47 "He needs us now!" follows "stand by him if he ever needed us," so there is one referent.
  - p47 "Then I'll go by myself." is complete and forceful.
- **Chronicle pages (7, 14, 23, 36, 42, 57).**
  - Each opens with its question in words, carries one idea on one dominant image, and gives context rather than a verdict. None debunks the story, carries a causal step, or spoils a later turn.
  - The claims I can assess look accurate: Lejre hall size, Sutton Hoo lyre and helmet, pattern-welding, Staffordshire Hoard scale, the 1731 fire, single manuscript, two scribes, the Hobbit cup.
  - With every Chronicle page removed, the story still passes the paraphrase test. p56's "whale-road" reads as plain poetry without p23.
  - No story page has a character explaining history. Wiglaf's "when he gave us these helmets and swords, we promised" is a situational reproach, not a lecture.
- **Locations.** Every page is at two or fewer, and every two-location page's turn is the cut. Every page has one dominant panel.
- **Cast.** Eight named characters, no functional crowd. Every named character changes what Beowulf or Wiglaf wants, knows or chooses.
