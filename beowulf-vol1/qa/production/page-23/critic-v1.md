## Blind read
An illuminated manuscript-style explainer asks who served as the king's poet, identifies him as a scop, and explains that he performed remembered songs with a lyre. The large central Sutton Hoo lyre unmistakably owns the page. The page turns from the poet and his instrument to the survival of a lyre from a king's grave, then to the kennings shown at right and restated at the bottom: whale-road, bone-house, and battle-light.

## Visible text
Illuminated opening prose: Who was the king’s poet? Every great hall had one. He was called a scop, a word that may mean ‘shaper’, and he sang to a small harp called a lyre. Songs like this one were sung aloud from memory, long before anyone wrote them down. In 1939, the pieces of a harp like his were found in a king’s grave at Sutton Hoo, in England.

Caption beneath whale illustration: whale-road: the sea

Caption beneath bone-house illustration: bone-house: the body

Caption beneath sword illustration: battle-light: a sword

Caption beneath central lyre: the Sutton Hoo lyre, about 625

Bottom prose: The singers had riddle-names for things, called kennings.
The sea was the whale-road. The body was the bone-house.
And a sword, flashing in the firelight, was a battle-light.

No speaker or character name is lettered. The place-name Sutton Hoo is lettered within the opening prose and the central-object caption.
