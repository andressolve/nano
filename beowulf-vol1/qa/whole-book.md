## 1. Blind account (all 57 proofs at 600 × 900, read in order, story material closed)

### Retell
Something huge with coal-red eyes crouches in the Danish fens, watching a hall that blazes with light. It hates the music coming from it (p1). King Hrothgar has built Heorot, the greatest hall in the world. Queen Wealhtheow gives him the first cup, and Unferth, the king's clever spokesman, has a speech ready. The poet sings, and outside the dark is listening (p2). That night Grendel bursts through the barred doors (p3). Thirty men are gone by morning. Unferth promises to fill the hall with swords. The queen asks what happens if swords can't stop it (p4). They can't. For twelve winters Heorot stands empty every night, and the old king waits alone for help. Sailors carry the story across the sea (p5).

Among the Geats, the story reaches Beowulf. As a boy he was laughed at as too big, too slow, always asleep by the fire. Hrothgar once paid a debt that saved his father's life. "Now it's my turn." Fourteen men go with him (p6). A chronicle map shows Danes, Geats, Lejre and England (p7). He sails, happier than he has ever been (p8). A coast guard sends him up the road to the gold-roofed hall (p9). Hrothgar welcomes the son of his old friend. Beowulf asks for one night and says he will fight bare-handed, since Grendel carries no sword. Unferth sneers (p10). Unferth taunts him in front of everyone about losing a boyhood swimming race (p11). The true story: seven nights at sea and nine sea-monsters killed (p12). Beowulf turns the hall against Unferth: "Tonight the Danes are going to see what a Geat can do" (p13). A chronicle explains the hall, ring-giving and promises (p14).

Wealhtheow brings Beowulf the cup and tests him. Her sons have never slept a night in their father's hall. He swears to win or die there (p15). The Danes leave. Beowulf lies down unarmed, eyes open (p16). Grendel comes out of the moor. He is too fast, takes the Geat on the nearest bench, and reaches for the next one (p17). It is Beowulf: "Looking for me?" For the first time Grendel is afraid, and swords bounce off his hide (p18). Grendel tears free and runs off to die, leaving his arm in Beowulf's hands (p19). The claw is hung over the doors. The tracks lead to a churning black lake (p20). Hrothgar takes Beowulf as a son. Beowulf grieves for the one man he lost. Unferth has nothing to say (p21). There is a feast with gifts, and the poet sings a song about Beowulf and an old one about a dragon-killer. Wealhtheow gives Beowulf the gold neck-ring and asks him to be kind to her sons. He promises. He sleeps in a guest house (p22). A chronicle covers the scop, the lyre and kennings (p23).

Grendel had a mother. She takes back the arm and Hrothgar's oldest friend, the bald warrior from p4 and p22. Beowulf, in the guest house, hears nothing (p24). Beowulf walks in cheerfully and finds Hrothgar in tears. The king says his sons slept here because Beowulf swore they could. Beowulf answers that it is better to go after her than to cry (p25). They ride to the lake. Even a hunted stag won't jump into it (p26). Unferth lends Beowulf his never-failing sword, Hrunting. Beowulf leaves his own sword to Unferth if he dies. "Come back, Geat. I want my sword" (p27). He dives, sinks, and she is waiting (p28). In a dry hall under the lake, Hrunting fails. "Then I'll do it with my hands!" (p29). Her knife can't get through his mail. He sees a giant's sword on the wall and swings it (p30). She falls, light floods in, the blade melts, and only the gold hilt is left (p31). The Danes have given up and gone home. The thirteen Geats are still waiting when he surfaces (p32). He gives Hrunting back and praises it in front of everyone. Unferth is speechless for the second time (p33). Over the hilt, Hrothgar warns him: sickness, the sword, fire or old age comes for everyone. Beowulf is nineteen and doesn't hear a word (p34). A tearful farewell. He comes home and gives the gold away, and nobody calls him the slow boy again (p35). A chronicle covers named swords and the Sutton Hoo helmet (p36).

Fifty winters later Beowulf is the old king of the Geats and still wears the queen's ring (p37). A runaway steals one cup from a sleeping dragon's hoard (p38). The dragon notices, and that night it burns the coast, starting with the king's own hall (p39). Wiglaf, Beowulf's cousin's boy, brings word. Beowulf orders an iron shield ("Wood burns") and refuses an army (p40). At the forge Wiglaf asks whether he was ever afraid. "Every time." He boasted so that he couldn't run away. Wiglaf invokes his dead father, and Beowulf lets him come with ten men to watch (p41). A chronicle covers barrows, hoards, Sigemund, Sutton Hoo and Staffordshire (p42). On the headland the old king sits and remembers Hrothgar's list (p43). "It's all three, all at once." He refuses help ("I'm too old to start now") and walks down alone (p44). He challenges the dragon, and fire breaks on the iron shield (p45). Naegling bites but not deeply enough, and the fire comes again (p46). The ten men run. Wiglaf shames them and goes alone (p47). His wooden shield burns. Under the iron shield: "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway." Naegling breaks (p48). The dragon bites the king's neck. Wiglaf strikes its belly and Beowulf finishes it with his knife (p49). The bite was poisoned, which adds sickness, so Hrothgar left nothing out. "Fifty years I never needed anyone… Today I did, and you came." He sends Wiglaf in for the treasure (p50). Wiglaf finds a gold banner that glows by its own light (p51). Beowulf gives Wiglaf his ring, helmet and mail and asks for a barrow. He dies with the sea wind in his hair (p52). The cowards come back. Wiglaf takes their names from them and says the gold, bought with the king's life, shouldn't be kept (p53). The funeral fire, and a woman sings "who will keep us safe now?" (p54). The Geats bury the gold with his ashes, and twelve riders sing (p55). Sailors still point and say, "There is Beowulf's Barrow" (p56). The final chronicle covers the one surviving manuscript, the 1731 fire and Tolkien's stolen cup (p57).

### Cast as identified blind
- **Beowulf**: tawny-haired Geat in a blue cloak. Young (pp 6–35) and old (pp 37–52), and I recognised him instantly across the fifty-year gap: same cloak, the queen's gold ring, the boar helmet.
- **Hrothgar**: crowned, very long white beard, crimson robe.
- **Wealhtheow**: auburn braids, white veil, green gown. Her two small sons.
- **Unferth**: dark, pointed beard, black and silver. The rival who becomes the lender.
- **Hrothgar's oldest friend**: bald, grey braided beard (pp 2, 4, 22, 24). Taken by the mother.
- **Grendel**, **Grendel's mother** (weed-hair, wolfish jaw, green eyes) and **the dragon**.
- **Wiglaf**: dark curls, freckles, yellow wooden shield.
- **Unnamed figures**: the runaway, the smith, the poet, the coast guard, the ten men, the singing woman.

Every speaker was attributed correctly on the blind pass. The one moment of hesitation is noted under N1.

### Causal chain
- Grendel hates the hall's joy, so he raids it. Swords fail, so the hall stands empty for twelve years.
- Hrothgar once saved Beowulf's father, so Beowulf comes to repay the debt. He boasts, Unferth mocks him, and he answers with the truth, which publicly commits him.
- He swears an oath to the queen, so he waits unarmed, loses one man, and tears off Grendel's arm.
- Grendel dies, so his mother takes revenge, takes the king's friend and breaks the oath's promise of safety. Beowulf goes into the lake carrying the rival's sword.
- Hrunting fails, so he uses the giant's sword. He is generous in returning Hrunting, which turns the rivalry into friendship.
- Hrothgar warns him about pride and death, and he ignores it.
- Fifty years on, a theft wakes the dragon, which burns his hall. Pride makes him fight alone ("I never needed anyone").
- The ten men run. Wiglaf keeps the promise his father made, so the two of them kill the dragon together. The poison completes Hrothgar's list.
- The king dies having needed someone after all. The gold he died for is buried with him, and his name lasts in the barrow, the sailors and the manuscript.

### The ending: what it cost and why the final image matters
It cost Beowulf his life and the Geats their protector ("who will keep us safe now?"). It also cost him the thing he fought for, because the gold goes back into the ground. What survives is the name: the barrow sailors point to on p56. The p57 chronicle carries that idea out of the story. The poem itself survived only one burned book, so the reader is the latest sailor looking up at the cliff. The last line ("Sound familiar?") hands the dragon's stolen cup to the reader's own bookshelf.

### Reading-pace record
- **Movement I (1–7)** opens cleanly and the threat is clear by p3. P5 compresses twelve years well. The p7 map lands at a natural pause.
- **Movement II (8–14)** is brisk. The flyting (pp 10–13) is the funniest and liveliest stretch, and the p12 flashback reads as Beowulf's answer.
- **Movement III (15–23)** is the strongest run of pace in the book. The p16 vigil slows down properly, then pp 17–19 hit fast. The p21 grief beat adds weight at the right moment.
- **Movement IV (24–32)**: the p24 turn is a real shock, and the p25 tie back to the oath stings. Pp 28–31 move well. The air-filled hall on p29 lacks a caption, but the p31 "hall under the lake" covers it.
- **Movement V (33–36)**: the Unferth payoff and the warning are both earned. The p34 closing caption is the book's hinge.
- **Movement VI (37–42)**: the fifty-year jump reads instantly. The dragon's waking (38–39) is a clean, quick setup. The p41 forge scene is the emotional core of the second half.
- **Movement VII (43–57)**: pp 43–44 recall the warning, and pp 47–50 are the best-built sequence in the book. The death (52) and Wiglaf's judgement (53) are not rushed. Pp 54–56 descend gently, and p57 closes outward.

Nothing stalled, and I never had to turn back to work out what had happened.

## 3. Acceptance questions by movement

**I. The Hall That Sang (1–7).** Grendel acts out of hatred for the hall's music. Hrothgar wants his hall and men safe, and chooses Unferth's swords. That fails because swords can't stop it. The Danes are affected: thirty men and then twelve years of empty nights. Hrothgar now believes he built the hall for the monster to walk into. We go across the sea because the sailors carry the story (p5 says so). This passes on the pages alone.

**II. The Stranger Who Boasted (8–14).** Beowulf wants to repay Hrothgar's debt and to be taken seriously. He chooses to fight bare-handed and to answer Unferth with the truth. His reason is spoken: the debt on p6 and the boy everyone laughed at. Unferth is shamed and the hall is won over. Beowulf now believes he has to keep his word ("now he has to keep his word" is the turn). The next scene follows because night is coming. This passes.

**III. Grendel (15–23).** Wealhtheow wants safety for her sons. Beowulf swears, waits unarmed, loses a man, and wins. He fights unarmed because Grendel carries no sword (p10). The Danes, the sons, the lost Geat and Unferth are all affected. Beowulf now believes his victory had a cost (p21). We move on because the hall is singing and asleep while the lake still churns (p20, p24). This passes.

**IV. The Mother in the Mere (24–32).** The mother acts out of revenge for her son, stated on p24. Beowulf goes after her with the rival's sword, because the oath was broken and the king's friend is dead (p25). When the sword fails he uses his hands and then the giant's blade. Hrothgar, Unferth and the waiting Geats are affected. We move on because he has a sword to give back. This passes.

**V. The Old King's Warning (33–36).** Beowulf chooses to honour Unferth's failed sword, and the reason is on the page ("you're a generous man to lend it"). Hrothgar warns him against pride. Beowulf believes he is young and doesn't hear it, which the p34 caption states outright. He goes home and gives the gold away. This passes.

**VI. Fifty Winters (37–42).** The runaway steals, the dragon burns, and Beowulf chooses to fight alone. His reasons are spoken: "I killed Grendel with my own two hands" (p40), and the boast that leaves him unable to run (p41). Wiglaf chooses to come because of his father's word. The coast and the king's hall are affected. This passes.

**VII. The Dragon (43–57).** Beowulf remembers the warning and still refuses help, out of pride ("I'm too old to start now"). The ten men run and Wiglaf keeps the promise. The king realises he needed someone (p50) and accepts sickness as the fourth item on Hrothgar's list. Wiglaf decides the gold must not be kept, and why is spoken on p53 and agreed on p55. The Geats lose their king. The ending moves from the barrow to the sailors to the manuscript. This passes.

No movement depends on information outside the pages. "My uncle the king" (p27, p37) is never named, but the pages never need the name.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

- **N1 (p2, panel 1):** Wealhtheow's balloon ("Then I give it to the king who built this hall.") sits beside Unferth's head, with a short tail pointing back toward the queen's raised cup. On the blind pass I attributed it correctly, after a moment's hesitation. The words fit her (she is holding the cup Hrothgar just asked her to give), and Unferth gets his own captioned introduction in panel 2. Low harm. Fix with an `--edit` only if it is cheap.
- **N2 (p26 → p27):** The ride to the lake reads as daytime, but p27 shows a full moon over the water. Pp 28 and 32 go back to daylight ("until the afternoon"). Lighting drift only; it doesn't change what happens.
- **N3 (p43 → p44):** Old Beowulf sits on the headland in grey daylight, and the next page has a moon over the same scene. Pp 45–48 are dusky or overcast. Atmosphere drift, not story.
- **N4 (p29, panel 1):** The jump from underwater (p28) to a dry, torch-lit hall has no caption. The script has none there either. The p31 caption ("the hall under the lake") resolves it two pages later, and the fight reads clearly in between.
- **N5 (p32, panel 3):** "Take me to Hrothgar. I've got a sword to give back." The sword goes to Unferth, not Hrothgar. This is script text, and it reads naturally as "take me back to the hall."
- **N6 (p53, panel 3):** The broken Naegling that Wiglaf lays on the gold renders short enough to read as a dagger. It's a small prop and the line carries the meaning.
- **N7 (p34):** Young Beowulf has a forehead scar that first appears here. Harmless continuity detail, possibly read as a mark from the mere fight.
- **Typos and wrong letters:** none found on any page.
