## 1. Blind account (written before any story material was opened)

### Retell
In the fens of Denmark, something that hates music crouches in the reeds and watches a golden hall on a hill (p1). King Hrothgar has built Heorot, the greatest hall in the world. His queen, Wealhtheow, offers him the first cup. His clever spokesman, Unferth, has a speech ready. The poet sings of the making of the world, and red eyes in the dark hear every note (p2). That night Grendel bursts the iron-barred doors (p3). By morning thirty men are gone. Unferth swears to fill the hall with swords, and the queen asks what happens if swords can't stop it (p4). Swords don't stop it. For twelve winters the hall stands empty at night while the old king waits for help, and sailors carry the story across the sea (p5).

Among the Geats, Beowulf hears it. He was once the slow boy everyone laughed at. Hrothgar once saved his father, so Beowulf calls for volunteers, and fourteen say yes (p6). A map page shows Danes, Geats and Lejre (p7). They sail, and Beowulf is happy at the prow (p8). A coast guard challenges them, then points them to the gold-roofed hall (p9). Hrothgar welcomes the son of an old friend. Beowulf promises to fight Grendel bare-handed, and Unferth sneers (p10). Unferth mocks him for losing a seven-night swimming race (p11). Beowulf tells the truth: he lost because he was killing nine sea-monsters (p12). He turns the jibe back on Unferth and wins the hall (p13). A chronicle page explains what a hall was, and what ring-giving and promises meant (p14).

The queen carries the cup to him. Her sons have never slept in their father's hall. Beowulf swears to win tonight or die in this hall (p15). The Danes leave. Beowulf lies down unarmed and doesn't close his eyes (p16). Grendel comes and is too fast: he takes the Geat on the nearest bench (p17). Beowulf grabs his arm ("Looking for me?"), Grendel is afraid for the first time, and the Geats' swords bounce off him (p18). Grendel tears himself free and runs off to die, leaving his arm in Beowulf's hands (p19). The claw is hung over the doors, and the tracks lead to a black, churning lake (p20). Hrothgar will love Beowulf like a son. Beowulf grieves the man he couldn't save. Unferth has nothing to say (p21). At the feast there are gifts, and the queen gives Beowulf a gold neck-ring and asks him to be kind to her small sons. He promises, and he sleeps in a guest house (p22). A chronicle page covers the scop, the lyre and kennings (p23).

Grendel's mother rises from the lake. She takes back the arm and the king's oldest friend, the bald warrior. Beowulf, in his guest house, hears nothing (p24). Beowulf walks in cheerful and finds the king weeping: there were two of them, and the boys slept here because of his oath. Beowulf says to go after her: "Show me the lake" (p25). They ride to the dreadful lake (p26). Unferth lends him the sword Hrunting. Beowulf asks that his gold go to his uncle the king and his own sword to Unferth. "Come back, Geat. I want my sword" (p27). He dives, sinks past the beasts, and she seizes him (p28). In her hall Hrunting won't bite, so he tries his hands (p29). Her knife can't pierce his mail. He sees a giant's sword on the wall and swings it (p30). She falls, the blade melts, and only the golden hilt is left (p31). The Danes give up and go home, the Geats wait, and Beowulf surfaces laughing (p32). He returns Hrunting publicly and praises it. Unferth is wordless a second time (p33). By the hearth Hrothgar warns him that sickness, or the sword, or fire, or old age comes for everyone. Beowulf is nineteen and doesn't hear a word (p34). A tearful farewell follows. At home he gives the gold away, and nobody calls him the slow boy again (p35). A chronicle page covers named swords and the Sutton Hoo helmet (p36).

Fifty winters later Beowulf is king of the Geats, old, and still wearing Wealhtheow's ring (p37). A runaway steals a cup from a sleeping dragon's hoard (p38). The dragon notices and burns the coast, starting with the king's own hall (p39). In the ashes, young Wiglaf, his cousin's boy, brings word of the runaway. Beowulf orders an iron shield and refuses an army (p40). At the forge Wiglaf asks whether Beowulf was ever afraid. "Every time." Boasting in Hrothgar's hall meant he couldn't run. Wiglaf asks to come, and Beowulf says bring ten men and watch (p41). A chronicle page explains dragons, barrows, Sutton Hoo and the Staffordshire Hoard (p42). On the headland Beowulf sits and remembers Hrothgar's warning (p43). He says it aloud: "It's all three, all at once." He sends Wiglaf back and walks down alone (p44). He challenges the dragon and shelters behind the shield as the fire comes (p45). Naegling bites but not deep enough, and the fire comes again (p46). The ten men run. Wiglaf shames them and then goes alone (p47). His shield burns, and Beowulf shelters him: "I told you to stay." "You told me you were afraid every time. So am I. I came anyway." Naegling shatters (p48). The dragon bites the king's neck, Wiglaf strikes its belly, and Beowulf finishes it with his knife (p49). The bite was poisoned, so sickness completes Hrothgar's list. "Fifty years I never needed anyone. Today I did, and you came." Beowulf tells Wiglaf to bring the treasure (p50). Wiglaf finds the hoard and the glowing gold banner (p51). Beowulf gives Wiglaf his ring, helmet and mail, asks for a barrow on the headland, and dies with the sea wind in his hair (p52). The ten come back. Wiglaf strips them of their names and says no one should keep gold paid for with the king's life (p53). The funeral fire burns while a woman sings "who will keep us safe now?" (p54). The gold is buried in the barrow, and riders circle it singing (p55). Sailors still point at Beowulf's Barrow (p56). A chronicle page covers the single scorched manuscript and Tolkien's stolen cup (p57).

### Cast as I identified them cold
- **Beowulf.** Blond, with a blue cloak and mail. Young on pp 6–36, old and grey on pp 37–52. He was always identifiable.
- **Hrothgar.** White beard to the waist, crimson robe, circlet.
- **Wealhtheow.** Veiled, auburn braids, green gown.
- **Unferth.** Dark, with a pointed beard, black and silver.
- **The king's oldest friend.** Bald, with a grey braided beard. Introduced p4, toasting p22, taken p24.
- **The poet / scop.**
- **Hrothgar's two small sons.**
- **The coast guard.**
- **Grendel.**
- **Grendel's mother.** Wolfish face, weed-hair.
- **The runaway.**
- **The dragon.**
- **Wiglaf.** Dark curls, freckles, yellow shield.
- **The ten men.**
- **The singing woman.**

I made no misidentifications and assigned every line to its speaker. On p4 the "Thirty of my men" balloon sits between Hrothgar and Wealhtheow, but "my men… my own house" pins it to the king.

### Causal chain
- Music in Heorot draws Grendel. Twelve years of killing follow, and sailors spread the story.
- Beowulf owes Hrothgar a debt for his father, so he comes.
- Unferth's mockery forces a public boast, and the boast becomes an oath to the queen.
- The unarmed fight tears off Grendel's arm. Hanging the trophy draws the mother, who takes the arm and the oldest friend. The oath is broken.
- Beowulf goes into the lake with Unferth's sword. The sword fails, the giant's sword wins, and returning Hrunting with grace reconciles the rivals.
- Hrothgar warns him against pride. He ignores it.
- Fifty years later the stolen cup wakes the dragon, and the king's hall burns.
- The pride he was warned about ("I never needed anyone") sends him in alone. The ten flee and Wiglaf comes, which echoes "afraid every time."
- Together they kill the dragon. The poison completes Hrothgar's list.
- The ring passes to Wiglaf. The gold is buried rather than kept, and the name endures.

### The ending: what it cost and why the final image matters
It cost Beowulf his life, and the Geats their protector ("who will keep us safe now?"). It cost the ten men their names. It cost the gold Beowulf wanted for his people, which they refuse to keep because his life paid for it. What survives is the name, which the old king asked for and Wiglaf guarantees. The last story image (p56) is that name still being spoken by sailors on the whale-road, years later. The kenning planted on p23 pays off there.

The final chronicle (p57) then hands that same "still being told" to the reader: one scorched book survived a fire, and Tolkien loved it. Fire ties the dragon to the manuscript, and the closing image works.

### Reading-pace record
- **pp 1–7 (opening):** brisk and frightening. The two-beat strip on p2 (the poet and the eyes in the dark) is the hook.
- **pp 10–13 (flyting):** the best dialogue run in the first half. The swim on p12 lands as a reveal, not a detour.
- **pp 16–19 (Grendel):** at speed. The "Looking for me?" turn is a real laugh-and-gasp beat.
- **p24 into p25:** cuts cleanly from horror to Beowulf's cheerful innocence. Strong.
- **pp 28–32 (under the lake):** fast.
- **p33 and p34:** slow down deliberately and earn it. "He nodded, and didn't hear a word" registers as a planted fuse.
- **pp 37–39:** the fifty-year jump reads cleanly.
- **pp 40–53:** the emotional core and the strongest stretch. The p41 "Every time" pays off on p48, and "Today I did, and you came" lands.
- **Chronicle pages:** they sit at scene breaks and never interrupt a fight.
- I never stalled or had to turn back to work something out.

## 3. Acceptance questions by movement

**I. The Hall That Sang (pp 1–7)**
- Who acts and what they want: Hrothgar wants a joyful hall, Grendel wants the music silenced, and Unferth wants to fix it with swords.
- Choice: swords and a hall full of men. It fails, and the hall stands empty.
- Who is affected: the Danes and the king, who grows old waiting.
- Why we move on: sailors carry the story across the sea.
- Everything needed is on the page. **Pass.**

**II. The Stranger Who Boasted (pp 8–14)**
- Who acts and what they want: Beowulf wants to repay his father's debt and prove himself; Unferth wants to shrink him.
- Choice: Beowulf boasts publicly and corrects the record about the swimming race.
- Why: he was laughed at as a boy.
- Who is affected: Unferth is humbled, and the hall is won.
- What Beowulf believes: his strength is enough.
- Why we move on: he now has to keep his word.
- **Pass.**

**III. Grendel (pp 15–23)**
- Who acts and what they want: the queen wants safety for her sons; Beowulf wants to win.
- Choice: he swears the oath and fights unarmed.
- Cost: one Geat.
- Who is affected: the Danes get their hall back, and the sons are promised.
- What Beowulf believes: he can protect them.
- Why we move on: the lake is still churning (planted on p20).
- **Pass.**

**IV. The Mother in the Mere (pp 24–32)**
- Who acts and what they want: the mother wants her son's arm back, and takes revenge.
- Who is affected: the oldest friend is killed, and the oath is broken (stated outright on p25).
- Beowulf's choice: go after her. Unferth's choice: lend Hrunting.
- Outcome: the sword fails and the giant's blade wins.
- Why we move on: he has a sword to give back.
- **Pass.**

**V. The Old King's Warning (pp 33–36)**
- Beowulf chooses grace toward Unferth.
- Hrothgar wants to warn him against pride.
- Beowulf, at nineteen, doesn't hear the warning. The page states this, so the reader holds it.
- Why we move on: he goes home famous and generous.
- **Pass.**

**VI. Fifty Winters (pp 37–42)**
- The runaway's theft wakes the dragon.
- Beowulf chooses to fight alone because "I killed Grendel with my own two hands."
- Wiglaf wants to stand by him because of what his father said.
- What Beowulf believes: he is still the man he was, with fear admitted.
- Who is affected: the burned coast.
- **Pass.**

**VII. The Dragon (pp 43–57)**
- Beowulf now remembers the warning but chooses pride anyway ("too old to start now").
- The ten flee. Wiglaf chooses courage despite fear.
- Together they kill the dragon. Beowulf learns, and says, that he needed someone.
- Who is affected: Wiglaf inherits, the ten lose their names, and the Geats lose their king and refuse the gold.
- Why we move on through the coda: the name endures.
- The story itself passes; nothing depends on outside knowledge. The one problem in the book is the factual error on the p7 map (Finding 1).
- **Pass for story.**

## 4. Mandatory findings (blocking)

**1. Page 7: the map labels the European mainland as "England," which contradicts the page's own text.**
- **What is on the page:** the chronicle tells the reader directly to "Look at the map." On the map, the label "England" sits on land at the lower-left edge, just south-west of the Danish peninsula. It is joined to the continent and separated from Denmark only by a narrow inlet. No North Sea is shown and England does not appear anywhere. The script asks for "the eastern coast of England at the left edge" across the sea.
- **Reader harm:** this is the book's orientation page and the reader's first chronicle. A 10-year-old who does what the page says learns something false: that England is a strip of land next door to Denmark. The same page's second field then says the poem "was made far away, in England, by people whose ancestors had sailed there from these very coasts." Picture and text contradict each other, and the picture is wrong. Chronicle pages carry the book's promise that what they show is true; a wrong label on the one map the reader is told to study breaks that promise. Every later Sutton Hoo, Staffordshire and British Library reference (pp 23, 36, 42, 57) points back to this mistaken idea of where England is.
- **Why replace the promoted page:** it is a factual error in the page's main teaching image and a picture/prose contradiction, the kind of note the script history says to take even after approval. The fix is small and needs no full regeneration. An edit that removes the "England" label from the mainland, or redraws the left edge as open North Sea with an English coast across it, replaces the page without touching the text fields, the map's Danes/Geats/Lejre content, or anything else in the book. Then re-gate p7.

No other blocking findings.

## 5. Nonblocking observations (for edit or record; none change reading)

- **p19:** the sound effect "AAAAAOOOOOOH!" rendered with one fewer O than scripted.
- **p55:** the script says twelve riders circle the mound. More riders are drawn, but the prose carries the number.
- **p43 to p44, and within p45:** p43 is grey daylight; p44 panel 3 and p45 panel 1 show a moon; p46–48 are daylight again. Time of day drifts across one continuous scene.
- **p2 vs pp 4, 15, 22:** Wealhtheow's hair reads brown under the veil on p2 and auburn elsewhere.
- **p18 vs pp 17 and 19:** Grendel's face shifts from reptilian to skull-like for one panel.
- **p21 panel 2:** the folded cloak on the empty bench is blue, Beowulf's colour, rather than the lost Geat's own. The dialogue carries the meaning.
- **p13 panel 3:** Hrothgar is too small and distant to read as "laughing out loud." The hall's roar carries the beat.
- **p53 panel 3:** the object laid on the gold reads as a knife or hilt rather than clearly "the old king's broken sword."
- **p27:** "my uncle the king" is the first mention of Hygelac's role. It is clear enough from context, and p37 resolves it.
