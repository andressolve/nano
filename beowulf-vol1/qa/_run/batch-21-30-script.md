## PAGE 21 · *dramatic*

**Location:** Heorot, morning.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** Under the hanging claw, HROTHGAR, on the left,
holds BEOWULF by both shoulders, tears in his eyes; Beowulf, on the right,
is a head taller and grinning.

> **HROTHGAR:** "Twelve winters I prayed for this, and God sent me a boy from across the sea."
>
> **HROTHGAR:** "Beowulf, from this day I will love you like a son."

**Panel 2 — 25%.** Beowulf, on the right, not grinning now. Behind him the
thirteen Geats stand round one empty bench with a folded cloak laid on it.

> **BEOWULF:** "Your hall is yours again, my lord. I wanted him to die here in front of you, not run off into the dark."
>
> **BEOWULF:** "But I wanted to bring all my men home. He took one of them before I could move."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Unferth stands alone under the claw, looking up at it,
his cup hanging forgotten in his hand.

> **CAPTION** *(bottom of the panel)*
> "And Unferth, for once, had nothing to say."

**Why you turn the page:** tonight there will be a feast in Heorot for the first time in twelve winters.

## PAGE 22 · *dramatic*

**Location:** Heorot, night, the victory feast.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** Heorot full of light and people again, every
bench crowded. Before the high seat, gifts laid out for BEOWULF: a golden
banner, a boar-crested helmet, a mail shirt, a sword, and through the open
doors, horses. The old poet sings with his harp. Two small boys (Hrothgar's
sons, unnamed) sit on the bench beside Beowulf, staring up at him. The king's
oldest friend, the old bald warrior with the grey braid, raises his cup to
Beowulf.

> **CAPTION** *(top left, over the rafters)*
> "That night Hrothgar loaded Beowulf with gifts, and the king's poet sang a new song about Beowulf — and an old one, about a hero who killed a dragon."

**Panel 2 — 25%.** WEALHTHEOW, on the left, holds out a heavy twisted gold
neck-ring to Beowulf, on the right. Behind her, her two small sons.

> **WEALHTHEOW:** "Wear this ring, Beowulf, and be happy. And be kind to my sons. They are small, and their father is old."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Beowulf, on the right, puts on the ring; then lays one
huge hand gently on the smaller boy's head.

> **BEOWULF:** "I'll be their friend as long as I live, my lady."
>
> **CAPTION** *(bottom of the panel)*
> "That night, for the first time in twelve winters, the Danes slept in Heorot, and Beowulf was given a guest house of his own."

**Why you turn the page:** the hall is singing again, and the king's poet has made a song about Beowulf.

## PAGE 23 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Who was the king's poet, and how did people sing songs like the one about Beowulf?

**Sources:** *Beowulf* 89–98, 867–874 (the scop's songs); Old English *scop*, possibly related to *scieppan*, "to shape" (Bosworth-Toller); the Sutton Hoo lyre (British Museum 1939,1010.205; reconstructed 1970s); Old English alliterative verse; kennings *hronrād* "whale-road" (*Beowulf* 10), *bānhūs* "bone-house" (2508), *beadolēoma* "battle-light" (1523).

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. The dominant image is a museum study in ink and watercolour
of the Sutton Hoo lyre as reconstructed: a small six-stringed wooden lyre,
round-shouldered, with little gilt fittings. Beside it, three small ink
vignettes in a column: a whale breaching on a wavy line, a tiny house built
of bones, a sword with rays of light around it. A red-and-lapis interlace
initial with a touch of gold begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(left of the lyre, on plain vellum)*
> "Who was the king's poet? Every great hall had one. He was called a scop, a word that may mean 'shaper', and he sang to a small harp called a lyre. Songs like this one were sung aloud from memory, long before anyone wrote them down. In 1939, the pieces of a harp like his were found in a king's grave at Sutton Hoo, in England."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below, on plain vellum)*
> "The singers had riddle-names for things, called kennings. The sea was the whale-road. The body was the bone-house. And a sword, flashing in the firelight, was a battle-light."

> **LABEL** *(beside the lyre)*
> "the Sutton Hoo lyre, about 625"

> **LABEL** *(beside the whale)*
> "whale-road: the sea"

> **LABEL** *(beside the house of bones)*
> "bone-house: the body"

> **LABEL** *(beside the shining sword)*
> "battle-light: a sword"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows the song of Beowulf is being sung — and the hall is asleep, and the lake is still churning.

## PAGE 24 · *spectacle*

**Location:** two — the black lake, and Heorot, night.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 45%.** The black lake at night. The surface parts,
and out of it rises a head: a long wolfish jaw full of teeth, eyes shining
pale green, a mass of black-green hair like river-weed streaming water.
GRENDEL'S MOTHER, larger than her son, rising.

> **CAPTION** *(top left)*
> "But Grendel had a mother. And she had lost her son."

**Panel 2 — 30%.** Inside Heorot: the Danes asleep on the benches under the
hanging claw. In the doorway, a vast dark shape reaching up to the claw.
The old bald warrior with the grey braid sits up on his bench, half-drawing
his sword.

**Panel 3 — 25%.** A moment later: an empty bench, a sword lying on the
floor, the place high above the doors where the claw hung empty, the doorway black. Danes leaping up,
shouting, torches; Wealhtheow clutching her two small sons, safe.

> **CAPTION** *(bottom of the panel)*
> "She took back her son's arm, and Hrothgar's oldest friend, and was gone before anyone could stop her. Beowulf, in his guest house, never heard a thing."

**Why you turn the page:** there is a second monster, and it has taken the king's friend.

## PAGE 25 · *dramatic*

**Location:** Heorot, dawn.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 45%.** Dawn in the wrecked hall. HROTHGAR sits on the
edge of the empty bench, holding the dropped sword across his knees. BEOWULF
comes in through the doors, on the left, cheerful, rubbing sleep from his
eyes, not yet understanding.

> **BEOWULF:** "Good morning, my lord! They said you sent for me. I hope it was a peaceful night?"

**Panel 2 — 30%.** Hrothgar, on the right, looks up. His face is wrecked, tears in his beard.

> **HROTHGAR:** "Don't speak to me of peace. My oldest friend is dead. He stood beside me in every battle of my life."
>
> **HROTHGAR:** "There were two of them, Beowulf. The old people always said Grendel had a mother. Her tracks go down into that black lake."
>
> **HROTHGAR:** "My sons slept in this hall last night because you swore they could. She came anyway."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Beowulf kneels in front of the old king, on the left, and
takes his hands. His grin is gone.

> **BEOWULF:** "Don't cry for him, my lord. It's better to go after the one who killed him than to cry. Show me the lake."

**Why you turn the page:** Beowulf is going to the lake.

## PAGE 26 · *illustrated prose*

**Location:** the cliffs above the mere.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A narrow goat-path along grey crags, a line of
riders on it: HROTHGAR, BEOWULF in helmet and mail, the thirteen Geats, the
Danes. Below them, the lake: black, steaming, in a cleft of rock under
dripping trees, a waterfall pouring into it, water-snakes coiled on the
rocks. At the water's edge a hunted stag stands with hounds behind it,
refusing to jump in.

> **PROSE FIELD 1** *(top of the page, over grey sky)*
> "Hrothgar told him about that lake. Nobody knew how deep it was. At night, people said, fire burned on the water. A stag chased by hounds would rather stand and die on the bank than jump into that lake.
>
> They rode there over the cliffs on goat-paths: the king, the Danes, and Beowulf with his thirteen Geats. They found the lake black and steaming under the dripping trees, with water-snakes coiled on the rocks."

**Why you turn the page:** Beowulf has to go into that water.

## PAGE 27 · *dramatic*

**Location:** the edge of the mere.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 45%.** At the black water's edge, BEOWULF, on the
left, in mail and boar-crested helmet. UNFERTH, who has stood a long moment staring at the black water
without going near it, steps forward from the Danes, on the right, holding a long sword across both palms: its blade marked with
wavy grey patterns like flowing water, its hilt wrapped in silver wire.

> **UNFERTH:** "Beowulf. Take my sword. Its name is Hrunting. In every battle it has ever seen, it has never failed the man who held it."

**Panel 2 — 30%.** Beowulf takes Hrunting and looks at Unferth for a long
moment. Then he turns to HROTHGAR, on the right.

> **BEOWULF:** "Thank you, Unferth. I won't forget it."
>
> **BEOWULF:** "My lord, if I don't come back, send my gold home to my uncle the king. And give my own sword to Unferth."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Unferth, on the right, with the ghost of his old sneer,
almost a smile.

> **UNFERTH:** "Come back, Geat. I want my sword."

**Why you turn the page:** Beowulf is going into the water with the sword of the man who mocked him.

## PAGE 28 · *spectacle*

**Location:** the depths of the mere.

**Panel 1 — 25%.** BEOWULF leaping from the rocks into the black water,
Hrunting in his fist, the Geats on the bank behind.

**Panel 2 — dominant, 50%.** Deep green-black water. Beowulf sinking, his
mail glinting, strange tusked water-beasts butting and snapping at his armour
from all sides, his eyes fixed downward.

> **CAPTION** *(top of the panel)*
> "He sank for a long, long time before he touched the bottom."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** From below, out of the dark: two huge webbed and clawed
hands seizing him, and above them pale green eyes and a cloud of weed-hair.

> **CAPTION** *(bottom of the panel)*
> "She was waiting for him."

**Why you turn the page:** she has him, at the bottom of the lake.

## PAGE 29 · *spectacle*

**Location:** the hall beneath the mere.

**Panel 1 — 30%.** A cave-hall under the water, full of air, lit by a
strange pale fire; old carved arches; weapons hung on the walls. GRENDEL'S
MOTHER flings BEOWULF onto the stone floor.

**Panel 2 — dominant, 45%.** Beowulf, back on his feet, swings Hrunting with
all his strength at her head. The patterned blade rings off her in a shower
of sparks and does not bite.

> **CAPTION** *(top of the panel)*
> "Beowulf struck her with Hrunting with all his strength. For the first time in all its battles, the sword would not bite."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Beowulf hurls Hrunting away across the floor and squares
up to her with his bare hands.

> **BEOWULF:** "Then I'll do it with my hands!"

**Why you turn the page:** the sword has failed, and she is stronger than her son.

## PAGE 30 · *spectacle*

**Location:** the hall beneath the mere.

**Panel 1 — 30%.** GRENDEL'S MOTHER on top of BEOWULF on the stone floor,
driving a long bone-handled knife at his chest; the point skids on his mail
shirt.

> **CAPTION** *(top of the panel)*
> "Her knife couldn't get through his mail."

**Panel 2 — 20%.** Over her shoulder, Beowulf sees it: on the wall, a huge
ancient sword, longer than a man, glowing in the pale fire.

> **CAPTION** *(bottom of the panel)*
> "On the wall hung a sword made by giants, so heavy that no other man alive could have lifted it."

**Panel 3 — dominant, 50%.** Beowulf on his feet, swinging the enormous
sword two-handed in a great arc; she rears up to meet it. The blow lands
beyond the panel's edge.

**Why you turn the page:** the blow has landed, and we don't yet know what it did.
