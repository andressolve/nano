## PAGE 23 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Who was the king's poet, and how did people sing songs like the one about Beowulf?

**Sources:** *Beowulf* 89–98, 867–874 (the scop's songs); Old English *scop*, possibly related to *scieppan*, "to shape" (Bosworth-Toller); the Sutton Hoo lyre (British Museum 1939,1010.205; reconstructed 1970s); Old English alliterative verse; kennings *hronrād* "whale-road" (*Beowulf* 10), *bānhūs* "bone-house" (2508), *beadolēoma* "battle-light" (1523).

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. The dominant image is a museum study in ink and watercolour
of the Sutton Hoo lyre as reconstructed: a small six-stringed wooden lyre,
round-shouldered, with little gilt fittings. Beside it, three small ink
vignettes in a column: a whale breaching on a wavy line, a tiny house built
of bones, a sword with rays of light around it. A red-and-lapis interlace
initial with a touch of gold begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(left of the lyre, on plain vellum)*
> "Who was the king's poet? Every great hall had one. He was called a scop, a word that may mean 'shaper', and he sang to a small harp called a lyre. Songs like this one were sung aloud from memory, long before anyone wrote them down. In 1939, the pieces of a harp like his were found in a king's grave at Sutton Hoo, in England."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below, on plain vellum)*
> "The singers had riddle-names for things, called kennings. The sea was the whale-road. The body was the bone-house. And a sword, flashing in the firelight, was a battle-light."
