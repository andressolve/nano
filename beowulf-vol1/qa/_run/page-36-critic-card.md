## Page intent

## PAGE 36

Chronicle. The book steps out of the story onto a page of an old chronicle to answer the question the last pages raised: why did Unferth's sword have a name, as if it were a person? Two museum studies in ink and wash sit side by side as one image: a long pattern-welded blade standing upright, its surface rippling like flowing water, and the Sutton Hoo helmet with its winged creature across the face and little boars' heads at the ends of its eyebrows. The fields explain how a smith twisted and hammered iron into such a blade, why it was worth a fortune and passed from father to son, and that helmets were treasures too. The tone is a museum guide's, curious and specific, never a verdict on the story. The dominant turn: the reader knows what a named sword meant, and the story is about to jump fifty years.

## Exact owner-controlled script

## PAGE 36 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Why did Unferth's sword have a name, as if it were a person?

**Sources:** *Beowulf* 1455–1464 (Hrunting), 2680 (Naegling), 303–306 and 1448–1454 (boar-figures on helmets); pattern-welding in early medieval swords (Hilda Ellis Davidson, *The Sword in Anglo-Saxon England*, 1962); the Sutton Hoo helmet (British Museum 1939,1010.93), with boars' heads at the ends of its eyebrows and a flying creature formed by its nose and brows; the Benty Grange boar-crested helmet (7th century).

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. Two museum studies in ink and watercolour, side by side: on
the left, a long double-edged sword blade standing upright, its surface
showing swirling herringbone patterns like flowing water; on the right, the
Sutton Hoo helmet as reconstructed — an iron face-mask with eyebrows that
end in little gilded boar heads, a nose and brows forming a creature with
spread wings. A red-and-lapis interlace initial with a touch of gold begins
the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(above the studies, on plain vellum)*
> "Why did a sword have a name, like Hrunting? Because a good sword was almost a person. A smith twisted rods of iron together and hammered them into one blade, over and over. When it was polished, the blade showed patterns like flowing water. A sword like that was worth a fortune, and it was handed down from father to son."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below the studies, on plain vellum)*
> "Helmets were treasures too. The one found in a king's grave at Sutton Hoo, in England, has a winged creature across its face and little boars' heads at the ends of its eyebrows, like the boar-helmets in the poem."

> **LABEL** *(beside the blade)*
> "a pattern-welded blade"

> **LABEL** *(beside the helmet)*
> "the Sutton Hoo helmet, about 625"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows what a named sword meant — and the story is about to jump fifty years.
