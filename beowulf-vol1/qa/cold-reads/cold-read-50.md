# Cold read — canonical pages 1–50

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands without outside documentation. I could follow the characters, stakes, two major time periods, and every fight on one normal-paced read.

## Retell of the story so far

King Hrothgar of the Danes builds Heorot, a magnificent hall where his people feast, hear songs, and share the rewards of their loyalty. His queen, Wealhtheow, presides beside him; his sharp-tongued spokesman, Unferth, sits at his feet. But the music draws Grendel, a huge, dark, red-eyed creature from the fens. Grendel smashes into the hall after the warriors sleep and carries men away. Hrothgar loses thirty men in the first attack. Unferth tries filling the hall with armed defenders, but Grendel keeps coming. For twelve years Heorot stands empty at night while Hrothgar grows old waiting for help.

Across the sea among the Geats, nineteen-year-old Beowulf hears the story. People mocked him as a slow, dreamy boy, but he has grown into an enormous, confident warrior. Hrothgar once saved Beowulf's father, so Beowulf chooses to repay that debt. Fourteen Geats sail with him to Denmark. Hrothgar welcomes him warmly, but Unferth tries to humiliate him by recalling a childhood swimming race that Beowulf supposedly lost. Beowulf explains that a storm separated him from his friend and that he spent the following nights killing nine sea-monsters. He turns the insult back on Unferth and promises to show what a Geat can do.

Wealhtheow asks Beowulf to remember her young sons if he survives. Beowulf vows publicly to kill Grendel or die in Heorot. Because Grendel carries no sword, Beowulf removes his own armor and weapon and waits bare-handed. Grendel enters, snatches one Geat, and reaches for another, but Beowulf seizes his arm. The other Geats' swords cannot pierce the monster. Beowulf tears Grendel's arm off, and Grendel escapes to die in the dark. His claw is hung above Heorot's doors. Hrothgar embraces Beowulf as a son; Wealhtheow gives him a gold ring and again asks him to befriend her boys. The Danes sleep safely in Heorot for the first time in twelve years.

Grendel's mother comes from the black lake to avenge her son. She retrieves his severed arm and kills Hrothgar's oldest friend while Beowulf sleeps in a separate guest house. The grieving Hrothgar tells Beowulf where she lives. Beowulf agrees to pursue her. At the lake, Unferth lends him his famous sword, Hrunting, and Beowulf dives alone through a mass of water-creatures to a hall at the bottom.

Hrunting, which has never failed before, cannot wound Grendel's mother. Beowulf discards it and fights with his hands, but her knife cannot pierce his mail. He sees an immense sword made by giants, lifts it, and kills her. Light floods the underwater hall; the giant blade melts away until only its golden hilt remains. The Danes on shore give him up for dead, but his thirteen Geats wait. Beowulf surfaces with Hrunting and the golden hilt. He returns Hrunting without blaming Unferth, publicly calling him generous for lending it. Hrothgar explains the ancient hilt, warns Beowulf that strength and fame pass, and tells him not to become proud. Beowulf is too young to absorb the warning. He returns home laden with treasure.

Fifty years pass. Beowulf's uncle, the Geatish king, and then the king's son die in war. Beowulf becomes king and rules so powerfully that no enemy attacks the Geats. He is now white-haired and old, but still physically formidable, and still wears Wealhtheow's gold ring.

A beaten runaway hides in an ancient coastal burial mound and steals one cup from a dragon's treasure. The dragon notices the missing cup and burns the coast, including Beowulf's hall. A young warrior reports the attack and the runaway's knowledge of the lair. Beowulf recognizes him as Wiglaf, the son of his cousin. Beowulf orders a wooden shield that can withstand fire. He admits to Wiglaf that he has always been afraid, but says fear cannot make him run after he has publicly promised to fight. He allows only ten men to accompany him and insists that the dragon is his own battle.

At the barrow, Beowulf thinks about Hrothgar's warning and understands that fire, sword, and old age have arrived together. He orders the men to stay on the hill. The dragon's fire overwhelms his shield, and his old sword Naegling wounds the dragon only shallowly. The ten retainers flee. Wiglaf reminds them that they accepted Beowulf's gifts and promised to help him, but none turns back, so he runs to Beowulf alone.

Wiglaf's shield burns, and Beowulf pulls him in behind his own. Beowulf rebukes him for disobeying; Wiglaf replies that Beowulf taught him courage means acting while afraid. Beowulf strikes again, but his own strength shatters Naegling. The dragon bites his neck. Wiglaf stabs the dragon in its softer belly, and Beowulf kills it with his knife. The bite is poisoned. Dying beside Wiglaf, Beowulf says Hrothgar was right: he has nothing left except the treasure he has won for his people. He asks Wiglaf to enter the barrow quickly and bring the treasure out so he can see it before he dies.

## Cast

- **Beowulf:** A very tall, broad, exceptionally strong Geat with blond hair, blue eyes, and a blue cloak over mail. At nineteen he is proud, direct, and hungry to prove himself, but also generous and tactful when returning Unferth's failed sword. Fifty years later he has swept-back white hair and a short white beard, remains powerfully built, and is the long-reigning king of the Geats. His central struggle is not the absence of fear but the refusal to break his word because of it.

- **King Hrothgar:** The aged king of the Danes, recognizable by his extremely long white hair and beard, narrow gold crown, red robes, and heavy fur mantle. He built Heorot, once rescued Beowulf's father, and becomes a father figure to Beowulf. His grief over his people and his oldest friend drives the two monster quests. His warning about pride, mortality, and the eventual coming of sickness, sword, fire, or age frames Beowulf's final battle.

- **Queen Wealhtheow:** Hrothgar's queen, a grave, auburn-haired woman in a green-and-cream gown, veil, gold ornaments, and amber necklace. She performs the ceremonial giving of the cup and later the gold ring. She is also a protective mother who asks Beowulf to look after her two young sons.

- **Unferth:** Hrothgar's lean, dark-haired spokesman, with a pointed black beard and black clothing. He is intelligent, jealous, cutting, and initially hostile to Beowulf. His attempt to shame Beowulf over the swimming race fails, but he later lends Beowulf Hrunting and receives the sword back with more grace from Beowulf than he expects.

- **Grendel:** A huge, hunched, long-armed humanoid monster with dark, stony scales, claws, sharp teeth, and glowing red eyes. He hates Heorot's music and terrorizes it for twelve years. His hide defeats swords, but Beowulf kills him by tearing off his arm.

- **Grendel's mother:** A large, dark-scaled, green-eyed female creature with long wet strands like weed for hair. She lives in a hall beneath a black mountain lake. She attacks Heorot to avenge Grendel, retrieves his arm, kills Hrothgar's oldest friend, and is killed by Beowulf with the giant-made sword in her hall.

- **Hrothgar's oldest friend:** A bald, grey-bearded veteran in fur and mail who comforts Hrothgar after the first massacre. Grendel's mother carries him away and kills him, turning the victory feast into a second quest.

- **The Geats:** Beowulf's fourteen companions at first, rugged mail-clad seafarers from across the water. One is taken immediately by Grendel. The remaining thirteen fight beside Beowulf and wait loyally at the lake after the Danes abandon hope.

- **Wiglaf:** A slight, curly dark-haired young warrior with a round yellow shield, identified as the son of Beowulf's cousin. He begins as an earnest messenger and admirer. He asks Beowulf about fear, then becomes the only retainer who keeps his promise in the dragon fight. He helps kill the dragon and remains with the poisoned king.

- **The dragon:** An enormous black-and-bronze, winged, fire-breathing creature with glowing cracks between its scales. It guards a burial hoard in a coastal barrow, knows every object in it, and attacks the Geats when one cup is stolen.

- **The runaway:** A small, ragged, frightened man abused badly enough to hide in the barrow. He steals the dragon's cup, later goes to Beowulf's people, and leads the king and his men to the lair.

- **The poet:** A round-faced brown-haired singer who plays a small lyre in Heorot. He preserves the community's history in song and turns Beowulf's victories into the story by which others know him.

## Causal chain

1. Hrothgar builds Heorot and fills it with nightly music and fellowship.
2. The sound enrages Grendel, who raids the hall and kills the Danes for twelve years.
3. Sailors spread the story, and Beowulf hears it across the sea.
4. Because Hrothgar once saved his father, Beowulf sails with fourteen Geats to repay the debt.
5. Beowulf's public vow and his answer to Unferth commit him to facing Grendel without weapons.
6. Beowulf tears off Grendel's arm, ending the first terror and winning Hrothgar's love and rich gifts.
7. Grendel's death brings his mother to Heorot for revenge; she kills Hrothgar's oldest friend and reclaims the arm.
8. Hrothgar's grief sends Beowulf to the black lake. Unferth's loan of Hrunting gives him a weapon, though it fails.
9. The failure of Hrunting forces Beowulf to use his hands and then the giant sword, with which he kills Grendel's mother.
10. Beowulf's victories bring him home with wealth and renown. Later deaths in the royal family make him king, and he rules safely for fifty years.
11. A mistreated runaway steals one cup from a guarded burial hoard.
12. The dragon notices the loss and burns Beowulf's land and hall.
13. Beowulf's lifetime of public promises and solitary heroism leads him to claim the dragon as his own fight, despite Hrothgar's old warning and his age.
14. Beowulf's retainers flee when the fight turns deadly; Wiglaf's memory of their accepted gifts and promises makes him return alone.
15. Wiglaf's intervention creates the opening that lets Beowulf kill the dragon, but the dragon's poisoned bite leaves Beowulf dying.
16. Beowulf asks to see the treasure because it is the last tangible benefit he can leave his people.

## Backtracking, rereading, or guesses

None required. I did not have to backtrack to identify a speaker, character, location, object, or time period. The fifty-year jump on page 37 is explicit, and old Beowulf remains recognizable through his build, blue cloak, gold neck ring, and facial features. Hrothgar's warning on page 34 returns clearly in Beowulf's thoughts on pages 43–44.

The only inference I made was intentional and immediately supported: on page 24, the bald older warrior taken by Grendel's mother is Hrothgar's “oldest friend.” I recognized him as the man who had comforted Hrothgar on page 4, and page 25 confirms his role in Hrothgar's grief. This did not require a guess about who had died or why it mattered.

## Text or attribution failures at reading pace

None. Every narration block and speech balloon was legible at reading pace, and every spoken line had a clear speaker. The dialogue order within panels was natural. I did not confuse Hrothgar, Unferth, Beowulf, Wiglaf, either monster, or any of the two groups of retainers.

## What I expect next

I expect Wiglaf to enter the barrow, see the hoard, and bring enough of it out for Beowulf to look on before he dies. Beowulf will probably entrust his people or kingship to Wiglaf, since Wiglaf alone kept the bond between a ring-giver and his warrior. I expect Beowulf's death to leave the Geats dangerously exposed: the safety described during his fifty-year reign depended on his reputation, while the ten fleeing retainers have shown that the next generation is not ready to replace him. The treasure may prove less like a reward than the material for Beowulf's funeral or memorial, and the deserters will likely face judgment when they return.
