# Cold read — canonical pages 1–57

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands without outside documentation. I could follow the people, motives, geography, two major time jumps, fights, and ending on a single normal-paced read.

## Retell

King Hrothgar of the Danes builds Heorot, a magnificent hall meant to be the warm center of his kingdom. His queen, Wealhtheow, gives him the first cup at its opening feast; his spokesman Unferth jokes at the king's expense, and the court poet sings. Out in the dark, the monster Grendel hears the music. That night he breaks into Heorot and kills thirty sleeping men. Hrothgar and Unferth try to oppose him, but neither weapons nor courage can keep the monster away. After twelve winters of attacks, the Danes abandon the hall at night and Hrothgar waits for help.

Across the sea among the Geats, nineteen-year-old Beowulf hears the story. He had been mocked as a slow, sleepy boy, but he remembers that Hrothgar once helped his father and decides to repay the debt. Fourteen Geats sail with him to Denmark. Hrothgar welcomes him warmly and gives him Heorot for the night. Beowulf promises to fight Grendel without a sword because the monster carries none. Unferth tries to shame him with the story of a swimming race Beowulf lost, but Beowulf explains that a storm separated him from his friend and that he spent the following nights killing nine sea-monsters. He then turns the insult back on Unferth: if Unferth were as brave as he boasts, Grendel would not have ruled the hall for twelve years. Wealhtheow asks Beowulf to protect her two young sons if he survives; Beowulf publicly swears that he will.

Grendel attacks that night, kills the nearest Geat, and reaches for another. Beowulf catches his arm. The Geats' swords cannot cut the monster, but Beowulf's grip terrifies him. Beowulf tears off Grendel's arm, and Grendel flees to die in the dark. The claw is hung over Heorot's doors. Hrothgar embraces Beowulf like a son, the Danes celebrate, and Wealhtheow gives Beowulf a gold ring while reminding him of her sons.

The victory is not complete. Grendel's mother comes to Heorot, takes back her son's severed arm, and kills Hrothgar's oldest friend. Hrothgar says that mother and son came from a black lake in the rocks. Beowulf and the others ride there. Unferth lends Beowulf his famous sword, Hrunting, and Beowulf dives alone. Grendel's mother drags him into her hall beneath the lake. Hrunting fails against her, and her knife cannot pierce his mail. Beowulf sees an enormous sword made by giants, uses it to kill her, and emerges with its golden hilt after the blade melts. The Danes had given him up for dead, but his thirteen Geats were still waiting. Back at Heorot, Beowulf returns Hrunting without humiliating Unferth, even after Unferth calls it a failed sword. Hrothgar warns the young hero that strength is temporary and that sickness, weapons, fire, or age come for everyone. Beowulf barely listens. He sails home laden with gifts.

Fifty winters pass. Beowulf's uncle and then his uncle's son die, and Beowulf becomes king of the Geats. He rules so successfully that no enemy attacks them. He is now an old, white-haired man, still wearing Wealhtheow's ring.

A beaten runaway hides in an ancient seaside grave mound, finds a sleeping dragon on a hoard, and steals one cup. The dragon notices the missing cup and burns the coast, including Beowulf's hall. Young Wiglaf, the son of Beowulf's cousin, reports the destruction. Beowulf orders an iron shield made and refuses to raise an army: he killed Grendel and Grendel's mother himself, so he means to kill the dragon himself. Wiglaf asks whether Beowulf was ever afraid. Beowulf says he was afraid every time; what mattered was that once everyone had heard his boast, he could not run away. Wiglaf says his late father taught him that their family always stands by Beowulf. Beowulf allows him and ten other men to come only as witnesses.

The runaway leads them to the barrow. Beowulf remembers Hrothgar's warning and recognizes that fire, the sword, and old age have all arrived together. He orders the men to stay back and challenges the dragon alone. His old sword, Naegling, wounds but cannot stop it, and the dragon's fire drives him back. The other men flee despite Wiglaf's reminder that they swore to help their king. Wiglaf goes on alone. His shield burns, but he reaches Beowulf. Beowulf admits fear and attacks again. Naegling breaks because Beowulf's strength is too great for any sword. The dragon bites his neck with poisoned teeth. Wiglaf wounds its belly, and Beowulf finishes it with his knife.

Dying from the poison, Beowulf sends Wiglaf into the barrow so he can see what he has won for his people. Wiglaf finds a vast hoard and a self-lit golden banner. Beowulf gives Wiglaf his ring, helmet, mail, and the care of his family, and asks for a high seaside mound called Beowulf's Barrow. He dies with Wiglaf holding his hand. When the deserters return, Wiglaf tells them they may keep their lives but not their names; no one will sing about them. He refuses to let anyone wear the hoard that cost Beowulf his life, apart from the ring Beowulf personally gave him. The Geats burn Beowulf with weapons and armor, bury his ashes and the dragon's gold in the high mound, and ride around it singing that he was gentle, kind to his people, and eager for fame. Sailors thereafter recognize the cliff as Beowulf's Barrow. A final historical page explains that the story survives in a single medieval manuscript and notes its echo in Tolkien's *The Hobbit*.

## Cast

- **Beowulf:** First a very tall, broad, blond nineteen-year-old in mail with a deep blue cloak; later the same imposing man with white hair, a short white beard, weathered skin, and the same blue cloak. He is a mocked boy turned Geat hero, then a long-reigning king. His public boasts force him to act despite fear. In youth he repays his father's debt to Hrothgar; in age he fights for his own people and entrusts them to Wiglaf.
- **King Hrothgar:** An elderly Dane with very long white hair and beard, a gold circlet, red robes, and a fur mantle. He built Heorot, once helped Beowulf's father, loses his men and closest friend to the monsters, receives Beowulf like a son, and gives him the warning about mortality that Beowulf understands only in old age.
- **Queen Wealhtheow:** A pale, auburn-haired woman in a green gown, gold jewelry, and a white veil. She is Hrothgar's queen and the mother of two young sons. She performs the cup-giving ceremony, asks Beowulf to protect her children, and gives him the gold ring he wears for the rest of his life.
- **Unferth:** A lean, dark-haired man with a pointed beard, black clothing, and a sword. He is Hrothgar's sharp-tongued spokesman. He first mocks Beowulf, is defeated verbally, later lends him Hrunting, and is treated generously when the sword fails.
- **Hrothgar's oldest friend:** An old, bald or close-shaven, gray-bearded warrior in mail and fur. He comforts Hrothgar after the first slaughter and is later killed by Grendel's mother, driving the second hunt.
- **The scop:** A round-faced, brown-haired man in a plain brown robe who sings with a small harp. He is Hrothgar's court poet and turns victories and old tales into communal memory.
- **Grendel:** A huge, long-armed, dark gray-black creature with overlapping stony scales, claws, a skull-like face, and glowing red eyes. Music draws him to Heorot; he terrorizes it for twelve years until Beowulf tears off his arm.
- **Grendel's mother:** A large dark, green-eyed lake creature with long weed-like hair. She avenges her son, kills Hrothgar's friend, and is killed by Beowulf in the hall beneath the lake.
- **The Geats who sail with Beowulf:** Thirteen bearded, mailed warriors after the first of their original fourteen is killed by Grendel. They support Beowulf against Grendel and wait by the lake after the Danes give him up for dead.
- **Wiglaf:** A slim young man with curly dark hair, a rust-red tunic under mail, and initially a yellow round shield. He is Beowulf's cousin's son and the only one of the dragon expedition who honors his oath. He helps kill the dragon, stays with Beowulf as he dies, receives the king's heirlooms and responsibilities, rebukes the deserters, and appears to become the keeper of Beowulf's memory and people.
- **The runaway:** A thin, ragged, frightened young man. He steals the dragon's cup after hiding from a beating, accidentally triggers the destruction, and later leads Beowulf to the barrow.
- **The dragon:** An immense winged dragon with black-bronze scales glowing orange from within. It guards an ancient burial hoard, wakes when one cup is stolen, burns the Geats' coast, and kills Beowulf before Beowulf and Wiglaf kill it.

## Causal chain

Hrothgar builds Heorot and fills it with music -> Grendel is drawn to the hall and begins twelve years of killings -> sailors spread the Danes' story -> Beowulf hears it, remembers Hrothgar's aid to his father, and sails to repay the debt -> Beowulf's public vow and refusal of weapons commit him to face Grendel -> he tears off Grendel's arm -> Grendel's mother comes to avenge her son and kills Hrothgar's friend -> Beowulf follows her to the lake -> Hrunting fails, forcing him to use the giant sword -> he kills her and restores Heorot -> Hrothgar warns that every hero will eventually fall to sickness, weapons, fire, or age -> Beowulf returns home, eventually becomes king, and rules for fifty years -> a runaway steals one cup from a dragon's hoard -> the dragon retaliates by burning the Geats' coast and Beowulf's hall -> Beowulf's old heroic code and sense of responsibility make him refuse an army -> he makes his men witnesses but orders them not to fight -> when the fight turns against him, the men obey fear rather than their old promises and flee -> Wiglaf's family loyalty and Beowulf's lesson that courage coexists with fear send him back alone -> together they kill the dragon, but its poisoned bite kills Beowulf -> Beowulf's death and last gifts make Wiglaf his moral heir -> Wiglaf preserves Beowulf's chosen ring, rejects the fatal hoard for personal use, arranges the funeral and barrow, and carries forward the king's story.

## What I expect next

No further plot is required: page 56 gives a complete legendary ending, and page 57 reads as closing historical back matter. If another page followed, I would expect credits, sources, or another short note about the manuscript and the poem's later influence, not a continuation of Beowulf's life. Within the story world, Wiglaf is positioned to bear responsibility for the Geats, but the book deliberately leaves their future uncertain after the loss of the king who kept enemies away for fifty years.
