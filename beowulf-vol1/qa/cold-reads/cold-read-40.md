## Retell of the story so far

King Hrothgar of the Danes builds Heorot, the greatest hall in the world, and fills it with feasting, gifts, and music. A huge marsh creature named Grendel hates the sound of that happiness. One night he smashes into the hall and carries off thirty sleeping men. Hrothgar's sharp-tongued spokesman Unferth promises to defend Heorot, but Grendel keeps returning. For twelve winters the Danes abandon the hall at night while an aging Hrothgar waits for help.

Across the sea, a young Geat named Beowulf hears the story. His countrymen once dismissed him as a slow, sleepy boy, but he believes he owes Hrothgar a debt because the king once saved his father's life. Beowulf sails to Denmark with fourteen companions. Hrothgar welcomes him. Beowulf promises to fight Grendel without a sword because the monster uses none.

Unferth tries to discredit Beowulf by saying he once lost a seven-night swimming race. Beowulf explains that a storm separated the swimmers and that he spent those nights killing nine sea-monsters. He turns Unferth's taunt back on him: if Unferth were as formidable as his words, Grendel would not have owned the hall for twelve years. Queen Wealhtheow gives Beowulf the ceremonial cup, tells him her sons have never safely slept in their father's hall, and hears his public vow to win or die there.

Hrothgar leaves Heorot to Beowulf and the Geats for the night. Grendel enters, kills the nearest Geat, and reaches for another. Beowulf catches his arm. The Geats' swords cannot pierce the monster, but Beowulf's grip terrifies him. Beowulf tears off Grendel's arm, and Grendel escapes into the dark to die. The Danes hang the claw over Heorot's doors. Hrothgar embraces Beowulf as a son; Beowulf regrets that he could not bring back the whole monster. At the victory feast, Wealhtheow gives Beowulf a gold ring and asks him to remain a friend to her young sons.

That night Grendel's mother comes from the black lake to avenge her son. She retrieves his severed arm and kills Hrothgar's oldest friend before escaping. Beowulf had been sleeping in a separate guest house and knew nothing until morning. He comforts the grieving king and asks to be shown the lake.

The Danes and the thirteen surviving Geats ride to the haunted water. Unferth lends Beowulf his famous sword, Hrunting, and Beowulf dives alone. Grendel's mother drags him into a hall beneath the lake. Hrunting fails to cut her, so Beowulf throws it aside and fights with his hands. Her knife cannot pierce his mail. He sees an enormous sword made by giants, lifts it, and kills her. Light fills the underwater hall, and the giant blade melts until only its golden hilt remains.

On shore, the Danes eventually assume Beowulf is dead and leave, but his thirteen Geats keep waiting. Beowulf surfaces with Hrunting and the golden hilt. Back in Heorot, he returns Hrunting without humiliating Unferth, even calling it a good sword; when Unferth admits that it failed, Beowulf publicly praises him for lending it. Hrothgar examines the ancient hilt and warns Beowulf that strength and glory do not last. Beowulf listens politely but, at nineteen, does not truly absorb the warning.

Hrothgar and Beowulf part like father and son. Beowulf sails home laden with treasure and gives nearly all of it away among the Geats. Fifty winters pass. After Beowulf's uncle and then his cousin die, the Geats make Beowulf their king. He rules long and well, becomes white-haired, and still wears Wealhtheow's ring.

A runaway hiding from a beating enters an ancient coastal grave and finds a dragon asleep on a mountain of treasure. He steals one cup. The dragon notices the missing piece and comes out at night to burn the coast, including Beowulf's own hall. In the ashes, a young warrior reports that the runaway knows the dragon's lair. Beowulf recognizes the messenger as Wiglaf, his cousin's son. He orders a wooden shield replaced with an iron one, refuses Wiglaf's offer to gather the army, and decides to fight the dragon himself just as he fought Grendel.

## Cast

- **Beowulf:** At nineteen, a very tall, broad, muscular blond Geat with a blue cloak, mail shirt, and an open, confident manner. Once dismissed as a slow boy, he is now a fearless champion who wants to repay a family debt and prove what he can do. He is generous and unexpectedly gracious to Unferth after their rivalry. Fifty years later he is a white-haired, white-bearded king, still physically formidable and still wearing the blue cloak and Wealhtheow's gold ring.

- **King Hrothgar:** A very old Danish king with extremely long white hair and beard, a narrow gold crown, red robes, and a heavy fur mantle. He built Heorot, carries guilt for the men lost there, loves Beowulf like a son, and provides the story's warning that strength and glory end.

- **Queen Wealhtheow:** Hrothgar's red-haired queen, usually in a green-and-cream gown, gold ornaments, and a white veil. She performs the hall's cup ceremony, speaks directly about the danger to her children, rewards Beowulf, and asks him to protect her sons.

- **Unferth:** A lean, dark-haired, sharp-featured man with a pointed beard, dressed in black and carrying a sword. He is Hrothgar's clever spokesman and begins as Beowulf's jealous verbal rival. He later lends Beowulf Hrunting, admits its failure, and accepts Beowulf's generosity.

- **Grendel:** A towering gray-black, scaled humanoid from the fens, with glowing red eyes, very long arms, hooked claws, and a mouth of sharp teeth. He hates Heorot's music and becomes the killer who empties the hall for twelve years.

- **Grendel's mother:** A large, dark-scaled water creature with green-yellow eyes and long weedlike hair. She is visually related to Grendel but distinct from him. Grief and revenge drive her to reclaim his arm and kill Hrothgar's friend.

- **Hrothgar's oldest friend:** A stocky, bald or close-shaven older warrior with a white beard, fur, and mail. He supports Hrothgar after the first disaster and is the important personal loss in the mother's revenge.

- **The king's poet:** A round-faced, brown-haired man in a plain brown robe who sings with a small harp or lyre. He turns victories and old stories into songs.

- **The Geats:** Beowulf's rough, mail-clad companions. Fourteen sail with him; one dies in Heorot, and the remaining thirteen wait at the lake after the Danes give up hope.

- **The Danish coast guard:** A mounted, helmeted spear-carrier who challenges the armed strangers on the beach, accepts Beowulf's explanation, and directs them to Heorot.

- **Wealhtheow's sons:** Two small brown-haired boys. They embody what the queen fears losing and what she asks Beowulf to safeguard.

- **The runaway:** A slight, ragged young man hiding from a beating. He steals a golden cup from the dragon's hoard and unintentionally starts the present disaster.

- **The dragon:** An enormous winged, horned dragon with dark bronze-black scales that glow like embers. It knows every object in its hoard and answers the stolen cup by burning Beowulf's coast and hall.

- **Wiglaf:** A young, curly dark-haired warrior in mail with a round yellow shield. He is the son of Beowulf's cousin, eager to call an army, and newly reintroduced to Beowulf after having last been seen as a baby.

## Causal chain

1. Hrothgar creates a rich, joyful hall whose music reaches the fens.
2. Grendel's hatred of that music brings him to Heorot, where he kills thirty men.
3. Repeated attacks empty Heorot at night for twelve years, and news of the Danes' helplessness crosses the sea.
4. Beowulf hears the story and feels bound to help because Hrothgar once saved his father.
5. Beowulf's vow and bare-handed strength let him defeat a monster that weapons cannot wound.
6. Grendel's death and displayed arm provoke his mother's revenge.
7. The death of Hrothgar's closest friend sends Beowulf to the black lake.
8. Hrunting's failure forces Beowulf to rely on himself until he finds the giant-made sword that can kill Grendel's mother.
9. Beowulf's victories bring him treasure, honor, and a father-son bond with Hrothgar; his generosity helps establish the kind of leader he will become.
10. Two deaths in the Geat royal line eventually make Beowulf king, and fifty peaceful years follow.
11. A mistreated runaway steals one cup from a sleeping dragon.
12. The dragon detects the loss and burns the coast and Beowulf's hall.
13. The old king treats this new monster as a final personal test and refuses the army Wiglaf offers to gather.

## What I expect next

I expect Beowulf to go to the dragon's grave with the iron shield and little or no army. His insistence on repeating the solitary feat of his youth, combined with Hrothgar's old warning and the emphasis on Beowulf's age, makes me expect this victory to cost him his life. Wiglaf has been introduced too specifically to remain behind: I expect him to follow, aid Beowulf when others cannot or will not, and become the loyal younger figure who carries the Geats forward. I also expect the stolen cup and the hoard to matter after the fight, not just the dragon itself.
