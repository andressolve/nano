## Retell of the story so far

King Hrothgar of the Danes has built Heorot, the greatest hall in the world, as a warm, bright place of feasting, music, gift-giving, and fellowship. That joy enrages Grendel, a huge creature living in the nearby fens. After the first feast, Grendel breaks through Heorot's barred doors and carries off thirty sleeping men. Hrothgar's spokesman Unferth promises an armed defense, but weapons do not stop the monster. Night after night, anyone who remains in Heorot dies. For twelve winters the Danes abandon the hall after dark while Hrothgar grows old waiting for help.

Across the sea, the story reaches Beowulf, a young Geat whom people once dismissed as slow and lazy. Beowulf knows that Hrothgar once saved his father's life and decides it is now his turn to repay that debt. Fourteen warriors sail with him to Denmark. A coast guard challenges them, then directs them to the golden-roofed hall. Hrothgar recognizes Beowulf as his old friend's son and allows him to face Grendel. Because Grendel carries no sword, Beowulf vows to fight him bare-handed.

Unferth, jealous of Hrothgar's welcome, tries to discredit Beowulf by recalling a childhood swimming race that Beowulf lost. Beowulf explains that a storm separated the swimmers and that he spent the following nights killing nine sea-monsters before reaching shore. He then turns the insult back on Unferth: if Unferth were as formidable as his words, Grendel would not have ruled the hall for twelve years. Queen Wealhtheow offers Beowulf the ceremonial cup and tells him that other men have made great promises in Heorot before. She reveals the personal stakes—her sons have never slept a night in their father's hall. Beowulf publicly swears that he will win or die there.

Hrothgar entrusts Heorot to Beowulf and leads the Danes away. Beowulf removes his armor, gives his sword to a companion, and waits awake among the sleeping Geats. Grendel comes from the moor, enters the hall, snatches the nearest Geat, and reaches for another. Beowulf catches his arm. Grendel is frightened by the strength of Beowulf's grip. The other Geats attack, but their swords rebound from the monster's hide. Beowulf holds on and tears off Grendel's arm. Grendel escapes into the night to die, leaving the arm behind as a trophy. In the morning the Danes hang the claw over Heorot's doors, follow the bloody tracks to a black lake, and celebrate Beowulf along the way. The lake churns, but nothing emerges.

## Cast

- **Beowulf:** A tall, powerfully built young Geat with long fair hair. He first appears in chain mail and a deep blue cloak, then strips down to a brown tunic for the fight. He is confident, direct, physically extraordinary, and motivated by both courage and a family debt to Hrothgar. He leads the fourteen Geats and defeats Grendel bare-handed.
- **Grendel:** A towering, long-armed humanoid monster with dark, stony or scaled skin, red eyes, and very long black claws. He lives in the fens, hates the music from Heorot, and has terrorized the Danes for twelve winters. Beowulf mortally wounds him by tearing off his arm.
- **King Hrothgar:** An elderly Danish king with very long white hair and beard, a gold circlet, red-and-gold robes, and furs. He built Heorot, once saved Beowulf's father, and has been broken down by years of being unable to protect his people.
- **Queen Wealhtheow:** A red-haired woman in a green-and-cream gown, white veil, and heavy gold jewelry. She performs the hall's cup ceremony, speaks plainly about failed promises, and gives the conflict its family stakes by mentioning her two sons.
- **Unferth:** Hrothgar's lean, dark-haired spokesman, dressed in black, with a narrow mustache and beard. He speaks sharply, proposes the failed sword defense, resents the king's favor toward Beowulf, and challenges Beowulf's reputation with the swimming story.
- **The fourteen Geats:** Beowulf's bearded, armed companions. They cross the sea with him, sleep in Heorot, and try unsuccessfully to wound Grendel with swords while Beowulf grapples him. One unnamed Geat is taken when Grendel enters.
- **Hrothgar's oldest friend:** An older bald, white-bearded warrior in mail and fur. He supports the king physically on the morning after the first massacre but has no distinct later action in these pages.
- **The poet:** A round-faced man in a plain brown robe who plays a small harp and sings of the world's creation at the opening feast. His music is part of what Grendel hears and hates.
- **The Danish coast guard:** An armored, mounted spearman who intercepts the arriving Geats, questions Beowulf, and points them toward Heorot.
- **Hrothgar and Wealhtheow's sons:** Two boys seen together during the queen's appeal. They represent the future of the royal family and have never been able to sleep safely in Heorot.

## Causal chain

Hrothgar builds Heorot as the center of Danish joy and fellowship → its music enrages Grendel → Grendel kills thirty men in his first attack → Unferth's armed resistance fails and Heorot is abandoned nightly for twelve winters → sailors carry the story to the Geats → Beowulf sees a chance to repay Hrothgar's help to his father and sails with fourteen companions → Hrothgar admits him to the hall → Beowulf chooses an unarmed fight because Grendel uses no sword → Unferth's taunt prompts Beowulf to establish his past endurance and publicly raise the stakes → Wealhtheow's appeal makes the danger to Hrothgar's family explicit → Beowulf vows to win or die and waits in Heorot → Grendel attacks the Geats → Beowulf's grip prevents him from continuing the slaughter → the Geats' swords fail, so Beowulf wins by strength alone and tears off Grendel's arm → the wounded monster flees toward the black lake → the Danes reclaim the hall, display the claw, track Grendel, and celebrate Beowulf.

## What I expect next

Immediately, I expect Hrothgar and Wealhtheow to celebrate and reward Beowulf, with Unferth forced to acknowledge the victory. The violent movement in the black lake makes Grendel's death feel like the end of the first threat rather than the end of the larger danger. I expect something connected to Grendel to emerge from, or draw Beowulf into, that mere. The title also promises a dragon later, so I expect the lake conflict to form a second movement before the story eventually turns to that separate, larger threat.
