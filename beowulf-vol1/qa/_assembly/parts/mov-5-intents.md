## PAGE 33

Dramatic. Evening in Heorot, the hall full of Danes, the old king beaming on his high seat with the golden hilt from under the lake. Beowulf, wearing the queen's gold neck-ring, finds Unferth and holds Hrunting out across both palms, exactly as Unferth once offered it to him at the lake, and thanks him: it's a good sword. Unferth will not take it; ashamed, he says everyone knows it failed. Beowulf says it again, louder, so the whole hall hears, and calls him generous. Unferth takes the sword back and bows his head over it, with nothing to say. The page belongs to the two former rivals and the sword passing between them; the king and the watching Danes stay subordinate. The dominant turn: the rivals are friends, and the old king has something to say to Beowulf alone. The reader turns to hear it.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. Late night, the hall empty except for two men by the hearth. Old Hrothgar turns the golden hilt in the firelight, its carving of giants and great waves, and tells young Beowulf that the giants who made it were strong too, and a flood took every one of them. Then, leaning in close and serious, he warns him not to let pride grow: strength is lent for a little while, and sickness, the sword, fire or old age come for everyone. Beowulf, rubbing the gold ring at his neck, nods politely at the fire and says "Yes, my lord," and the caption tells us he didn't hear a word. The page belongs to the old king's warning and the young man's polite, absent face; the empty hall stays dark around them. The dominant turn: the warning has been given, and it is time to go home.

## PAGE 35

Illustrated prose. Two places, because the turn is the journey home. First, the white-cliffed Danish beach at morning: the old king holds Beowulf in his arms like a son, weeping, his long white beard against the young man's shoulder, while the queen and Unferth stand behind and the Geats load horses and chests of gold onto the ship. Then the Geats' harbour: Beowulf, grinning, hands gold cups and arm-rings out of a chest to the same crowd that once laughed at him. The prose field over the morning sky carries the parting, the gold, and the end of "the slow boy by the fire." The embrace owns the page; the harbour is the payoff beneath it. The dominant turn: the young hero is home and famous, and he has forgotten the warning.

## PAGE 36

Chronicle. The book steps out of the story onto a page of an old chronicle to answer the question the last pages raised: why did Unferth's sword have a name, as if it were a person? Two museum studies in ink and wash sit side by side as one image: a long pattern-welded blade standing upright, its surface rippling like flowing water, and the Sutton Hoo helmet with its winged creature across the face and little boars' heads at the ends of its eyebrows. The fields explain how a smith twisted and hammered iron into such a blade, why it was worth a fortune and passed from father to son, and that helmets were treasures too. The tone is a museum guide's, curious and specific, never a verdict on the story. The dominant turn: the reader knows what a named sword meant, and the story is about to jump fifty years.
