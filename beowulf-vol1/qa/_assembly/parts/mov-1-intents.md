## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The book opens on a black fen at night under a cold rising moon: dark pools, reeds, mist lying in long strips. Far off on a hill a great hall blazes with gold light and drifting music. In the foreground, low among the reeds, something enormous crouches with its back to us: only the curve of a huge stooped grey-green back, a long arm resting in the water, and two eyes glowing like coals, turned toward that hall. The title sits in the night sky above the hall; one caption says that something in the fens hated the sound of music. The dominant turn: something in the dark is listening to that hall, and it hates what it hears. The creature stays half-seen and silent; the gulf between the cold dark and the distant gold owns the page. The reader turns to see what it will do.

## PAGE 2

Dramatic. Inside Heorot by firelight, a feast: the long hearth, carved pillars, shields on the walls, crowded benches. King Hrothgar, very thin with a white beard to his waist, raises his arms and tells the hall to drink, and gives the first cup to his queen; Wealhtheow, holding the great gold cup in both hands, gives it back to the king who built the hall. Behind him his oldest friend laughs. Then Unferth, the king's sly spokesman on his low stool, rises with his cup and promises a speech he has already written. Last, in one strip, the old poet sings by the fire while, outside the open doors in the blackness, two coal-red eyes watch. The dominant turn: the happiest hall in the world doesn't know it is being watched. The joy of the feast owns the page; the eyes stay small, low and outside. The reader turns because the thing outside has heard every note.

## PAGE 3

Illustrated prose. That same night, after the feast, the iron-barred double doors of Heorot burst inward in a storm of splinters; the long hearth-fire is blown flat. Across the doorway falls an enormous shadow, man-shaped and wrong, too tall, arms too long, hands ending in hooks, with two coal-red eyes in the doorway. On the benches the warriors still sleep under their blankets, dark shapes, nobody yet awake. The prose names the thing, Grendel, and tells how one claw broke the doors and he was across the hall before the first man woke. The dominant turn: the monster is inside the hall with the sleeping men. No one is harmed in the picture; the shadow and the splintering doors own the page. The reader turns to learn what is left in the morning.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. Grey morning in Heorot: benches overturned, blankets trailing, a cup on its side, the doors hanging off their hinges, huge clawed tracks in the mud leading away toward the fen. No bodies anywhere; the men are simply gone. Hrothgar stands grey-faced on his staff, his oldest friend gripping his shoulder, and cries out that he slept through it in his own house. Unferth, sword already drawn, steps up: tonight every sword in Denmark will wait in this hall. Then, close, the queen asks what happens if swords can't stop it, and the king answers bitterly that he built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into. The dominant turn: the Danes are going to fight it with swords, and the queen doubts that swords will be enough. The empty, wrecked hall owns the page; the queen's question is the last word. The reader turns to see whether the swords hold.

## PAGE 5

Illustrated prose. Time passes in one long breath. Heorot stands at night under snow, its gold roof white, its doors shut, not one light in it, while below the hill small houses huddle with barred shutters and a thin glow in each. Then, by day, inside the cold empty hall, old Hrothgar sits alone in his high seat, gaunter and older, staring at the dead hearth. The prose tells it plainly: Unferth's swords failed, whoever stayed after dark was gone by morning, soon nobody stayed, the king waited winter after winter for anyone to help, and sailors carried the story across the sea. The dominant turn: the story is crossing the sea, looking for someone. The dark snowbound hall owns the page; the lonely king is its echo. The reader turns to find who hears it.

## PAGE 6

Dramatic. Across the sea, in the Geats' harbour of turf-roofed houses and beached longships, two sailors tell the monster story to a crowd, one making claws of his hands. On an upturned boat sits Beowulf, a young man, huge and still rangy, tawny hair tied back, a deep sea-blue cloak, his grin gone, very still. The captions say that as a boy the whole country laughed at him and nobody expected anything of him. He stands, frowning, and says King Hrothgar once saved his father's life by paying a debt nobody else would pay. Then he jumps up on the boat, grinning, fist raised, and asks who is coming to Denmark with him; hands go up, and his companions say yes. The dominant turn: the boy everyone laughed at is going to fight the thing no army could stop. Beowulf owns the page; the sailors and crowd stay subordinate. The reader turns to put him on the sea.

## PAGE 7

Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from an old chronicle: warm ivory vellum, a ruled brown-ink margin, iron-gall ink and watercolour wash, one illuminated initial of red and lapis interlace knotwork touched with gold. The dominant image is a hand-drawn map of the lands around the North Sea and the Baltic long ago: the Danish islands and peninsula, southern Sweden across the water, the open sea between, the coast of England at the edge, a dotted sea-road joining the Geats' coast to the Danes, and a tiny long hall marking Lejre. The first field asks who the Danes and the Geats were and answers from the map; the second tells of the great halls found at Lejre and the poem made far away in England. The dominant turn: the reader knows where Heorot stood and how far Beowulf has to sail, and the next page puts him on the sea. No story character appears.
