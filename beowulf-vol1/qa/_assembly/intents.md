# Page intents

## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The book opens on a black fen at night under a cold rising moon: dark pools, reeds, mist lying in long strips. Far off on a hill a great hall blazes with gold light and drifting music. In the foreground, low among the reeds, something enormous crouches with its back to us: only the curve of a huge stooped grey-green back, a long arm resting in the water, and two eyes glowing like coals, turned toward that hall. The title sits in the night sky above the hall; one caption says that something in the fens hated the sound of music. The dominant turn: something in the dark is listening to that hall, and it hates what it hears. The creature stays half-seen and silent; the gulf between the cold dark and the distant gold owns the page. The reader turns to see what it will do.

## PAGE 2

Dramatic. Inside Heorot by firelight, a feast: the long hearth, carved pillars, shields on the walls, crowded benches. King Hrothgar, very thin with a white beard to his waist, raises his arms and tells the hall to drink, and gives the first cup to his queen; Wealhtheow, holding the great gold cup in both hands, gives it back to the king who built the hall. Behind him his oldest friend laughs. Then Unferth, the king's sly spokesman on his low stool, rises with his cup and promises a speech he has already written. Last, in one strip, the old poet sings by the fire while, outside the open doors in the blackness, two coal-red eyes watch. The dominant turn: the happiest hall in the world doesn't know it is being watched. The joy of the feast owns the page; the eyes stay small, low and outside. The reader turns because the thing outside has heard every note.

## PAGE 3

Illustrated prose. That same night, after the feast, the iron-barred double doors of Heorot burst inward in a storm of splinters; the long hearth-fire is blown flat. Across the doorway falls an enormous shadow, man-shaped and wrong, too tall, arms too long, hands ending in hooks, with two coal-red eyes in the doorway. On the benches the warriors still sleep under their blankets, dark shapes, nobody yet awake. The prose names the thing, Grendel, and tells how one claw broke the doors and he was across the hall before the first man woke. The dominant turn: the monster is inside the hall with the sleeping men. No one is harmed in the picture; the shadow and the splintering doors own the page. The reader turns to learn what is left in the morning.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. Grey morning in Heorot: benches overturned, blankets trailing, a cup on its side, the doors hanging off their hinges, huge clawed tracks in the mud leading away toward the fen. No bodies anywhere; the men are simply gone. Hrothgar stands grey-faced on his staff, his oldest friend gripping his shoulder, and cries out that he slept through it in his own house. Unferth, sword already drawn, steps up: tonight every sword in Denmark will wait in this hall. Then, close, the queen asks what happens if swords can't stop it, and the king answers bitterly that he built the greatest hall in the world for that thing to walk into. The dominant turn: the Danes are going to fight it with swords, and the queen doubts that swords will be enough. The empty, wrecked hall owns the page; the queen's question is the last word. The reader turns to see whether the swords hold.

## PAGE 5

Illustrated prose. Time passes in one long breath. Heorot stands at night under snow, its gold roof white, its doors shut, not one light in it, while below the hill small houses huddle with barred shutters and a thin glow in each. Then, by day, inside the cold empty hall, old Hrothgar sits alone in his high seat, gaunter and older, staring at the dead hearth. The prose tells it plainly: Unferth's swords failed, whoever stayed after dark was gone by morning, soon nobody stayed, the king waited winter after winter for anyone to help, and sailors carried the story across the sea. The dominant turn: the story is crossing the sea, looking for someone. The dark snowbound hall owns the page; the lonely king is its echo. The reader turns to find who hears it.

## PAGE 6

Dramatic. Across the sea, in the Geats' harbour of turf-roofed houses and beached longships, two sailors tell the monster story to a crowd, one making claws of his hands. On an upturned boat sits Beowulf, a young man, huge and still rangy, tawny hair tied back, a deep sea-blue cloak, his grin gone, very still. The captions say that as a boy the whole country laughed at him and nobody expected anything of him. He stands, frowning, and says King Hrothgar once saved his father's life by paying a debt nobody else would pay. Then he jumps up on the boat, grinning, fist raised, and asks who is coming to Denmark with him; hands go up, and his companions say yes. The dominant turn: the boy everyone laughed at is going to fight the thing no army could stop. Beowulf owns the page; the sailors and crowd stay subordinate. The reader turns to put him on the sea.

## PAGE 7

Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from an old chronicle: warm ivory vellum, a ruled brown-ink margin, iron-gall ink and watercolour wash, one illuminated initial of red and lapis interlace knotwork touched with gold. The dominant image is a hand-drawn map of the lands around the North Sea and the Baltic long ago: the Danish islands and peninsula, southern Sweden across the water, the open sea between, the coast of England at the edge, a dotted sea-road joining the Geats' coast to the Danes, and a tiny long hall marking Lejre. The first field asks who the Danes and the Geats were and answers from the map; the second tells of the great halls found at Lejre and the poem made far away in England. The dominant turn: the reader knows where Heorot stood and how far Beowulf has to sail, and the next page puts him on the sea. No story character appears.

## PAGE 8

Illustrated prose. One great image of a long striped-sailed ship running hard before the wind over grey-green seas, foam flying at the bow, his Geats aboard. Beowulf stands at the prow, one foot up on the gunwale, his blue cloak streaming, grinning into the spray: the happiest he has ever been, and the farthest from home. Far ahead on the horizon a thin line of white cliffs. The prose tells the crossing and ends on one word: Denmark. The dominant turn: they have arrived in the monster's country. The ship and Beowulf's joy own the page; the crew stays small, and the cliffs stay a thin promise at the edge of the sea.

## PAGE 9

Illustrated prose, two places joined by one road. On a pebble beach under white cliffs, a Danish guard on horseback has galloped down with his spear levelled at the armed strangers; Beowulf faces him with his helmet under one arm and his other hand open and empty, his Geats waiting behind. Then the guard lifts his spear and points it up a stone road climbing a green hill to Heorot, long and high, its roof shining gold. The prose carries the challenge and the decision. The dominant turn: Beowulf is about to stand in front of the king. The confrontation on the beach owns the page; the hall is the destination, glimpsed and shining, not yet entered.

## PAGE 10

Dramatic. Heorot by day. Old King Hrothgar leans out of his high seat with both hands reaching to the young stranger, remembering the debt he paid for Beowulf's father; Beowulf answers that this is why he has come and asks for one night in the hall. Then, close, Beowulf grins and holds up his two enormous empty hands: Grendel carries no sword, so neither will he. Last, close on Unferth at the king's feet, his eyes narrowing into a slow sneer, while a caption names him. The dominant turn: the young stranger has boasted in front of the whole hall, and Unferth is about to make him pay for it. The welcome owns the page; the queen and the Geats stay subordinate and silent.

## PAGE 11

Dramatic. Heorot by day. Unferth rises from his stool with a cup in his hand and turns to the whole hall like a performer, and asks whether this is the boy who swam out to sea racing another boy and lost. He enjoys it: a week in the water, beaten by a boy, so what will Grendel do to him? The benches roar with laughter. Beowulf, arms folded, is still grinning, but his eyes are not, and he tells Unferth he has had a lot of mead and offers to tell how the race really went. The dominant turn: Beowulf is about to tell the true story. Unferth's mockery owns the page; Beowulf's cold grin is the reply the reader waits for.

## PAGE 12

Illustrated prose. A flashback years before, on a black heaving winter sea at night under lightning. A big boy in a mail shirt, the young Beowulf with the same crooked nose and tied-back tawny hair, swims with a sword in his fist while a huge tusked sea-beast rises beside him, its coils wrapped around his legs; far off, small on the swell, another boy's head moves away. The prose tells what really happened: the two boys swimming side by side, the storm, the monsters he killed one after another until the sea was quiet, and nobody at home believing him. The dominant turn: Unferth has just heard what really happened, in front of everyone. The boy's fight with the sea owns the page.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Heorot by day. Beowulf spreads his hands to the hall, enormous, grinning and mock-apologetic: that is how he lost the race, he was busy killing sea-monsters. Unferth has stopped smiling. Then Beowulf steps toward him and the grin goes; very still and very plain, he says that if Unferth were half as fierce as his words Grendel would never have dared come back, and that tonight the Danes will see what a Geat can do. Last, the whole hall erupts, warriors pounding the tables, Hrothgar laughing out loud on his high seat, and Unferth sitting down with his face dark, saying nothing. The dominant turn: Beowulf has won the hall, and now he has to keep his word. Beowulf, enormous and grinning before the hall while Unferth stops smiling, owns the page; the plain, still threat follows it, and the hall's roar stays a small, distant strip.

## PAGE 14

Chronicle. The story steps aside and the page becomes a page from an old chronicle: ivory vellum, brown ink and watercolour, an illuminated interlace initial. One dominant image carries the idea: a cutaway of a great timber hall with its roof lifted away, a long hearth down the middle with smoke rising through the roof, benches along both walls with shields and spears above them, a high seat at the far end, great double doors; tiny figures feast at one end while at the other the same benches are laid with straw mattresses. The fields answer what a hall was, why everyone slept in it, and why the king was called the ring-giver. The dominant turn: the reader knows what a cup and a promise mean in a hall, and the queen is coming down the hall with the cup. No story character appears.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. Late afternoon in Heorot. Queen Wealhtheow carries the great gold cup high down the long hall, past every bench of Danes, and last of all to the stranger, who rises to meet her. She offers it, but her eyes go past him to her two small sons on the bench: men have sworn great things here before, and her boys have never slept a night in their father's hall. Beowulf takes the cup in both hands and holds it up to the whole hall: tomorrow night they will; tonight he wins or dies in this hall. The dominant turn: he has sworn it in front of everyone, and night is coming. The queen's walk with the cup owns the page; her glance at the boys is the hinge; the oath is the answer. The reader turns into the night.

## PAGE 16

Illustrated prose. Night in Heorot, the hearth dying to red. The king has given his hall to another man for the first time in twelve winters; the Danes are gone and the great doors are shut and barred. The Geats lie asleep on the benches under their cloaks, none of them expecting to see home again. In the foreground Beowulf lies on his back in his tunic alone: no mail, no sword, his huge empty hands on his chest, eyes wide open, watching the doors. The prose tells it over the dark rafters. The dominant turn: he is waiting in the dark with no sword, and Grendel always comes. The waiting man and the barred doors own the page; the sleepers stay quiet shapes. The reader turns because something is coming through those doors.

## PAGE 17

Spectacle. Out of the moonlit moor, through strips of mist, Grendel strides toward the hall on the hill, seen from behind and below: twice a man's height, gaunt, hunched, arms nearly dragging, one coal-red eye catching the light. A hooked claw touches the iron band of the barred door. Then the doors burst inward: Grendel, half in shadow, eyes blazing, in the doorway; the nearest bench is empty, its blanket trailing to the floor where a Geat slept a moment ago; and his claw reaches for the big shape under a cloak on the next bench. The captions say he was too fast. The dominant turn: the next sleeper is Beowulf. Grendel's approach on the moor owns the page; the empty bench says the death without showing it. The reader turns to see who is under that cloak.

## PAGE 18

Spectacle. The claw closes on the sleeper, and the sleeper's hand closes on the claw. Close and huge, an enormous human hand clamps Grendel's scaled wrist; Beowulf, rising off the bench in his tunic, grins and asks if the monster was looking for him, and Grendel's coal eyes go wide. Grendel heaves back toward the door, dragging Beowulf off his feet; Beowulf plants his heels and a bench splinters; for the first time in his life Grendel is afraid. The Geats wake and hack at him, and their swords bounce off his hide in sparks. The dominant turn: no sword can hurt him, and Beowulf is the only thing holding him. The grip owns the page; the Geats' useless swords are a shallow strip. The reader turns to see who lets go first.

## PAGE 19

Spectacle. The hall shakes: a pillar cracks, benches fly, the hearth scatters in sparks. Beowulf, one foot braced on a pillar, both arms locked round Grendel's arm; Grendel wrenching toward the doorway, head thrown back in a howl that fills the top of the page. A moment later, the doorway: Grendel is a hunched shape fleeing down the dark hill, and Beowulf stands in the door breathing hard, holding a long dark scaled arm with hooked claws, longer than he is tall, its shoulder end lost in shadow. The caption says Grendel ran into the dark to die, but left his arm behind. The dominant turn: the monster is beaten, and the Danes don't know it yet. The wrestling and the howl own the page; the arm in the doorway is the proof. The reader turns to the morning.

## PAGE 20

Illustrated prose. Morning. The Danes come running up the hill and crowd in through Heorot's broken doors, pointing up: high over the doors, where everyone can see it, hangs Grendel's claw. Below, riders who followed the tracks across the moor stop at a black lake in the rocks; the water heaves and churns, huge clawed tracks lead down into it, and nothing comes up. The prose tells it and ends with the riders singing about Beowulf all the way home. The dominant turn: the king is about to meet the young man who saved his hall. The claw over the doors and the crowd looking up at it own the page; the black lake is smaller, an uneasy aside the reader will remember. No named character is on this page. The reader turns to meet the king's gratitude.

## PAGE 21

Dramatic. Morning under the hanging claw. Old King Hrothgar holds Beowulf by both shoulders with tears in his eyes: twelve winters he prayed, and God sent him a boy from across the sea; from this day he will love him like a son. Beowulf, grinning, is a head taller. Then the grin goes: the hall is the king's again, but Beowulf wanted all his men home, and behind him the Geats stand round one empty bench with a folded cloak laid on it. Last, Unferth alone under the claw, looking up, cup forgotten in his hand, with nothing to say for once. The dominant turn: tonight there will be a feast in Heorot for the first time in twelve winters. The king's embrace owns the page; the empty bench is the cost; Unferth's silence is the aftershock.

## PAGE 22

Dramatic. The victory feast: Heorot full of light and people, every bench crowded, gifts laid before the high seat for Beowulf, a golden banner, a boar-crested helmet, a mail shirt, a sword, horses through the open doors. The old poet sings with his harp; the king's two small sons stare up at Beowulf; the king's oldest friend raises his cup to him. Then Queen Wealhtheow holds out a heavy twisted gold neck-ring and asks him to be kind to her small sons, since their father is old. Beowulf puts the ring on, lays one huge hand gently on the smaller boy's head, and promises to be their friend as long as he lives. The dominant turn: the hall is singing again, and the king's poet has made a song about Beowulf. The feast owns the page; the ring and the promise are the quiet weight beneath it.

## PAGE 23

Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from an old chronicle: warm ivory vellum, brown ink, watercolour wash, one red-and-lapis interlace initial touched with gold. The dominant image is a museum study of the Sutton Hoo lyre as reconstructed, a small round-shouldered six-stringed wooden lyre with little gilt fittings, and beside it a column of three tiny ink pictures: a whale on a wavy line, a house built of bones, a sword with rays of light. The first field answers who the king's poet was, the scop, singing from memory to a lyre like the one found at Sutton Hoo; the second explains kennings, the riddle-names. The dominant turn: the song of Beowulf is being sung, and the hall is asleep, and the lake is still churning. No story character appears.

## PAGE 24

Spectacle, across two places. Out of the black lake at night rises a second monster: Grendel's mother, larger than her son, a long wolfish jaw, pale green eyes, black-green weed-hair streaming water. The page belongs to her rising. Then Heorot: the Danes asleep under the hanging claw, a vast dark shape in the doorway reaching up to it, the king's oldest friend sitting up and half-drawing his sword. A moment later the bench is empty, a sword lies on the floor, the place above the doors where the claw hung is bare, and the hall is on its feet with torches while the queen holds her two small sons, safe. The dominant turn: there is a second monster, and it has taken the king's friend. The taking happens between the panels; nothing is shown. She never speaks. Beowulf is not here.

## PAGE 25

Dramatic. Dawn in the wrecked hall. Hrothgar sits on the edge of the empty bench with his friend's dropped sword across his knees. Beowulf walks in cheerful and sleepy, innocent, asking whether the night was peaceful. The old king looks up with tears in his beard and tells him everything in three hard sentences: his oldest friend is dead, there were two of them, the tracks go into the black lake, and his sons slept here because Beowulf swore they could. The page belongs to the moment the grin leaves Beowulf's face; he kneels, takes the old man's hands, and says what a hero says: go after the killer, show me the lake. The dominant turn: Beowulf is going to the lake. The empty place above the doors stays in the background, never a focus.

## PAGE 26

Illustrated prose. One great image owns the page: a narrow goat-path along grey crags with a small line of riders on it, the king, the Danes, Beowulf in helmet and mail with his Geats; and below them the lake, black and steaming in a cleft of rock under dripping trees, a waterfall pouring in, water-snakes on the rocks, and at the water's edge a hunted stag standing with hounds behind it, refusing to jump. The prose tells what the lake is and how they came to it. The dominant turn: Beowulf has to go into that water. The riders stay small; the lake and the stag carry the dread.

## PAGE 27

Dramatic. At the black water's edge, Unferth, the man who mocked Beowulf in the hall, steps out of the Danes holding his famous sword Hrunting across both palms and offers it. Beowulf takes it, looks at him a long moment, thanks him, and then turns to Hrothgar with the words of a man who may not come back: send my gold home to my uncle, give my own sword to Unferth. Unferth, with the ghost of his old sneer, almost smiles: come back, I want my sword. The page belongs to the offering of the sword. The dominant turn: Beowulf is going into the water with the sword of the man who mocked him. Hrothgar listens and does not speak.

## PAGE 28

Spectacle. Beowulf leaps from the rocks into the black water with Hrunting in his fist, the Geats on the bank behind him. Then the page's great image: deep green-black water, Beowulf sinking for a long time, his mail glinting, strange tusked water-beasts butting and snapping at his armour from every side while his eyes stay fixed downward. At the bottom, out of the dark, two huge webbed and clawed hands seize him, with pale green eyes and a cloud of weed-hair above them. The dominant turn: she has him, at the bottom of the lake. Nobody speaks. The water carries the page.

## PAGE 29

Spectacle. A cave-hall under the lake, full of air, lit by a strange pale fire, old carved arches, weapons on the walls. Grendel's mother flings Beowulf onto the stone floor. Then the page's great image: Beowulf back on his feet, swinging Hrunting with all his strength at her head, and the patterned blade ringing off her in a shower of sparks, not biting. Last, he hurls Hrunting away across the floor and squares up to her with his bare hands, shouting that he will do it with his hands. The dominant turn: the sword has failed, and she is stronger than her son. She never speaks; no blood anywhere.

## PAGE 30

Spectacle. Grendel's mother is on top of Beowulf on the stone floor, driving a long bone-handled knife at his chest; the point skids on his mail. Over her shoulder he sees it: on the wall, a huge ancient sword longer than a man, glowing in the pale fire. Then the page's great image: Beowulf on his feet, swinging the enormous sword two-handed in a great arc as she rears up to meet it. The blow lands beyond the panel's edge; the reader never sees it strike. The dominant turn: the blow has landed, and we don't yet know what it did. No blood, no wound, no body.

## PAGE 31

Spectacle. Light floods the cave like sunrise. Beowulf stands alone in it holding up the giant's sword, and its long blade is melting away like an icicle in the sun, bright drops falling and vanishing; Hrunting lies on the floor behind him, and of her there is only a dark still mass of weed-hair at the edge of the picture. The light and the melting blade own the page. Then, close: only a heavy golden hilt carved with tiny pictures is left in his hand, and in his other hand he has picked up Hrunting. The dominant turn: he has won, and he is still at the bottom of the lake. No body is shown.

## PAGE 32

Dramatic. Late afternoon on the shore: far up the cliff path, small, the Danes ride away, and only Beowulf's Geats still sit by the black water, staring at it. Then the page's great image: Beowulf bursts up out of the water holding the golden hilt high in one fist and Hrunting in the other, the Geats leaping to their feet, and he asks, grinning, whether they thought he wasn't coming back. They haul him out laughing and pull off his helmet; dripping, he lifts Hrunting and asks to be taken to Hrothgar, because he has a sword to give back. The dominant turn: Unferth is about to get his failed sword back.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic. Evening in Heorot, the hall full of Danes, the old king beaming on his high seat with the golden hilt from under the lake. Beowulf, wearing the queen's gold neck-ring, finds Unferth and holds Hrunting out across both palms, exactly as Unferth once offered it to him at the lake, and thanks him: it's a good sword. Unferth will not take it; ashamed, he says everyone knows it failed. Beowulf says it again, louder, so the whole hall hears, and calls him generous. Unferth takes the sword back and bows his head over it, with nothing to say. The page belongs to the two former rivals and the sword passing between them; the king and the watching Danes stay subordinate. The dominant turn: the rivals are friends, and the old king has something to say to Beowulf alone. The reader turns to hear it.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. Late night, the hall empty except for two men by the hearth. Old Hrothgar turns the golden hilt in the firelight, its carving of giants and great waves, and tells young Beowulf that the giants who made it were strong too, and a flood took every one of them. Then, leaning in close and serious, he warns him not to let pride grow: strength is lent for a little while, and sickness, the sword, fire or old age come for everyone. Beowulf, rubbing the gold ring at his neck, nods politely at the fire and says "Yes, my lord," and the caption tells us he didn't hear a word. The page belongs to the old king's warning and the young man's polite, absent face; the empty hall stays dark around them. The dominant turn: the warning has been given, and it is time to go home.

## PAGE 35

Illustrated prose. Two places, because the turn is the journey home. First, the white-cliffed Danish beach at morning: the old king holds Beowulf in his arms like a son, weeping, his long white beard against the young man's shoulder, while the queen and Unferth stand behind and the Geats load horses and chests of gold onto the ship. Then the Geats' harbour: Beowulf, grinning, hands gold cups and arm-rings out of a chest to the same crowd that once laughed at him. The prose field over the morning sky carries the parting, the gold, and the end of "the slow boy by the fire." The embrace owns the page; the harbour is the payoff beneath it. The dominant turn: the young hero is home and famous, and he has forgotten the warning.

## PAGE 36

Chronicle. The book steps out of the story onto a page of an old chronicle to answer the question the last pages raised: why did Unferth's sword have a name, as if it were a person? Two museum studies in ink and wash sit side by side as one image: a long pattern-welded blade standing upright, its surface rippling like flowing water, and the Sutton Hoo helmet with its winged creature across the face and little boars' heads at the ends of its eyebrows. The fields explain how a smith twisted and hammered iron into such a blade, why it was worth a fortune and passed from father to son, and that helmets were treasures too. The tone is a museum guide's, curious and specific, never a verdict on the story. The dominant turn: the reader knows what a named sword meant, and the story is about to jump fifty years.

## PAGE 37

Illustrated prose. The story jumps a whole lifetime. A sturdy timber hall stands on a rise above the Geats' harbour, smoke curling peacefully from its roof, fields and ships below. On its steps stands Beowulf, old now: still the biggest man in sight but heavy, white hair tied back, a short white beard, the same crooked nose and notched brow, his blue cloak faded, the queen's twisted gold ring at his neck, which he rubs with his thumb as he watches the sea. The prose field over the sky tells how the long winters passed, how he became king of the Geats and kept them safe, and that he still wears Wealhtheow's ring. The dominant turn: those peaceful years are about to end. Calm owns the page; the reader feels the quiet before something breaks it.

## PAGE 38

Spectacle. At night on a high headland by the sea, a ragged young runaway, hiding from a beating, scrambles into an ancient grave mound, looking back in terror. Inside, the page opens onto its real subject: a mountain of gold in a stone chamber, and coiled over it, asleep, an enormous dragon, bronze-and-black scales seamed with faint ember light, its horned head resting on the treasure. At the edge of the gold, tiny beside it, the runaway lifts one golden cup. Two captions carry the theft. The dominant turn: the dragon is going to wake up. The sleeping dragon owns the page; the boy stays small, and the single cup in his hands is the act that will cost a kingdom.

## PAGE 39

Spectacle. In the dark of the barrow one huge molten-gold eye opens; then the dragon's head hangs low over the hoard, sniffing at the small gap where the cup used to be. The caption says it knew every cup and knew one was gone. Then the page breaks open into the night sky over the Geats' coast: the dragon in flight, wings spread, pouring a river of fire onto the villages, and on the rise the old king's great hall going up in flames. The dominant turn: the old king's own hall has burned. The burning sky owns the page; the eye and the sniffing are the small fuse that leads to it. Nobody is seen dying; the cost is the hall.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic. Dawn over the burned black timbers of Beowulf's hall, smoke still rising. Old Beowulf, who fought the fire all night, stands huge and still in the ashes. Wiglaf, a slight curly-haired boy with soot on his face, a too-big mail shirt and a yellow wooden shield, runs up with the news: half the coast burned, and a runaway who stole a cup knows where the dragon sleeps. Beowulf looks at the boy properly for the first time, names him as his cousin's son, and orders a shield all of iron and the runaway found. Wiglaf offers to gather the army; Beowulf refuses. The dominant turn: the old king means to fight the dragon alone. The ruin and the old man's stillness own the page; the boy's eagerness runs into a wall.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Night in a stone forge shed lit orange. A smith hammers a great round shield of dark iron on the anvil while old Beowulf and Wiglaf sit on a bench watching the sparks. Wiglaf gathers his courage and asks whether Beowulf was ever afraid. "Every time." In the forge light the old face remembers: a boy everyone laughed at, a boast in a king's hall that everyone heard, and after that no running away. Wiglaf leans in and asks to come tomorrow, as his father promised their family would; Beowulf, with a ghost of his old grin, says he may come and watch, but the dragon is his. The dominant turn: tomorrow the old king walks out to the dragon, and Wiglaf will be watching. The confession owns the page; the forging stays behind it.

## PAGE 42

Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from a chronicle: warm ivory vellum, brown ink, watercolour wash, and one red-and-lapis interlace initial touched with gold. The dominant image is a museum-style cutaway of a grassy grave mound on a headland, opened to show a stone chamber heaped with gold, cups, a helmet, a shield boss and rings, with a small serpent-dragon curled around it, drawn in thin ink like a figure in an old manuscript. The first field asks why a dragon sleeps on gold and answers with the old northern stories of dragons guarding treasure in the graves of the long dead, and the heroes who killed them. The second field says people really did bury treasure with their kings, and tells of two real finds in England. The dominant turn: the reader knows what the old king is walking toward, a hoard in a grave and the thing that guards it. No story character appears.

## PAGE 43

Illustrated prose. The shaking runaway has led old Beowulf, his ten armed men and young Wiglaf to the high grassy headland, where steam rises from the stream that runs out of the barrow's stone door. The old king sits alone on a rock in his mail, the iron shield against his knee and the boar helmet beside him, looking at the grey sea for a long time. The prose carries what he is thinking: the boy by the fire, Grendel's claw, the black lake, and old Hrothgar by the hearth in Heorot naming sickness, the sword, fire and old age. The dominant turn: the old king has remembered the warning, and he is about to say it out loud. The old man and the sea own the page; the waiting men, the boy with the yellow shield and the pointing runaway stay small behind him. The memories live only in the words; none is drawn.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. On the headland, old Beowulf gets slowly to his feet and puts on his boar helmet, telling Wiglaf that an old king once warned him fire, the sword or old age would come for him, and now it is all three at once. He turns to his ten men and orders them to wait on the hill; Wiglaf steps forward and argues that the king would send an army to help anyone else. Beowulf's answer comes without looking back: fifty years of songs say he never needed anyone, and he is too old to start. The dominant turn: the old king is walking alone toward the dragon. The last image, one small armed figure going down the slope toward the steaming stone door, owns the page; the argument above it is the reason he goes alone.

## PAGE 45

Spectacle. Old Beowulf stands at the barrow's stone door, helmet on, head thrown back, and roars his challenge into the dark: come out and fight the king of the Geats. Then the dragon answers. It bursts out of the barrow in a river of fire, ragged wings spreading, ember seams blazing along its bronze-and-black scales, and the old king crouches behind his iron shield as the flames split around it on both sides. The dominant turn: the fire is on him. The dragon and its fire own the page; the challenge above is the small human voice that called it out. The dragon makes no sound in words.

## PAGE 46

Spectacle. Old Beowulf strikes his old sword Naegling against the dragon's neck; sparks fly, the blade scores one scale and stops, and the caption says it bit, but not deep enough. Then the fire comes again and wraps all the way around him: the iron shield glows red, and the old king is a dark shape standing inside the flame, still on his feet. The dominant turn: the old king is burning, and his men are watching. The fire around the standing figure owns the page; the failed stroke above explains why the fight has turned against him. No wound is drawn; the danger is the flame.

## PAGE 47

Dramatic. Up on the slope, the ten men break and run for the woods, dropping their spears. Wiglaf alone turns on them and shouts after them: they promised, in the king's hall, when he gave them their helmets and swords, to stand by him if he ever needed them, and he needs them now. Their backs disappear into the trees, and not one of them turns around. So the boy lifts his yellow shield and runs down the slope into the smoke, toward the fire, by himself. The dominant turn: the boy is running into the fire. The deserting slope with one boy turned against it owns the page; the choice to go alone is the last, smallest beat.

## PAGE 48

Dramatic. A blast of dragon fire sets Wiglaf's yellow wooden shield alight and he flings it away. Old Beowulf hauls him in behind the iron shield, furious and astonished, shouting that he told him to stay on the hill. Wiglaf, shaking, answers with the old king's own confession: he was afraid every time, and so is Wiglaf, and he came anyway. Then Beowulf swings Naegling with all his strength into the dragon's head, and the old blade breaks in pieces; the caption says his arm was too strong for any sword. The dominant turn: the old king has no sword left. The shattering sword owns the page; the shared shelter behind the iron shield is the human heart of it.

## PAGE 49

Spectacle. The dragon's jaws close on the old king at the neck and shoulder, seen only as the jaws and his helmet in silhouette against the fire. Then Wiglaf, shieldless, darts in low under the flames and drives his short sword up into the dragon's belly between the ember seams, and the fire in its throat goes out. Last, Beowulf draws the long knife from his belt and swings; the blow lands beyond the panel's edge and the great head falls away. Three captions carry it. The dominant turn: the dragon is dead, and the old king has been bitten. The boy's stroke under the fire owns the page. No blood, no wound and no severed part is ever seen.

## PAGE 50

Dramatic. Beside the barrow, old Beowulf sits against the stones with his helmet off, his white hair loose and his face grey; Wiglaf kneels beside him bathing his face with water from the stream, and the dragon lies behind them, a dark dead coil. Beowulf says the teeth were poisoned, sickness too, so old Hrothgar left nothing out; Wiglaf begs him not to talk like that. Then the old king grips the boy's arm and looks at him properly: fifty years he never needed anyone, today he did, and Wiglaf came. He sends the boy into the barrow for the treasure he won for his people. The dominant turn: the boy has to go into the dragon's grave. The dying king and the kneeling boy own the page.

## PAGE 51

Spectacle. Wiglaf goes alone into the dragon's grave. Inside the barrow's stone chamber he stands small, knee-deep in a sea of gold — cups, dishes, rings, old helmets eaten by rust — gathering an armful, and high above the hoard hangs a banner woven all of gold that glows in the dark by its own light, lighting his astonished face. One caption tells it. The dominant turn: he has to carry it out to a dying man. The glow and the boy's face own the page; the gold is a field, not an inventory. No dragon here, nobody else, no voice: the silence is the point. The reader turns because the treasure is only worth something if the old king lives to see it.

## PAGE 52

Dramatic. Beside the barrow, Wiglaf kneels with gold heaped at old Beowulf's feet, and the old king looks at it and smiles: it is for their people. Then he takes off the twisted gold ring and presses it into Wiglaf's hand, the boar helmet between them, and names the boy the last of their family, asking for a mound high on the headland that sailors will call Beowulf's Barrow. Last, the quiet panel that owns the page: the old king's eyes closed, his head back against the stones, the sea wind in his white hair, Wiglaf holding his hand; one caption says he died. The death is closed eyes and a held hand, nothing more. The dominant turn: the men who ran away are coming back. The reader turns to see what Wiglaf will say to them.

## PAGE 53

Dramatic. Evening by the barrow. The ten men who ran creep out of the trees, shields lowered, heads down. Wiglaf, the gold ring now at his neck, stands to meet them; behind him old Beowulf lies covered to the chest by his faded blue cloak, his face peaceful. Wiglaf speaks alone: they come back now, after running; they keep their lives but lose their names. Then he lays the old king's broken sword on the heaped gold by the barrow door, touches the ring at his neck, and says none of them will wear the gold, the old king paid for it with his life. The dominant turn: the Geats have to say goodbye to their king. Wiglaf owns every line; the ten men stay ashamed and silent. The reader turns to the farewell.

## PAGE 54

Spectacle. Night on the headland. A great funeral fire hung with helmets and shields throws flames and smoke up into a sky full of stars. The Geats stand in a ring around it, small and dark against the blaze. Closest to the fire, one woman with her hair bound up sings with her face lifted, and Wiglaf stands near her wearing the gold ring. One caption carries her song's question: who will keep us safe now? The dominant turn: the king is gone, and his people have to remember him. The fire and the singing woman own the page; no body is seen, only the fire. The reader turns to learn how he will be remembered.

## PAGE 55

Illustrated prose. Ten days later a tall grassy mound stands newly raised on the highest point of the headland over the sea. Twelve riders circle it, cloaks streaming, singing, and Wiglaf rides at their head in the boar-crested helmet with the gold ring at his neck. The prose field tells why the Geats would not keep the gold he paid for with his life, how they buried his ashes and the dragon's gold inside the mound where it lies still, and what the riders sang: the gentlest of kings, the kindest to his people, the most eager for fame. The dominant turn: the king is buried, and his name is all that is left. The mound and the riders own the image; the prose carries the meaning. The reader turns to see what that name became.

## PAGE 56

Spectacle. Years later, a longship under a striped sail rides far out on a gold evening sea. A sailor at the prow points up at a high cliff where a green mound stands against the sunset, and the others turn to look. One caption says that ever since, sailors far out on the whale-road have looked up at that cliff and named it: Beowulf's Barrow. The dominant turn: the story is still being told, and the reader wants to know how it reached them. The mound on the cliff and the pointing arm own the page; the sailors are small and nameless, and nobody from the story is aboard. The reader turns to find out how the tale crossed a thousand years.

## PAGE 57

Chronicle. The book steps out of the story one last time onto a page of an old chronicle. The one dominant image is a museum study of an open page of the Beowulf manuscript, dense dark old handwriting, its edges browned, scorched and crumbled. Three fields answer the question of how we still know this story: every word comes from one single copy written by two scribes about a thousand years ago; in 1731 the library holding it caught fire, and the scorched book survives in the British Library; and J. R. R. Tolkien, who loved the poem, put a thief stealing a golden cup from a sleeping dragon into The Hobbit. One label names the manuscript. The dominant turn: the book is over, and the reader knows the story came to them through fire too.
