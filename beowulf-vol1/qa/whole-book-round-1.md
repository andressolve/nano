# Beowulf Vol I — whole-book release gate (canonical pages 1–57)

## 1. Blind account (written before opening the script or the contract)

### Retell
In the Danish fens, a creature that hates music listens to King Hrothgar's new hall, Heorot. At the feast, Queen Wealhtheow gives the first cup to the king. Unferth, the king's sly spokesman, has a speech ready, the poet sings, and red eyes watch from the doorway (pp1–2). That night Grendel bursts the iron-barred doors (p3), and by morning thirty men are gone. The king's oldest friend, an old bald warrior with a braided beard, stands at his shoulder. Unferth promises to fill the hall with swords. The queen asks what happens if swords can't stop it (p4). They can't: for twelve winters the hall stands empty at night, Hrothgar waits alone, and sailors carry the story across the sea (p5).

Among the Geats, Beowulf hears the story. He was the boy everyone laughed at. Hrothgar once paid his father's debt, so Beowulf says "now it's my turn," and fourteen men go with him (p6). A map page places the Danes, the Geats and Lejre (p7). The crossing is joyful (p8). A coast guard challenges them and then points the way to the gold-roofed hall (p9). Hrothgar welcomes the son of his old friend, and Beowulf boasts that he will fight bare-handed. Unferth sneers (p10), mocks him about losing a swimming race (p11), and the true story follows: seven nights at sea and nine sea-monsters killed (p12). Beowulf turns the tables, and the hall roars (p13). A hall cutaway explains benches, rings and promises (p14).

Wealhtheow brings the cup and says what she wants: her sons have never slept in their father's hall. Beowulf swears to win or die (p15). He lies down unarmed with his eyes open (p16). Grendel takes one Geat and reaches for the next sleeper (p17). That sleeper is Beowulf: "Looking for me?" Grendel is afraid, and swords bounce off him (p18). He tears free and runs off to die, leaving his arm (p19). The claw is hung over the doors, and his tracks lead into a churning black lake (p20). Hrothgar says he will love Beowulf like a son. Beowulf grieves for the Geat he lost, and Unferth has nothing to say (p21). At the feast there are gifts, and the queen gives Beowulf a neck-ring and asks him to be kind to her small sons. He promises, the Danes sleep in Heorot again, and Beowulf goes to a guest house (p22). A page on the scop, the lyre and kennings follows (p23).

Then Grendel's mother comes, takes back the arm and carries off the king's oldest friend, while Beowulf sleeps elsewhere (p24). Hrothgar weeps and blames Beowulf's oath: "she came anyway." Beowulf answers, "Show me the lake" (p25). They ride to the mere, where even a stag won't go in (p26). Unferth lends him Hrunting. Beowulf leaves his gold to his uncle and his own sword to Unferth. "Come back, Geat. I want my sword" (p27). Beowulf dives and she seizes him (p28). Hrunting won't bite, so he fights with his hands (p29). Her knife fails against his mail, and he sees a giant's sword on the wall and swings it (p30). She falls, the blade melts, and only the gold hilt is left (p31). The Danes give up and leave, but the thirteen Geats wait. He comes up out of the water: "I've got a sword to give back" (p32). In the hall he praises Hrunting in public, and Unferth is silenced a second time (p33). By the fire, Hrothgar warns him about pride: sickness, the sword, fire or old age will come for him. Beowulf is nineteen, says "yes, my lord," and doesn't listen (p34). They part in tears, and at home Beowulf gives the gold away and nobody calls him slow again (p35). A page on named swords and the Sutton Hoo helmet follows (p36).

Fifty winters later Beowulf is an old king, still wearing the queen's ring (p37). A runaway steals one cup from a sleeping dragon (p38). The dragon burns the coast, and the king's own hall burns first (p39). Wiglaf, his cousin's boy, brings the news. Beowulf orders an iron shield, refuses the army and says he will kill it himself (p40). At the forge, Wiglaf asks whether he was ever afraid. "Every time." He boasted so that he couldn't run. Wiglaf asks to come, and Beowulf allows Wiglaf and ten men to come and watch (p41). A page on barrows, Sigemund and the hoards follows (p42). At the barrow, Beowulf remembers Hrothgar's warning (p43): "It's all three, all at once." He sends the men back because he "never needed anyone" (p44). He challenges the dragon and fire breaks over his shield (p45). Naegling bites but not deep enough, and the fire comes again (p46). The ten run away. Wiglaf shames them and goes in alone (p47). His wooden shield burns. "So am I. I came anyway." Naegling shatters (p48). The dragon bites the king's neck, Wiglaf stabs its belly, and Beowulf finishes it with his knife (p49). The bite was poisoned, which completes Hrothgar's list. "Today I did [need someone], and you came." He sends Wiglaf for the treasure (p50). Wiglaf finds a glowing gold banner (p51). Beowulf gives Wiglaf his ring, helmet and mail, asks for a barrow sailors will see, and dies (p52). The ten come back. Wiglaf tells them they have lost their names, keeps only the ring he was given, and says nobody should keep the rest (p53). There is a funeral fire, and a woman sings "who will keep us safe now?" (p54). The gold is buried with the king and twelve riders sing (p55). Sailors still point to Beowulf's Barrow (p56). Afterword: the single manuscript survived a fire, and Tolkien's cup-thief is in The Hobbit (p57).

### Causal chain
1. Hall and music → Grendel's hatred → raids.
2. Swords fail → twelve empty winters → sailors' story.
3. Father's debt → Beowulf sails.
4. Boast and flyting → public oath to the queen.
5. Grip → arm torn off → hall restored, but one Geat lost.
6. Hall reoccupied, Beowulf sleeps elsewhere → mother's revenge, and the oath is broken in Hrothgar's eyes.
7. Unferth's sword lent → it fails → giant's sword → victory → generosity to Unferth.
8. Hrothgar's warning is ignored.
9. Fifty years → stolen cup → dragon burns the king's hall.
10. Pride ("never needed anyone") → goes alone → men flee.
11. Wiglaf keeps the family promise → together they kill the dragon.
12. Poison → death, bequest to Wiglaf.
13. Wiglaf's judgment → gold buried, cowards nameless → barrow → the story survives.

### Ending: what it cost and why the final image matters
It cost the king his life, and with him the people's protection ("who will keep us safe now?"). It also cost all the gold he died for, because his people bury it rather than keep what he paid for with his life. The ten men lose their names. The last image, sailors on the whale-road pointing at the barrow, is the one thing he asked for on p52 being granted. Fame is what is left of him. That links back to the boy everyone laughed at and to the "eager for fame" line on p55, and the manuscript page then shows the same story surviving its own fire.

## 5. Nonblocking observations
- **p6, panel 2:** the balloon reads "…saved my father's life. he paid a debt…" with a lowercase "he" after the full stop. The script has "He". This is a typo and suits a `pagerun.py --edit` fix rather than a redraw.
- **Time of day, pp43–53:** p43 is daylight, pp44–46 are moonlit night, pp50–52 are overcast day and p53 is sunset. The reader follows the fight without trouble, so this is cosmetic.
- **pp46 and 48:** the backgrounds show silhouettes of ruined stone towers that look like castles, which don't belong on a Geatish headland. They are background only.
- **p17, panel 3:** the sleeper the claw reaches for is under a blue blanket, so he reads as Beowulf. That is the intended setup for "Looking for me?" on p18.
- **p13, panel 3:** the script says Hrothgar laughs out loud. On the page he is small and seated, and the laugh doesn't read. The cheering hall carries the beat anyway.
- **p22 and p37:** the "ring" is a neck-ring, not a finger ring. The pictures are consistent across pp22, 34, 37, 52 and 53, so the word "ring" never misleads.
- **p7:** the map's coastline is stylized and the "Danes" label sits on the peninsula. It is enough to understand where things are.
