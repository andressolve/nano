# Sequence gate — pages 51–57

## Uninterrupted visible sequence

Wiglaf enters the barrow, finds the dragon's immense hoard and a gold-woven banner shining by its own light, and gathers treasure for the dying king. Outside, Beowulf says the gold is for his people, gives Wiglaf his ring, helmet, and mail, charges him to keep their family name alive, orders a sea-visible burial mound, and dies with Wiglaf holding his hand. The warriors who fled return at evening; Wiglaf condemns their desertion and loss of honor, distinguishes Beowulf's personally gifted ring from the blood-bought hoard, and refuses the rest of the treasure for them or himself. The Geats burn Beowulf on the headland while a woman mourns the danger now facing his people. Over ten days they build the requested barrow, seal the king's ashes and the dragon's gold inside it, and send twelve riders around it to sing his character and fame. Years later, sailors still recognize the mound from the whale-road as Beowulf's Barrow. A chronicle coda then explains how the poem survived in one fire-damaged manuscript and how its stolen-cup episode echoed in Tolkien's *The Hobbit*.

## Gate judgment

The batch forms a clear causal and emotional denouement: discovery leads to Beowulf's final disposition of treasure and regalia; his death leads to Wiglaf's judgment of the deserters; that judgment leads to the communal refusal and burial of the hoard; cremation, mound-building, praise, and the sailors' later recognition give the death progressively wider aftermath. The final chronicle page is a legible change of mode, and page 56's claim that the story endures motivates its question of transmission.

All scripted text is present with the intended wording and attribution. Wiglaf and old Beowulf remain legible across transitions. The gifted ring, boar-crested helmet, broken sword, rejected hoard, headland barrow, and coastal geography retain their consequential meanings from page to page. The sequence stays with Wiglaf through the immediate aftermath, then widens coherently to the Geats, later sailors, and the surviving book; there is no focal break that confuses the story.

Pacing varies effectively across spectacle, two dramatic pages, funeral spectacle, illustrated prose, maritime spectacle, and chronicle. Page 55 compresses ten days of building and the riders' lament, but the compression reads as intentional elegiac passage rather than a summary wall: the preceding pyre supplies the immediate communal grief, while the mound and circling riders give the burial and remembrance their own visible aftermath. No consequential beat is stranded, and no page turn creates a material story, continuity, or attribution failure.
