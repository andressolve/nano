## Uninterrupted blind read

Wiglaf asks old Beowulf whether he was ever afraid. Beowulf answers that he
was afraid every time, but that his youthful boast made retreat impossible.
Wiglaf invokes his family's loyalty and asks to accompany him; Beowulf allows
him and ten men to watch, while reserving the dragon for himself. A Chronicle
pause explains why dragons guard grave-gold and connects the barrow to northern
story and real burial treasure.

The runaway then leads the company to the coastal barrow. Beowulf sits above
it, remembers the dangers and losses of his life, and recognizes that fire,
the sword, and old age have arrived together. He refuses Wiglaf's offer of
help, orders everyone to remain on the hill, and approaches alone. The dragon
answers his challenge with fire. Naegling wounds it only shallowly, and a
second blast engulfs Beowulf.

The ten retainers flee. Wiglaf calls after them, reminds them of their promise,
and, when none returns, runs into the fight alone. His wooden shield burns;
Beowulf pulls him behind the iron shield and rebukes him. Wiglaf answers
Beowulf's lesson about courage: he is afraid and came anyway. Beowulf's next
stroke shatters Naegling. The dragon bites Beowulf in the neck, Wiglaf strikes
its belly beneath the fire, and Beowulf kills it with his knife. The poisoned
bite is fatal. Beowulf thanks Wiglaf for coming when he was finally needed and
sends him into the barrow to bring out the treasure so he can see what he has
won for his people before he dies.

## Comparison with the batch script

- The material story and every causal transition match the script. The
  Chronicle interruption supplies the barrow-and-hoard context before the
  expedition reaches the grave, and the return to the headland is immediate
  and clear.
- Text and attribution remain clear across page turns. The fear exchange on
  page 41 pays off directly in Wiglaf's answer on page 48; Beowulf's order to
  stay on page 44 produces both the retainers' flight on page 47 and his rebuke
  of Wiglaf on page 48. The sword failure, bite, paired killing blows, poison,
  and final request occur in the scripted order.
- Old Beowulf and Wiglaf remain unmistakable throughout. Beowulf's blue cloak,
  white hair, boar-crested helmet, iron shield, and Naegling are continuous;
  Wiglaf's youth, curls, and yellow shield are continuous until the shield
  burns and is discarded as scripted. Naegling's failure and break are legible,
  and Beowulf's finishing knife is distinct from the lost sword.
- The coastal headland, steaming stone barrow, grey sea, and open grassy fight
  ground remain one consequential setting. Movement between the waiting hill
  and barrow door is spatially intelligible.
- Each page preserves its focal turn and makes the next page necessary: fear,
  grave-gold, reckoning, solitary choice, first fire, failed strike, desertion,
  Wiglaf's return and the broken sword, the kill and bite, then the fatal
  aftermath and treasure request.
- Pacing is sustained rather than compressed. The Chronicle and illustrated
  prose pages create breadth before the fight; spectacle pages carry the
  dragon's force; dramatic pages carry the choices and emotional consequences.
  The run is not a wall of dramatic scenes, and no consequential beat lacks an
  aftermath within the batch.
