# Sequence gate — canonical pages 31–40

## Uninterrupted read

The victory beneath the lake resolves in light: Grendel's mother falls, the giant blade melts, and Beowulf is left with its golden hilt while recovering Hrunting. The Danes abandon their watch, but the thirteen Geats remain and are rewarded when Beowulf surfaces with both objects. Their relief becomes laughter, and Beowulf immediately turns toward unfinished human business: returning Unferth's sword.

In Heorot, Beowulf gives Hrunting back without humiliating its owner. Unferth calls the sword a failure; Beowulf publicly calls it good and Unferth generous, allowing the former rival to accept it in humbled silence. Hrothgar then studies the ancient hilt and uses the vanished giants to warn Beowulf that strength, pride, and life all end. Nineteen-year-old Beowulf answers respectfully but does not absorb the warning. At the shore, Hrothgar embraces him like a son, already grieving their final separation. Beowulf sails home with treasure and horses, gives nearly all the treasure away, and replaces his old reputation with fame and affection.

A chronicle page pauses the plot to explain why exceptional swords were named and inherited, how pattern-welded blades were made, and how the Sutton Hoo helmet relates to the poem's boar-helmets. Fifty winters then pass. Beowulf outlives his uncle and cousin, becomes a long-peaceful king, ages into a white-haired man, and still wears Wealhtheow's ring.

That peace ends when a runaway hiding from a beating enters a grave mound, finds a dragon sleeping on its hoard, steals one cup, and flees. The dragon detects the exact loss, wakes, and burns the Geats' coast, beginning with Beowulf's hall. After fighting the fire through the night, old Beowulf stands in its ashes. Wiglaf brings the cause and the location of the barrow. Beowulf recognizes his young kinsman, orders an iron shield and the runaway found, refuses Wiglaf's offer to gather the army, and chooses to face the dragon alone, revealing that Hrothgar's warning still has not taken hold.

## Gate judgment

The pages match the batch script in material story, exact visible text, speaker attribution, and named identity. Hrunting passes cleanly from the cave to the shore, from Beowulf to Unferth, while the golden hilt passes from Beowulf to Hrothgar and motivates the warning. Wealhtheow's ring remains legible as the bridge from young Beowulf to the old king. The coast, hall, barrow, stolen cup, dragon, burned royal hall, and iron-versus-wood shield decision remain causally continuous across turns. Young and old Beowulf are distinct by age but retain a coherent focal identity; Unferth, Hrothgar, the unnamed runaway, the dragon, and Wiglaf enter and act without identity confusion.

The batch does not read as compressed summary. Page 37's deliberate fifty-year compression is framed by the chronicle pause and followed by scene-level cause and consequence. Page modes vary effectively among spectacle, dramatic exchange, illustrated prose, and chronicle. The sustained dramatic material on pages 32–34 changes emotional function from relief to reconciliation to warning, so it does not form a repetitive wall. The farewell gives the Danish movement its aftermath, and page 40 gives the dragon's burning an immediate personal and strategic aftermath. Every page turn advances or reframes a live consequence; none breaks continuity or withholds necessary information.
