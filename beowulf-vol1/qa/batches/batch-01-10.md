# Sequence gate — pages 1–10

## Blind sequence record

The title page establishes Heorot as a bright hall across the marsh from a creature that hates its music. Inside, Hrothgar dedicates the hall to his people, Wealhtheow ceremonially returns the first cup to him, Unferth answers with a barbed toast, and a poet celebrates the hall while something outside listens. After the feast, Grendel bursts through the barred doors while the sleeping warriors are defenseless. Morning reveals that thirty men are gone. Hrothgar grieves; Unferth promises to fill the hall with swords; Wealhtheow questions whether swords can stop the attacks; Hrothgar recognizes that the hall he built has become a trap.

Unferth's defense fails. Grendel keeps killing until Heorot is abandoned at night for twelve winters, Hrothgar ages alone in the empty hall, and sailors carry the story abroad. In Geatland, Beowulf—once mocked as a slow, sleepy boy—hears it. Because Hrothgar once saved his father's life and accepted no repayment, Beowulf takes the debt as his own and calls for companions; fourteen volunteer. A map-and-history interlude locates the Danes and Geats across the sea from one another and ties the old story to Lejre and to the poem's later life in England.

Beowulf and his Geats sail eagerly to Denmark, with Beowulf visibly happiest at the prow, and sight the white cliffs on the second day. A mounted Danish guard challenges the armed strangers. Beowulf identifies himself and his purpose; the guard accepts the answer and points them toward the golden hall. In Heorot, Hrothgar recognizes Beowulf through his father and explains the old debt. Beowulf asks for one night in the hall and chooses to fight Grendel without a sword because Grendel carries none. Hrothgar receives him warmly; Unferth watches that warmth with open resentment.

## Script comparison and sequence judgment

The rendered text is exact and correctly attributed throughout. The page turns preserve the intended causality: the feast's music draws Grendel; the intrusion yields the morning loss; Unferth's armed answer fails into twelve winters of abandonment; the sailors' story reaches Beowulf; Hrothgar's aid to Beowulf's father motivates the expedition; the geographic interlude clarifies the crossing; the voyage reaches the Danish guard; and the guard's direction leads into Beowulf's audience with Hrothgar. The last page cleanly carries both forward pressures: Beowulf's unarmed boast and Unferth's resentment.

Named identities remain clear across transitions. Hrothgar, Wealhtheow, Unferth, and Beowulf retain distinct, recognizable appearances, relationships, and positions in the story. Consequential objects and settings also remain coherent: Unferth's sword vow is answered by its failure and later contrasted with Beowulf's empty hands; Beowulf's blue cloak, helmet, ship, companions, the Danish cliffs, the stone road, and Heorot all carry cleanly through the journey. Heorot's shift from ruined morning to twelve years of cold abandonment and then to the occupied daytime audience reads as elapsed history, not a contradiction.

Focal integrity is strong. Each page advances one dominant turn, and the batch varies spectacle, dramatic scenes, illustrated prose, and chronicle rather than becoming a wall of one mode. The twelve-winter passage is purposeful compression with visible emotional aftermath in the aged, isolated Hrothgar. The map interlude briefly widens context between decision and voyage without draining urgency. Major beats receive their needed aftermath: the first attack has the morning reckoning, the Danes' failed defense has prolonged abandonment, and Beowulf's decision becomes departure, arrival, admission, and audience.

No material sequence, transcription, identity, continuity, focal, pacing, or page-turn failure warrants a complete-page redraw.
