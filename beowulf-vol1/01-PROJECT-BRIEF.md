# Beowulf: The Hall, the Mere, and the Dragon — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, ~56 pages: 50
story pages and 6 Chronicle pages (Mode 4).
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** *Beowulf*, the Old English poem (one manuscript, British Library
Cotton Vitellius A.xv, copied about 1000 AD), read through the public-domain
translations of Francis B. Gummere (1910) and John R. Clark Hall (1901). For
the Chronicle pages: the Lejre excavations, the Sutton Hoo ship burial (1939),
the Staffordshire Hoard (2009), the Benty Grange helmet, the *Völsunga saga*
for Sigurd and Fafnir, and the manuscript's own history (the Cotton fire of
1731, Thorkelin's transcripts, the first printed edition of 1815). Seamus
Heaney's translation, John Gardner's *Grendel*, and every film are **not**
used: still under copyright, and their inventions stay out.

---

## The engine

A young man his own people laughed at as good-for-nothing crosses the sea to
pay his father's debt by fighting, bare-handed, a monster no army could stop —
and fifty years later, an old king walking out alone to meet a dragon, finds
that a hero's name is kept alive only by the one who stays beside him.

**If Beowulf reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a
stretch, the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.

## The spine

As a boy in the land of the Geats, Beowulf was the one everybody laughed at:
big, slow, asleep by the fire. Now he is nineteen and wants one thing above
everything — for the world to know his name. Across the sea, old King Hrothgar
of the Danes, who once paid off a blood-debt to save Beowulf's father, has
built the greatest hall in the world, and for twelve years a monster called
Grendel has come out of the fen every night to carry men out of it. Beowulf
sails with fourteen companions, boasts that he will fight Grendel without a
sword, is mocked by the king's jealous spokesman Unferth, and wins: he tears
off the monster's arm with his bare hands. Then Grendel's mother comes for
revenge, and Beowulf dives into a black lake, where Unferth's borrowed sword
fails him and an ancient giant's sword saves him. Hrothgar, giving him
everything, warns him that strength does not last: one day fire, or the sword,
or old age will come for him.

Fifty years later all three come at once. Beowulf is the old king of the
Geats when a runaway steals one golden cup from a dragon's hoard and the
dragon burns the land, Beowulf's own hall with it. Too proud to let anyone
share the danger, he walks out to fight it alone with eleven men behind him —
and when the fire comes, ten of them run. Only Wiglaf, the youngest of his
family, comes into the flames. Together they kill the dragon, and Beowulf
dies, giving Wiglaf his helmet, his collar and his name to carry.

**The fork the last story page lands on:** the hero is dead and his gold is
buried with him, as useless as it ever was; but a young man who once was
nobody is standing where he stood, and sailors far out at sea are steering by
a mound on a cliff called Beowulf's Barrow.

## What this volume is

- Three monsters, three fights, each one different: a wrestling match in the
  dark, a fight at the bottom of a lake, a fight in fire.
- A young hero who is loud, funny, hungry for glory and brave enough to back
  every boast, and an old hero who learns what all that glory was for.
- A rivalry that turns to respect: Unferth, who mocks him, and lends him his
  own sword.
- A warning given in the middle of the book and paid off at the end.
- A last fight won by two people, because one man stayed.
- Six Chronicle pages that open the real world around the poem: the Danes and
  the Geats, the mead-hall, the singer and his harp, named swords and the
  Sutton Hoo helmet, dragons and buried treasure, and the one manuscript that
  nearly burned.

## What this volume is not

- Not a brawler: every fight turns somebody.
- Not two books stapled together: the dragon is the climax the warning
  promised, and it gets the room the Grendel fight gets.
- Not the poem's feuds: no Finnsburh, no Swedish wars, no Hama, no Thryth,
  no Heathobards.
- Not a history book with a story attached. The story pages carry no history.

## Cast ceiling — 8. Hard.

1. **Beowulf** — 19 in Movements I–V; about 70 in VI–VII. One character, two
   locked states.
2. **Hrothgar** — the old king of the Danes, who built Heorot; generous,
   grieving, wise too late for himself.
3. **Wealhtheow** — Hrothgar's queen, who carries the cup and fears for her
   small sons.
4. **Unferth** — the king's spokesman, clever and jealous; mocks Beowulf, and
   later lends him his sword.
5. **Grendel** — the thing from the fen. Never speaks.
6. **Grendel's mother** — older, stronger, grieving, in her hall under the
   lake. Never speaks.
7. **Wiglaf** — about 17, Beowulf's young kinsman, the one who stays.
8. **The dragon** — the guardian of the hoard. Never speaks.

**Collapses and cuts.** Hygelac, Beowulf's uncle and king → "his uncle, the
king", unnamed, seen once in the background, and told in narration across
the jump. Wulfgar the herald and the coast-guard → unnamed, no balloons; the
coast-guard's challenge becomes a silent spear lowered and a pointed hand.
Aeschere → "Hrothgar's oldest friend", unnamed, seen at Hrothgar's shoulder
before he is taken. Breca → "the other boy", unnamed, small, in one prose
page. Hrothgar's sons → two small boys at the feast, unnamed. Hrethel,
Ecgtheow, Hrothulf, Freawaru, Hygd, Weohstan, Ongentheow, Heardred, Eadgils
and every other name the poem lists → cut. The thief → unnamed, no balloon.

## The audience promise

These readers have finished *Three Kingdoms*, *The Three Musketeers*,
*Monkey King* and *King Arthur* in this form and love oaths, a hero who is
underestimated, rivals who become friends, and real fights with real stakes.
They know dragons, and they may know that Tolkien's dragon came out of this
poem. The promise: **you'll meet the boy everyone laughed at, watch him fight
three monsters with his bare hands, a giant's sword and his last breath — and
find out what the world was like when people first sang about him.**

The central dramatic question: *who will stand with you when the fire comes?*

Complexity budget: eight names, three monsters kept visibly unlike each
other, one time jump (fifty years, at the centre of the book, across a
Chronicle page and a prose page), two lands (Denmark and the Geats' coast),
four swords kept visibly different (Beowulf's own, Hrunting, the giant's
sword, Naegling). Assumed knowledge: none.

Refuses to become: a muscle-man cartoon; a gore show; a lecture.

## Length

56 pages: seven movements of story (50 pages) and six Chronicle pages at the
breaks. Reached by cutting the poem's digressions, its genealogies, the
Finnsburh song, the Swedish wars, Hygelac's court and the homecoming report
— not by compressing.

## Series position

Standalone. The poem is complete in one volume.

## The reader

A first-time reader who will never read the poem. They must never have to
solve the adaptation, supply a missing causal step, or recognize a character
the book has not introduced. "Recoverable," "eventually clear," and
"understandable after looking back" are revision flags, not passing grades.
