Test Your Understanding

1. Why was young René allowed to stay in bed every morning while other students went to class?
That's right. René was born small and sickly, and was not expected to survive childhood. His teachers at La Flèche gave him special permission to stay in bed each morning — and those quiet hours became the foundation of his thinking life.
Not quite. René was born small and sickly — so fragile his doctors feared he wouldn't survive. His teachers let him stay in bed each morning as a medical privilege, and he used those hours to think.
2. What did René discover at school that excited him more than anything else — because it offered something no other subject could?
Exactly. When René opened Euclid's Elements, he found proofs that were true beyond any doubt. In a world full of opinions and arguments, mathematics was the one thing no one could dispute.
Not quite. It was mathematics — specifically Euclid's geometry. René was captivated because mathematical proofs offered absolute certainty, unlike philosophy or theology where people endlessly disagreed.
3. What happened to René on the night of November 10, 1619, alone in a stove-heated room in Germany?
Yes! Three extraordinary dreams in one night: a whirlwind and phantoms, a room full of sparks, and a book of poetry open to the question "What path shall I follow in life?" He woke shaking but certain — the answer was mathematics.
Not quite. That night, René had three vivid dreams — including one where a book of poetry opened to the question "What path shall I follow in life?" He woke before dawn, shaking but certain that mathematics could unify all knowledge.
4. Why did René choose to write his book Discourse on the Method in French instead of Latin?
Right. Latin was for scholars. French was for everyone — farmers, merchants, women, anyone with a curious mind. René believed that clear thinking should not be locked behind a language only the educated could read.
Not quite. René wrote in French so that anyone could read it — not just university scholars who knew Latin. He wanted his ideas about clear thinking to reach farmers, merchants, women, and anyone with a curious mind.
5. What does “Cogito, ergo sum” mean, and why did René consider it the most important idea he ever had?
Exactly. René imagined an evil demon deceiving him about everything — the world, his body, even mathematics. But one thing survived: if he was being deceived, there had to be an “I” being deceived. Three words that no demon, no dream, and no grief could destroy.
Not quite. “Cogito, ergo sum” means “I think, therefore I am.” René stripped away every certainty — even doubting whether the world was real — and found one thing that could not be taken: the fact that he was thinking proved he existed.
