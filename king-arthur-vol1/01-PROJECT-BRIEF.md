# King Arthur, Volume I: The Sword and the Table — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, ~54 pages: 48
story pages and 6 Chronicle pages (Mode 4).
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** Sir Thomas Malory, *Le Morte d'Arthur* (finished 1469–70, printed
by Caxton 1485), Books I–III, as the main quarry; Geoffrey of Monmouth,
*History of the Kings of Britain* (c. 1136), Wace, *Roman de Brut* (1155), and
Robert de Boron, *Merlin* (c. 1200), for single elements; Gildas (c. 540) and
the *Historia Brittonum* (c. 830) for the Chronicle pages. All public domain.
T. H. White's *The Once and Future King* and every modern retelling are
**not** used: still under copyright, and their inventions (Wart, the
animal transformations, Merlin living backwards) stay out.

---

## The engine

A boy who doesn't know who his parents are pulls a sword no lord in Britain
can move, loses the only father he ever had in the same moment, and has to
grow — against kings who want him dead and against his own pride — into a king
worth the sword.

**If Arthur reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a stretch,
the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.

## The spine

Arthur is fifteen, a foster son, his brother's squire, and wants one thing he
can't name: to be *somebody's*. He pulls the sword from the stone to fetch his
brother a weapon, and in one afternoon he is king of Britain and nobody's son,
because the man he called father kneels to him and says *I am not your
father*. The great lords, led by King Lot of Orkney, refuse a farm boy and go
to war. Arthur wins his first battle only because his brother rides in to save
him, then believes the songs about his shining sword, rides out alone to beat
a champion for glory, and is beaten: the sword from the stone breaks, and only
Merlin's magic saves his life. From a lake he gets a second sword, Excalibur,
and a lesson he doesn't want — the scabbard, which keeps its wearer from
bleeding, is worth ten swords. He stops trying to win and starts trying to
keep people safe. The book ends with the thing he builds out of that: a round
table where no knight sits higher than another, an oath that the strong serve
the weak, the old champion who beat him sitting at it — King Lot walking out
of it — and one seat with no name, kept for someone who hasn't come yet.

**The fork the last story page lands on:** Arthur has made a fellowship of
equals and earned it. But Lot is still out there, the Lady of the Lake will one
day ask for her gift, Merlin has just said *I won't always be beside you*, and
nobody knows who the empty seat is for.

## What this volume is

- The sword in the stone, done as a scene with a cost, not a coronation: the
  moment Arthur becomes king is the moment Ector stops being his father.
- A brother story: Kay, who lies about the sword, and later saves Arthur's
  life, and gets the only gift Ector asks for.
- A boy's education in what a king is, taught by failure, not by speeches.
- One real battle, one real duel, one lake, one table.
- Six Chronicle pages that tell the boys what was really going on: Britain
  after the Romans, whether Arthur existed, where each famous piece of the
  legend came from, and why the knights in every picture wear armour from 700
  years after Arthur.

## What this volume is not

- Not Camelot's greatest hits: no Lancelot, no Guinevere, no Grail, no
  Mordred, no Morgan le Fay. They are later storytellers' additions and later
  volumes' business; the last Chronicle page tells the boys they are coming.
- Not a history book with a story attached. The story pages carry no history.
- Not a tournament tour: there is one battle and one duel, and both turn
  Arthur.

## Cast ceiling — 8. Hard.

1. **Arthur** — 15, the boy who pulls the sword.
2. **Merlin** — very old, the wizard who hid him and now advises him; impatient,
   funny, knows more than he says.
3. **Sir Ector** — Arthur's foster father, a country knight.
4. **Sir Kay** — Ector's son, 19, just knighted, sharp-tongued, Arthur's
   brother in everything but blood.
5. **King Lot of Orkney** — the proudest of the lords; the face of everyone
   who refuses a farm boy.
6. **King Pellinore** — the old champion at the forest fountain who beats
   Arthur, and later sits at his table.
7. **The Lady of the Lake** — gives Excalibur; asks for a gift someday.
8. **King Uther** — Arthur's father, on his deathbed, one page.

**Collapses.** The eleven rebel kings (Lot, Urien, Nentres, Carados, the King
with the Hundred Knights…) → **Lot** and a field of banners. The Archbishop of
Canterbury, who in Malory sets up the stone and crowns Arthur → **Merlin** for
the stone; the crowning is done by unnamed churchmen with no balloons. Sir
Ulfius and Sir Brastias, Uther's and Arthur's knights → unnamed. Griflet, the
young squire Arthur knights and Pellinore beats → his news comes through
**Kay**. Kings Ban and Bors of France, who win the war for Arthur in Malory →
cut; Arthur's side is Arthur, Kay, and the common people's army.

## The audience promise

These readers have just finished *Three Kingdoms*, *The Three Musketeers*, and
*Monkey King* in this form and love oaths, brotherhood, a hero who is
underestimated, and real fights with real stakes. They know Arthur from a game
in which he is a legendary hero with a famous sword — so they arrive knowing
the *icon* and not the *boy*. The promise: **you'll meet the boy before the
legend, see exactly how he became the king in the pictures — and find out
which parts really happened.**

The central dramatic question: *can a boy nobody chose become a king people
would choose?*

Complexity budget: eight names, one villain faction (Lot and his banners), one
time jump (fifteen years, in the first movement, in narration), two magic
swords kept visibly different, four places that matter (Ector's farm, London,
the forest, Camelot). Assumed knowledge: none. The Chronicle pages assume none
either.

Refuses to become: a pageant of tableaux; a Disney sword-in-the-stone; a
lecture.

## Length

54 pages: seven movements of story (48 pages) and six Chronicle pages at the
breaks between them. Reached by cutting the conception, the May babies,
Balin, the French kings, Guinevere and everything after the founding of the
Table — not by compressing.

## The reader

A first-time reader who will never read Malory. They must never have to solve
the adaptation, supply a missing causal step, or recognize a character the
book has not introduced. "Recoverable," "eventually clear," and
"understandable after looking back" are revision flags, not passing grades.
