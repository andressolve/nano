# Volume Mandate — King Arthur, Volume I: The Sword and the Table

The craft authority for this book is the workspace method in `../../method/`,
starting with `00-CRAFT-MANDATE.md`. This file carries only what is specific to
this volume. **It relaxes no method rule.** If a future editor adds a clause
that lowers a number, widens a tolerance, or softens a gate, that clause must
name the rule it relaxes and why, in the clause itself.

## The adaptation stance — say it first

> This volume is an adaptation. Fidelity to the source is not a goal, not a
> tiebreaker, and not a defense. Every element earns its place by what it does
> for this reader in this book, or it is cut.

There is no single source to be faithful to, which makes the stance easier to
keep and more important: the Arthur legend is fifteen centuries of
storytellers each adding what their own readers wanted. This book is one more
of them, and says so on its Chronicle pages.

## Why this book has Chronicle pages (the readers' request, 2026-10-12)

The family has been playing *Civilization VI*'s heroes mode and asked for a
book about the heroes, starting with King Arthur. Offered a graphic novel or
an illustrated story, they asked for **something in between**: a story that is
not boring, and that also tells them *what was going on* — the history and the
myth — which they did not expect a graphic novel to give them. The owner added
Mode 4 (Chronicle) to the workspace method for exactly this. The story pages
obey every rule the other books obeyed; the context lives on six Chronicle
pages at the chapter breaks.

## The Chronicle — voice and register

**Voice.** An unseen narrator who talks straight to the reader in the present
tense, like the best museum guide alive: curious, a little conspiratorial,
specific. Names, dates, places, numbers, real objects. It says *legend* when
it means legend, *history* when it means history, and *nobody knows* when
nobody knows. It is never a character in the story and never in the story's
voice. It never says "it is important to know." It may say "here is a secret
the story doesn't tell you."

**Register.** Each Chronicle page is **a page from a chronicle**: warm ivory
vellum, a ruled margin, iron-gall brown ink, one red-and-lapis illuminated
initial with a touch of gold, and one dominant image in ink and watercolour
wash — a map, a real artifact painted as a museum study, a timeline drawn as a
scroll, a scribe at a desk. No painted full-bleed scene, no story character.
The reader must know at a glance they have stepped out of the story.

**The run of questions** (each answered by one Chronicle page, each raised by
the pages before it): Why does Britain have no king? · Did the sword in the
stone really happen? · Was there a real King Arthur? · What did knights really
look like? · Are there two magic swords? · Who told this story first?

## Volume constraints

- **Deaths are real and never drawn.** A battle kills men. No blood, wound,
  body, or severed anything is drawn; a blow lands in the gutter, a rider falls
  out of frame, the cost is shown as empty saddles, dropped shields, and the
  faces of the living. (Carried from *Three Kingdoms* Vol I, where it worked.)
- **The story pages wear the storytellers' armour, not 500 AD's.** Mail
  hauberks, surcoats, great helms, castles of stone: the world of the
  romances, about 1200. Chronicle page 4 tells the reader why, and shows them
  what a real warrior of 500 AD wore. This is a choice, not an error, and no
  critic should flag it.
- **Arthur's birth is a secret, not a scandal.** The source's account of how
  Arthur was conceived is cut entirely; the book says only that Merlin carried
  the baby away for his safety and that King Uther named him on his deathbed.
- **Names cost.** Eight people have names. Every lord, knight, monk, and
  messenger outside the eight is unnamed, unlocked, and has no balloon.

## The specific failure this volume must avoid

**The pageant, and the encyclopedia.**

Arthur is the most-illustrated king who never existed. The tempting book is a
procession of famous tableaux: sword, crown, lake, table, each beautiful and
each inert, with an Arthur who is noble from the first page. That Arthur is
calm and above it all, which is the engine written out. In this book Arthur is
**fifteen, a nobody, and hungry**: to belong, to be someone's son, to prove
the sword was right about him. He is quick to anger and quicker to charge. He
nearly gets himself killed twice by pride, and the second time it costs him
the sword from the stone. A page where Arthur is serene before page 44 is a
script-gate blocker.

The second failure is new to this method: **context leaking into scenes.**
With Chronicle pages in the book, the temptation is to let Merlin explain the
Saxons or Kay mention Rome. No. Story pages contain only what a character's
immediate situation needs; every bit of history waits for its Chronicle page.
A character explaining history is a script-gate finding.
