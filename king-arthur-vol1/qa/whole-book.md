## 1. Blind account (all 59 proofs read once, in order, at 600 × 900, before opening any story material)

### Retell
On a stormy night, the wizard Merlin carries a newborn down a cliff path away from a castle. Two winters later, King Uther is dying. He whispers to Merlin to ask whether "my Arthur" is safe. Then he tells his lords only that he has a son who will be king, and he dies. Nobody finds the son. The lords fight one another for thirteen years. On a muddy farm, old Sir Ector raises a boy who never wonders about any of it.

The boy is Arthur, squire to Ector's newly knighted son Kay. All three ride to a New Year tournament in London. Arthur asks whether he'll ever be a knight. Ector only says he'll be "whatever you were born to be," and sits up worrying afterward.

**First Chronicle page.** Who built London's crumbling walls (the Romans), why Britain has no king, and where history ends and legend begins.

In a churchyard stands a stone with a sword through an anvil and gold letters: whoever pulls it out is the true-born king. King Lot of Orkney fails and laughs it off as Merlin's trick. Merlin, in the church doorway, says the sword is waiting for someone else.

On the tournament field Kay finds he left his sword at the inn. Arthur runs to fetch it, finds the inn locked, remembers "a sword nobody was using," and draws it out of the stone with no effort and nobody watching. Kay sees what it is and shouts to Ector that he, Kay, is king. At the stone, Ector makes Kay swear on a book. Kay tells the truth. Arthur puts the sword back and draws it again. Ector and Kay kneel, and Ector says he is not Arthur's father: Merlin brought him a baby fifteen years ago. Arthur cries: this morning he had a father and a brother, and now he has only a sword. Ector offers to stay family ("When nobody's listening, lad. Always.") and asks the new king to make Kay his steward. The brothers settle Kay's lie, and Arthur swears Kay will be his steward for life. That night Arthur sits alone in the church, sure the lords won't kneel.

**Second Chronicle page.** The sword in the stone first appears around 1200 (Robert de Boron), about seven hundred years after any real Arthur.

The lords mock Arthur, led by Lot ("the king of the hens"). The people of London burst in shouting "WE WILL HAVE ARTHUR!" and the sword flares. Arthur is crowned and swears an oath. He asks Merlin who his father was, and Merlin promises to tell him "tonight." At the feast, Lot returns Arthur's gifts and threatens war. Merlin silences the hall and announces that Arthur is Uther's son, so the hall hears it before Arthur does. Lot leaves, promising battle. Alone, Arthur is furious at the broken promise. Merlin explains why and says he would hide him again. Uther saw him once and named him. Arthur asks about his mother; Merlin says that is another night's story. Arthur admits he has never fought. Kay offers to stand beside him. Merlin reminds him the people didn't ask how many fights he'd won. Arthur decides they will all fight together.

**Third Chronicle page.** Gildas, the History of the Britons, and Badon: nobody knows whether Arthur was real.

The night before the battle, Arthur's small army of farmers faces Lot's many campfires, and he arms himself. At Bedegraine, Arthur charges alone, is unhorsed in a stream and surrounded. Kay rides in ("that's my brother!") and hauls him back to the line. The spear line holds against Lot, the sword blazes, and Lot runs, shouting "This isn't over!" The aftermath is grey, with wounded men. Arthur says winning doesn't feel like winning. Merlin answers that he's looking at what it cost: good. Arthur admits he'd be dead without Kay. At Camelot, songs make Arthur a lone steel-armoured hero, and nobody in them is called Kay. Arthur starts to believe them.

**Fourth Chronicle page.** What warriors of about 500 really looked like, compared with knights of about 1200 and the plate armour of the 1400s.

Kay reports a knight at a forest fountain who has unhorsed three knights. Kay forbids Arthur to go ("Kings in the songs don't have stewards"). Arthur sneaks out at night, happy and composing his own song. The knight is King Pellinore. They joust, and the sword from the stone snaps. Pellinore offers to let him yield. Arthur refuses ("Kings don't yield"). As the killing stroke comes, Merlin's hand puts Pellinore to sleep. Arthur accuses Merlin of killing him; Merlin says Pellinore is snoring. Through questions, Merlin makes Arthur admit the fault: "Me." At dawn they walk out, Arthur holding the broken sword. Merlin says there is another sword.

An arm in white silk rises from a misty lake holding a sword. The Lady of the Lake offers Excalibur in return for a future gift, "whatever I ask." Arthur promises at once, over Merlin's alarm, and takes the sword from the hand. Merlin asks which is better, sword or scabbard. Arthur says the sword. Merlin calls him a fool: the scabbard means he cannot bleed. Arthur lays the sword down and buckles on the scabbard. At the fountain, Pellinore offers a rematch. Arthur declines because the scabbard would make it cheating, and invites Pellinore to Camelot. At the gate, Kay ("I hate waiting") takes the broken halves. Arthur admits Kay was right. Kay promises to tell him no "Every day."

**Fifth Chronicle page.** Two swords: Caledfwlch, Caliburnus, Escalibor, Excalibur. This book chose two swords so Arthur could lose the first.

At Pentecost, knights brawl over seats near the king, and one draws a knife. Arthur bangs Excalibur's pommel on the table: "Nobody bleeds in my hall over a chair!" By the hearth he invents a round table with no head; Kay calls it a cartwheel. It takes a year to build. Pellinore arrives on the eve of the next Pentecost. Names appear in gold as knights sit, Pellinore first, with one chair left blank. Lot comes under a white flag. Arthur offers him his own chair. Lot sneers at sitting level with a steward and "an old boar." Kay and Pellinore answer him, and Lot walks out. Arthur kneels and swears first: mercy, protection, never to fight for a wrong cause. The knights answer "WE SWEAR!" Kay offers to take the empty seat. Merlin says it is kept for someone who hasn't come, and anyone else who sits in it will be sorry. Kay: "I'll keep my own, then." Arthur says "Goodnight, Father" to Ector. Merlin reminds him he owes the Lady a gift and won't always be beside him. Who will tell Arthur when he's a fool? "Kay." Final story image: the empty chair in moonlight, waiting for a knight not yet born.

**Sixth Chronicle page.** The legend grew like a snowball (1136, 1155, 1180, 1470), ending at the real Winchester Round Table: "The storytellers weren't finished."

### Cast as I identified them cold
- **Merlin:** white forked beard, crooked staff, green ring.
- **Uther:** dying king.
- **Arthur:** straw-blond hair; brown squire's tunic, then a blue surcoat.
- **Kay:** black hair and moustache; black-and-yellow checked surcoat, later steward's black with yellow trim and keys.
- **Sir Ector:** bald, ruddy, russet.
- **King Lot of Orkney:** copper braided beard, bearskin cloak.
- **King Pellinore:** grey, huge, green surcoat with a white boar.
- **The Lady of the Lake:** long black hair, white gown.
- **Others:** churchmen, a minstrel, carpenters, and an unnamed wounded smith.

I made no misidentifications and assigned no lines to the wrong speaker. The script later confirmed every attribution.

### Causal chain
1. Uther's hidden heir leads to thirteen years of war.
2. The tournament brings Arthur to London.
3. Kay's forgotten sword leads Arthur to the stone.
4. Kay's lie is undone by Ector's oath.
5. The second draw leads to Ector's confession.
6. The lords' refusal is overruled by the people's choice, and Arthur is crowned.
7. Lot's insult forces Merlin's public reveal and Lot's declaration of war.
8. Arthur's reckless charge is saved by Kay; the people's line and the blazing sword win.
9. The songs make Arthur proud.
10. Disobeying Kay leads to the sword breaking against Pellinore.
11. Merlin's rescue and Arthur's admission ("Me.") lead to the Lady and Excalibur, at the price of an open promise.
12. Choosing the scabbard leads to declining Pellinore's rematch and recruiting him.
13. The fight over seats leads to the round table, Pellinore's arrival, Lot's walkout, and the oath.
14. The empty seat points to the future.

### The ending: what it cost and why the final image matters
Arthur ends with his crown, Excalibur, a sworn fellowship and a brother who will "tell him no every day." It cost him:
- **His lineage, told twice and both times painfully.** Ector is no longer his father except "when nobody's listening," and the truth about Uther came out publicly as a weapon against Lot.
- **The sword that chose him,** lost to his own pride.
- **A blank-cheque debt to the Lady.**
- **Merlin's warning** that he won't always be there.
- **Lot,** still an enemy outside the hall.

The empty chair in moonlight is the right last story image. It puts the theme of equality (no head, no foot) on the table and also holds an open promise, so the volume closes and invites the next one. The final Chronicle answers the reader's last question honestly ("is the Round Table real?") and turns "the empty seat" into "the storytellers weren't finished."

## 3. Acceptance questions by movement
Every answer below comes from the pages alone.

- **I. The Hidden King (pp 1–7)**
  - **Who acted:** Merlin, Uther and the lords.
  - **Wanted:** Merlin wanted the heir safe; the lords wanted the crown.
  - **Choice and why:** Uther names a son but not where he is, so the lords can't kill him.
  - **Affected:** all of Britain (thirteen years of war) and a farm boy who doesn't know.
  - **Arthur now believes:** he is Ector's son and Kay's squire, and hopes to be a knight one day.
  - **Why next:** the tournament brings him into London.
- **II. The Sword in the Stone (pp 8–19)**
  - **Who acted:** Arthur, Kay, Ector.
  - **Wanted:** Arthur wanted to get Kay a sword; Kay, for a minute, wanted the crown.
  - **Choice and why:** Arthur takes the "unused" sword; Kay lies, then tells the truth under oath; Ector confesses.
  - **Affected:** the whole family.
  - **Arthur now believes:** he is "not anybody's son," yet still has a father "when nobody's listening" and a brother he has sworn to keep.
  - **Why next:** in the morning the lords must see him.
- **III. The Kings Who Laughed (pp 20–27)**
  - **Who acted:** Lot, the people, Merlin, Arthur.
  - **Wanted:** Lot wanted to refuse a nameless boy; the people wanted Arthur.
  - **Choice and why:** Merlin reveals Arthur's father in public to stop Lot calling him "nobody's son"; Arthur chooses war for the people who chose him.
  - **Arthur now believes:** he is Uther's son and a king who has never fought.
  - **Why next:** Lot promised battle.
- **IV. The First Battle (pp 28–36)**
  - **Who acted:** Arthur charges alone and Kay saves him; the people hold the line.
  - **Arthur now believes:** first that winning cost something and Kay saved him; then, through the songs, that he is the hero of the songs.
  - **Why next:** a challenge reaches Camelot.
- **V. The Knight at the Fountain (pp 37–43)**
  - **Who acted:** Arthur disobeys Kay out of pride.
  - **Choice and why:** he refuses to yield to Pellinore because "kings don't yield."
  - **Affected:** the sword from the stone breaks, and Merlin has to save him.
  - **Arthur now believes:** "Me." His pride broke the sword.
  - **Why next:** Merlin points him to another sword.
- **VI. The Lady of the Lake (pp 44–50)**
  - **Who acted:** the Lady, Arthur, Merlin.
  - **Choice and why:** Arthur promises "whatever you ask" to get the sword, then chooses the scabbard over the sword, refuses an unfair fight with Pellinore and invites him to Camelot instead; he admits Kay was right.
  - **Arthur now believes:** he needs people who tell him no.
  - **Why next:** the court needs keeping in order.
- **VII. The Round Table (pp 51–59)**
  - **Who acted:** the knights fight over seats, so Arthur builds a table with no head.
  - **Who joined and who refused:** Pellinore joins; Lot refuses to sit level with others.
  - **Choice:** Arthur gives up his own higher seat and swears first.
  - **Arthur now believes:** he is "no higher than anyone," and Kay will tell him when he's a fool.
  - **Open threads:** the empty chair, the Lady's gift, Merlin's coming absence.

No movement depends on information outside the pages.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

### Typos and wrong letters transcribed
- **p2, panel 2 (Merlin):** rendered "Safe, and far from here, If your lords knew where…". The script reads "here. If". It is a comma where a full stop belongs, followed by a capital I. Candidate for a `pagerun.py --edit` fix.
- **p32, panel 1 (Lot):** rendered "There you aré, farm boy!", with a stray accent on "are". The script reads "are". Candidate for `--edit`.

### Small object and continuity defects
- **p11:** the script says "the gold letters glow at the bottom of the page." The rendered stone face shows a gold Celtic knot or pentacle emblem instead of the inscription. The stone is also shaped differently: a rounded boulder here, a squared block on p8. The inscription on p8 and the "gold letters" in the p10 prose carry the meaning, so no reading harm.
- **Pages 38–48, setting and season:**
  - **Fountain:** the script's "pool" by the fountain renders on p40–41 as a broad lake with snowy banks, while the fountain clearing on p39, p42 and p48 is a small basin.
  - **Season:** it swings between autumn oak leaves (p38, p42, p43), snow (p40, p47, p48) and green grass (p41).
  - **Harm:** a careful reader might briefly wonder whether the fountain's water is the Lady's lake. But p43 clearly says they walk out of the forest toward new open water. Cosmetic.
- **p55, panel 3:** Kay's face drifts heavier and older than on p51–57. He is still clearly Kay from the steward's black with yellow trim, the keys and his self-naming line ("This steward…"). Not a misidentification.
- **p55, panels 1–2:** the left/right staging of Arthur and Lot is mirrored from the script. Reading order and attribution are unaffected.
- **p19:** the timeline scroll is mostly empty vellum. This matches the script ("Almost all of it is empty vellum") and is the point of the image. Recorded only because it reads as sparse.
- **p56:** the open doors Lot walked out through, which the script places behind the oath, are not clearly visible. No reading harm.
