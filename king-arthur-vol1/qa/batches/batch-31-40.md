# Sequence gate — pages 31–40

## Blind sequence read

Kay breaks into the ring around Arthur, gets him back behind the line, and
scolds him for charging alone. Arthur answers that Kay came for him alone,
which lands as both gratitude and a warning Arthur does not yet fully accept.
When Lot attacks, Arthur stands with his people, raises the blazing sword, and
holds the line until Lot retreats.

The victory turns quiet. Arthur surveys the wounded and the wreckage, then
tends an injured man while Merlin makes him face what winning cost. Arthur
admits that Kay saved his life; Merlin tells him to remember. At Camelot,
however, songs turn the battle into a grander story: an invincible armoured
Arthur charged alone, while Kay disappears. Arthur gradually begins to believe
the flattering version. A chronicle page pauses the story to explain how later
storytellers likewise dressed Arthur in the armour of their own eras.

Back in Camelot, Kay reports a knight challenging travellers beside a forest
fountain. Arthur wants to answer personally, but Kay refuses and reminds him
that kings send knights. Already thinking like the songs, Arthur slips out
alone that night, delighted by his freedom and imagining the song the exploit
will become. At the fountain, King Pellinore accepts no royal exemption.
Arthur eagerly agrees to fight him. Their mounted clash gives way to a duel on
foot, and Pellinore shatters the sword from the stone, leaving Arthur shocked.

## Gate judgment

The batch preserves the material story, exact dialogue and narration,
attribution, named identities, and consequential continuity. Kay's rescue and
Arthur's solitary charge remain legible through the battlefield aftermath;
the songs' omission of Kay then causes Arthur's renewed recklessness. The
forest ride, fountain, horses, armour, heraldry, and sword carry coherently
through the final page turn. Arthur remains the focal character throughout,
with his emotional movement from chastened victory to seductive self-belief
clearly visible.

Pacing has purposeful variation: battle action yields to a silent aftermath,
an intimate reckoning, illustrated prose, a chronicle interlude, court
dialogue, a moonlit journey, and renewed action. The winter passage is a
controlled time compression rather than compressed dramatic summary. The
battle receives meaningful aftermath, while the shattered sword is an
effective unresolved cliffhanger rather than a missing consequence within
this batch.

No material sequence, text, identity, continuity, focal, pacing, or page-turn
failure justifies a complete-page redraw.
