# Sequence gate — pages 41–50

## Verdict

APPROVED

## Blind sequence record

Pellinore defeats Arthur and is about to deliver the killing blow after Arthur refuses to yield. Merlin puts Pellinore to sleep. Arthur first believes Merlin has killed the bravest knight he has met, then learns Pellinore is only snoring. Merlin makes Arthur confront the chain of choices that brought him there: Kay warned him, Pellinore offered to let him go, and Arthur's own pride made him refuse.

At dawn Arthur leaves the forest holding the broken sword and leading his horse. He is subdued and will not put the pieces down. Merlin tells him that another sword exists and leads him to a misty lake. An arm in white silk rises from the water holding a sword. The Lady of the Lake appears, names it Excalibur, and offers it to Arthur in exchange for any gift she may ask of him one day. Arthur promises before he knows the cost, despite Merlin's warning, and takes the sword and scabbard from the hand.

On shore Arthur delights in the sword, but Merlin teaches him that the scabbard is more valuable because its wearer cannot bleed. Arthur accepts the correction and secures the scabbard first. Returning through the fountain clearing, he meets the recovered Pellinore. Arthur refuses a rematch because the scabbard would make the fight unfair, then asks the stronger knight to come to Camelot and keep telling him no. Pellinore does not accept yet, but considers it.

At Camelot, Kay reveals that he waited while Merlin followed Arthur. Arthur admits that Kay was right and gives him the two pieces of the broken sword. Their teasing restores the brothers' ease: Arthur asks Kay to warn him again next time, and Kay promises to do it every day. The batch ends with a chronicle page explaining the name and traditions behind Arthur's two magic swords.

The emotional movement is coherent: defiance and near-death become shame and self-recognition; wonder and renewed power are immediately tempered by obligation and instruction; Arthur then demonstrates growth through restraint, recruitment, apology, and renewed trust.

## Gate judgment

- Material story and causality are intact across every turn. Merlin's rescue leads directly to the reckoning over Arthur's pride; the broken sword leads to the lake; the gift of Excalibur leads to the lesson about the scabbard; that lesson changes Arthur's choice when Pellinore challenges him again; the return to Camelot resolves the rupture with Kay.
- All scripted dialogue, prose, captions, and chronicle labels are present with the intended speakers or narrative attribution. The transitions do not create a material text or attribution error.
- Named identities remain legible and continuous: Arthur, Merlin, Pellinore, the Lady of the Lake, and Kay are visually distinct and stable. Arthur remains the focal character, and each page advances his error, learning, choice, or consequence.
- Consequential objects remain continuous: the first sword is visibly broken and carried through the remorse sequence before Arthur returns its halves to Kay; Excalibur and its red scabbard remain recognizable from the lake through the Pellinore encounter and Camelot return; Arthur's grey horse and the lake boat support the intervening travel and retrieval actions without confusion.
- Settings and time shifts read cleanly: the fountain at night, departure at dawn, misty lake, fountain by day, and Camelot at dusk form an understandable journey. Pellinore's sleep on pages 41–42 and recovery on page 48 need no omitted bridge.
- The batch has effective page-mode variation: two tense combat-aftermath pages, reflective illustrated prose, lake spectacle, dramatic bargain, retrieval spectacle, instruction, a changed-choice encounter, fraternal comedy, and a chronicle close. The three late dramatic pages have distinct functions and do not form a monotonous wall.
- No stretch reads as compressed summary. The illustrated-prose bridge on page 43 compresses travel while preserving Arthur's emotional aftermath, and page 50 is clearly framed as historical commentary rather than story compression.
- Consequential beats receive sufficient aftermath. Arthur's pride is answered by confession, subdued grief, refusal of an unfair rematch, recruitment of a truth-teller, and apology to Kay. The Lady's future gift remains an intentional open obligation, while Merlin's immediate warning gives the promise appropriate weight in this batch.
- Page turns preserve the intended questions and payoffs. No turn causes a focal break, false implication, lost action, or premature resolution.

No material sequence defect justifies a complete-page redraw.
