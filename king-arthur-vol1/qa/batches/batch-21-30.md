# Sequence gate — pages 21–30

## Blind sequence read

The people of London break the lords' resistance by acclaiming Arthur in the snowy churchyard, and the sword answers with light. Arthur is crowned and publicly promises justice and protection, then privately presses Merlin to tell him who his father was. Before Merlin can keep his promise, King Lot returns Arthur's gifts at the crowning feast, rejects the unknown boy as king, and threatens him with war. Merlin answers the challenge by revealing to the whole hall that Arthur is Uther's son. Lot laughs off the claim and leaves for the battlefield.

After the feast, Arthur confronts Merlin for breaking his promise and for taking him from his father. Merlin says concealment kept Arthur alive; Arthur's anger gives way to a quieter need to know whether Uther ever saw him. Merlin says Uther saw and named him on the night of his birth. Arthur asks about his mother, but Merlin postpones that story. Arthur then admits that Lot is right about his inexperience. Kay's joking loyalty steadies the exchange, and Merlin turns the people's earlier choice into Arthur's reason to fight for them. Arthur commits them all to the fight.

A chronicle interlude separates legend from surviving history: Gildas records Badon and Ambrosius but not Arthur; the later *History of the Britons* assigns Arthur twelve battles, ending at Badon; the historical question remains unresolved. Back in the story, Arthur sees how badly his small force is outnumbered, lies awake thinking about the ordinary people who chose him, and arms himself alone before dawn. At Bedegraine, his thin line faces Lot's much larger force across the valley. Arthur impulsively charges ahead of everyone despite Kay's warning, is swallowed by the enemy advance, and falls with his horse in the stream.

## Script comparison and gate judgment

The material story is complete and causal across the run. Public choice produces the coronation; Arthur's unanswered identity question is exploited by Lot; Merlin's public disclosure defeats that attack but causes the private rupture; the repaired conversation gives Arthur a reason to accept the coming fight; the camp and dawn pages turn that decision into immediate danger; the reckless charge pays off Arthur's admitted inexperience and creates a strong page-turn crisis.

All scripted text is present with correct wording and attribution. Arthur, Merlin, Lot, and Kay remain named and visually legible across their transitions. Arthur's crown carries from coronation to feast to the private confrontation, then disappears appropriately when he arms for battle. The sword remains the central legitimizing and battlefield object. The snowy London spaces, candlelit church and hall, night camp, dawn valley, opposing slopes, and stream establish a coherent progression of time and place. Page 29's opposing-force geography is preserved on page 30: Arthur rides away from his own line, down into the stream, and into Lot's closing force.

Focal integrity stays with Arthur even when the scale widens to the crowd, historical record, armies, and battlefield. The chronicle page is a deliberate pause rather than a derailment; its closing uncertainty sharpens the return to the vulnerable boy facing his first battle. The batch does not read as compressed summary. Pages 22–26 sustain a long dramatic exchange, but the coronation, public confrontation, private reckoning, and decision to fight each change the pressure and emotional register, so the stretch does not become a wall of interchangeable scenes. Spectacle, dramatic panels, chronicle, illustrated prose, and battlefield spectacle provide meaningful mode variation. Consequential beats receive aftermath: the acclamation is followed by coronation, Merlin's disclosure by Arthur's confrontation, the decision to fight by Arthur's sleepless fear, and the army reveal by the failed charge.

No material story, text, attribution, identity, continuity, focal, pacing, aftermath, or page-turn failure justifies a complete-page redraw.
