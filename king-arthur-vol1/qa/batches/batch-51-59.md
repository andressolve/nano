# Sequence gate — canonical pages 51–59

## Blind sequence record

At Camelot's Pentecost feast, the knights' argument over who deserves the seats nearest Arthur escalates until one draws a knife. Arthur stops the fight by striking the table with his sword and forbidding bloodshed over a chair. Later, with Kay and Merlin, he traces the quarrel to the long table's hierarchy and proposes a round table where everyone, including the king, sits equally far from the middle.

Camelot's carpenters spend most of the next year building the enormous table inside the hall. Arthur helps, Merlin watches, and Pellinore arrives when it is finished. Pellinore is the first knight to sit; the table answers each sitter with a name in gold, while one place remains unnamed. Lot then enters under a white flag, but rejects Arthur's offer of an equal seat and leaves rather than sit level with Kay or Pellinore.

Arthur responds by kneeling and taking the fellowship's oath first, admitting that he needs its restraints most. The knights swear with him. Kay notices the unnamed chair, and Merlin warns that it is reserved for someone yet to come and will punish any other sitter. After Ector leaves for bed, Merlin reminds Arthur of his debt to the Lady of the Lake and warns that he will not always remain at Arthur's side. Their exchange ends in humor, but the empty chair and the knight not yet born leave the hall in expectation. The final page widens from this completed episode to the centuries of storytellers who enlarged the Arthurian legend and promises that many more knights remain to be added.

## Script comparison and sequence judgment

- The visible sequence preserves the material story and its causal chain: the seating quarrel produces Arthur's round-table idea; the year of construction produces the inaugural seating; Lot's rejection sharpens the table's egalitarian meaning; the shared oath turns that meaning into conduct; and the unnamed seat carries the fellowship into future story.
- All scripted text is present and exact, with clear attribution across the transitions. Kay, Arthur, Merlin, Pellinore, Lot, and Ector remain distinguishable by face, costume, role, and blocking.
- Consequential continuity holds. Camelot's long feast table gives way to the table under construction and then the finished round table. Pellinore's boar device carries through his arrival, first seating, answer to Lot, and oath. Lot's white flag is visible on entry. The single unnamed place remains the focal empty chair, and Excalibur in its red scabbard lies before it during the late-night coda.
- Focal integrity and page turns are sound. Each turn answers the preceding question while creating the next: quarrel to solution, idea to construction, construction to first sitting, fellowship to refusal, refusal to oath, oath to mystery, and mystery to future legend. The chronicle shift on page 59 is visually decisive and reads as an intentional widening of scope rather than a break in continuity.
- Pacing is well controlled. Page 53 compresses the year of construction deliberately without reducing a consequential emotional beat to summary. Page 54 gives the finished table a full spectacle page. Although pages 55–58 are dramatic, they do not form a monotonous wall: public confrontation, ceremonial oath, comic-ominous prophecy, and quiet nocturnal aftermath have distinct rhythms and focal scales. Lot's departure receives an ideological answer in the oath, and the oath receives both immediate mystery and reflective aftermath.

No material story, text, identity, continuity, focal, pacing, aftermath, or page-turn failure justifies a complete-page redraw.
