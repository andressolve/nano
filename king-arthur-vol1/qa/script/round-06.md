## 1. Cold read

In a storm, an old man named Merlin carries a newborn away from a castle. Two years later King Uther is dying. He whispers "my Arthur" to Merlin alone, then tells his lords only that he has a son. Nobody finds the son. For thirteen years the lords fight over the crown. Meanwhile a boy grows up on Sir Ector's muddy farm as the squire to Ector's cocky son, Kay.

At a London tournament, Kay forgets his sword. The inn is locked, so Arthur pulls the sword out of the stone in the churchyard, the one King Lot failed to move. Kay claims it, then tells the truth when Ector makes him swear on the church's book. Arthur pulls the sword again and Ector kneels: "I'm not your father." Arthur loses a family and gains a kingdom. Ector keeps him anyway ("When nobody's listening, lad. Always."). The brothers make peace, and Kay becomes steward.

The lords laugh at Arthur. The people of London shout for him, and he is crowned. Lot throws back the king's gifts and threatens him at his own feast. Merlin tells the hall that Arthur is Uther's son, breaking his promise to tell Arthur first, and Arthur confronts him. At Bedegraine, Arthur charges alone and Kay drags him back. The townspeople's spear line holds, the sword blazes, and Lot runs. Arthur sees what the win cost.

At Camelot, songs make Arthur a lone hero and he starts to believe them. He sneaks out to fight Pellinore, refuses to yield, breaks the sword from the stone, and is saved only by Merlin's sleep-spell. Merlin's three questions ("Who told you not to come here?" and so on) make him name himself as the proud one. The Lady of the Lake gives him Excalibur in return for an open-ended promise. He chooses the scabbard over the sword and turns down a fight he could not lose. He gives the broken halves to Kay, who "will tell him no every day."

At the next Pentecost the knights brawl over seats, and Arthur invents a round table. Pellinore comes back to be the first to sit. Lot walks out rather than sit level with anyone. The knights swear an oath, and one seat stays empty for a knight not yet born.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book, and in places a very good one.

**Movement I (pp. 1–7).** The cold open works: a baby in the storm, and a dying king whispering a name only the wizard hears. p5 is the first real sign of life. Ector stares into the coals and gives an answer that isn't one ("you'll be whatever you were born to be"). Arthur catches it ("That isn't an answer."). That is subtext, and it plants the turn of p14. Arthur and Kay's teasing on p4 gives each of them a voice by the second balloon.

**Movement II (pp. 8–19).** The strongest opening act the method has produced. The want-from is sharp on every page:
- Kay wants not to be laughed at (p9).
- Arthur wants to be useful to Kay (p10).
- Kay wants the crown "for about a minute" (p12, p17).
- Ector wants the truth (p13), then wants to keep the boy (p16).

"Arthur gave it to me" (p13) is a five-word confession that carries the whole moral turn. Grief gets room to breathe across pp. 15–18. The attribution-strip test passes easily: Kay is sarcastic and defensive, Ector plain and warm, Arthur blurts.

**Movement III (pp. 20–27).** The heat is real. Lot is a pleasure to hate ("The king of the hens!"). p25 is the right scene: a boy furious at the man who decided his whole life. "I'd do it again" is Merlin at his best. The weak spot is the pair of balanced sentences before it (nonblocking note 1). Kay's "It's terrible… which makes it less terrible" (p26) is the book's comic voice working on a dramatic page.

**Movement IV (pp. 28–36).** The battle is enacted, not reported:
- the lone charge (p30);
- the fall in the stream (p30);
- Kay's rescue, "You're very bad at this" (p31);
- the line holding before the sword blazes, so the people's choice earns the miracle (p32).

pp. 33–34 stay with the cost. p35 is the best prose page in the book. The songs leave out Kay, and Arthur goes from laughing at them to believing them in three sentences. That is appetite turning into a flaw, on the page.

**Movement V (pp. 37–43).** The heart of the book. Arthur is hungry here and wrong:
- "Kings in the songs don't." / "Kings in the songs don't have stewards." (p37)
- "Kings don't yield" with a sword at his throat (p41).
- Merlin's three questions, "Kay." "Pellinore." "Me." (p42). One-word answers carry a self-conviction. That is the anti-terse rule used correctly, not broken.

**Movement VI (pp. 44–50).** The Lady has menace without saying a hostile word, and Merlin's alarm ("You've promised her anything at all") gives the bargain weight. p48 is the book's moral climax, and it is chosen, not announced: "That isn't a fight. That's cheating." p49 is the best page in the script. Arthur hands Kay the broken sword hilt first, echoing the tournament, and Kay answers "Every day."

**Movement VII (pp. 51–58).** Warmer and lower-stakes than V and VI. It still earns its place:
- the knife at the table (p51);
- "That's a cartwheel" (p52);
- Pellinore having "thought about it after all" (p53);
- Lot's sneer and Kay's retort (p55);
- "I'll swear first, because I need it most" (p56). Arthur is admitting his flaw in public, which is exactly where his arc should land.

Kay wanting the empty seat, then sitting down fast, puts Merlin's warning on a person and gets a laugh. The ending is quiet, and the Lady's unpaid gift and Merlin's "I won't always be beside you" give the reader appetite for Volume II.

**Chronicle pages.** As ideas they are excellent: true, specific, honest about legend versus history, and each built around one image. Pulling them out leaves a complete story. But four of the six break the method's first rule at the exact moment the reader steps out of the story (finding 1). They answer questions the reader cannot see.

The story pages have no logistics dialogue and no blocking aphorism. Every scene has a want-from, and Arthur has an appetite all the way through. The single blocker is cheap to fix and costs nothing in the story.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**1. pp. 19, 27, 50, 59: the chronicle pages answer questions that are never printed.**

**The failure.** The script header says each `**Question:**` line is non-rendering. Each of these pages opens on the answer anyway:
- **p19:** "Almost certainly not, and here is how we can tell."
- **p27:** "Nobody knows. Here is what we have."
- **p50:** "In this story there are two, and storytellers have argued about it for eight hundred years."
- **p59:** "Nobody made it up alone. It grew like a snowball."

Read aloud cold, each one gets the listener question §7.8 forbids: "Not what?" "Knows what?" "Two what?" "Made what up?" Each also breaks §7.4: the "it" and "two" have no antecedent anywhere on the page. And §7.1: the question that drives the page has no object.

**What the first-time reader has to repair.** They have just left a story scene for a vellum page with a different look. Before they can take in the answer, they must work out the question from the picture or from later sentences:
- p27's subject only becomes clear in the last lines of field 2 ("Was he real…?").
- p50's "two" only resolves to "swords" through the image.

That is "understandable after looking back," which the mandate treats as a revision flag. It happens at the one moment the reader most needs to be told where they are.

p50 has a second problem. Its declared question, "Wasn't the sword in the stone Excalibur?", assumes the reader already knows the source. Nothing in the story says the stone sword was Excalibur. Rendered as written, it would introduce an idea the reader never had.

**Prescribed revision.** On each page, make the question itself the first rendered words. Either open the first field with it, or add a `LABEL` at the top of the vellum, under the illuminated initial, in the chronicle's red ink. Suggested wording:
- **p19:** "Did the sword in the stone really happen? Almost certainly not, and here is how we can tell."
- **p27:** "Was there a real King Arthur? Nobody knows. Here is what we have."
- **p50:** Change the Question line to the one the story actually raises: *Why does Arthur get a second magic sword, and where does the name Excalibur come from?* Open with "Two magic swords? In this story, yes: the sword from the stone, and Excalibur. Storytellers have argued for eight hundred years about whether they were one sword or two."
- **p59:** "Who made up the story of King Arthur, and is the Round Table real? Nobody made it up alone. It grew like a snowball."

These add 8–20 words per page, and every page stays well under the 170-word chronicle ceiling. For consistency, do the same on p7 and p36 (see note 6).

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **p25, Merlin's voice.** "In the churchyard, I let the people choose you without a father. / At your own table, I wouldn't let Lot take your father from you." The parallel shape sounds like the writer, not the dry, impatient Merlin of p42 and p47. "Take your father from you" is also figurative where it needs to be concrete. Consider: "In the churchyard I kept quiet, so the people would choose you for yourself. At the feast Lot was telling the whole hall you were nobody's son, and I wasn't going to let him finish."
2. **p45, the Lady's title.** "And here comes the Lady of it" is awkward read aloud. It is also the only spoken introduction of her title before p58. Use "And here comes the Lady of the Lake herself."
3. **p55, Arthur's want.** Lot's line tells us Arthur sent for him, but Arthur never says what he wants from Lot. One clause would give the scene its engine and make the walkout a refusal of something offered. For example, before "Take my chair": "I'd rather have you at this table than across a valley, King Lot."
4. **p55, Kay's retort.** "This steward chased you off a battlefield" overstates what the book shows: Kay rescued Arthur, and the line and the sword broke Lot. It works as Kay's bragging. If you want it read that way, give Pellinore or Arthur a visible flicker of amusement in panel 3.
5. **p55, text load.** About 89 words in six balloons, including Lot's 23-word exit, is above the 80-word dramatic norm. Carry it to the packet as protected balloon lanes; do not cut words.
6. **pp. 7 and 36.** Both open clearly enough. Rendering their questions as well ("Who built those old walls?" / "What did warriors in Arthur's time really look like?") would keep all six chronicle pages consistent.
7. **p7, image accuracy.** The map shows "small longships with square sails." Whether fifth-century Saxon ships carried sails is uncertain; the surviving earlier boat (Nydam) was rowed. Draw them as oared ships, so the picture claims no more than anyone knows.
8. **p4, Kay's tournament.** "My first tournament!" is never paid off; the sword in the stone takes it over. A half-line somewhere later (p17 or p49) would close it and suit Kay's voice. Optional.
9. **Mode balance** over the 53 story pages is about 66 / 17 / 17, with chronicle pages at 10% of the book. That is within tolerance. The p59 audit warning is resolved: the story's last turn (the empty seat and the unpaid gift) lands on p58, before the closing chronicle.
10. **Facts.** All six chronicle pages hold up on the claims checked: Gildas, Ambrosius and Badon; *Historia Brittonum*; Geoffrey's Caliburnus and Merlin; Wace's table; Chrétien's Camelot, Lancelot and Grail "around 1180" (acceptably approximate); Malory "writing in prison" (his own "knight prisoner"); Winchester "around 1290"; plate armour "nearly a thousand years away." Each is hedged where it should be.
