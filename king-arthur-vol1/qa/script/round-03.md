## 1. Cold read

On a stormy night an old man named Merlin carries a newborn away from a castle. Two winters later the dying King Uther whispers to Merlin, and only Merlin, the name "Arthur." He tells his lords only that he has a son and dies. For thirteen years the lords fight over the crown. Meanwhile a boy grows up on Sir Ector's muddy farm, squire to his cocky older brother Kay.

They ride to London for a New Year tournament. A sword in a stone says that whoever pulls it out is king. King Lot can't pull it and calls it Merlin's trick, and Merlin stares at the farm boy. Kay leaves his sword at the inn. Arthur, finding the inn locked, pulls the stone sword out "as easily as a stick out of snow." Kay claims it as his own. Ector makes him swear on a book and Kay tells the truth. Arthur pulls it again, and Ector kneels and tells him "I'm not your father." Arthur sits on the stone and cries. Ector stays his father "when nobody's listening" and asks Arthur to make Kay his steward. The brothers settle the lie.

The lords laugh at "the king of the hens," and then the people of London pour in roaring his name. Arthur is crowned. Merlin promises to tell him his father's name tonight, but at the feast Lot threatens war and Merlin announces it to the whole hall. Arthur rages at him alone, Merlin says "I'd do it again," and tells him Uther named him. Arthur decides to fight for the people who chose him. In the battle he charges alone and Kay saves him ("You're very bad at this"). The people's spear line holds and the sword blazes. Arthur sees the cost and Merlin tells him to remember it.

Then come the songs, which leave Kay out. Arthur starts to believe them, defies Kay, sneaks out to fight Pellinore at the fountain, and the stone sword snaps. Merlin saves him and draws out the confession ("Me."). The Lady of the Lake gives him Excalibur for a promise he doesn't understand. Merlin teaches him that the scabbard is worth ten swords. Arthur refuses a fight he can't lose and invites Pellinore to Camelot. Knights fight over seats, so Arthur invents a table with no head. It is built over the winter and Pellinore arrives at the door. Names appear in gold. Lot walks out. Arthur kneels for the oath "because I need it most." One seat stays empty for a knight not yet born, and Merlin warns that he won't always be there: "Kay. He's had years of practice."

**Where I stopped:**
- **p18.** "All London was whispering his name." Only three people saw the second pull. I worked out that Ector must have told someone. Small stop.
- **p40–p43, the return home.** I waited for Arthur to face Kay and the book never brings them together. This is the one real hole in the heart of the book (Finding 1).
- **p45.** "The arm is hers" while she is walking toward us with two arms. Small stop.
- **p49.** "Yes" contradicts what I watched happen on p40. I reread the question twice (Finding 2).
- **p50 → p52.** The feast is in spring, and the next prose opens "All that winter." Summer and autumn vanish. Small stop.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes, and in places it's very good. This is the best version of this script so far. I would read it to the two boys gladly, and they would ask for the next one.

- **Movement I (pp 1–7).** Efficient and atmospheric. The p2 whisper ("My Arthur") to Merlin alone, set against the public "I have a son," is a clean secret that the reader holds. Page 4 has real sibling heat ("They'll hear you coming"). Page 5's "That isn't an answer." / "It's the only one I've got" is real subtext: Ector is evading, and you can feel it.
- **Movement II (pp 8–19).** The book's strongest run. Pages 12–17 are a family breaking and re-forming in the snow. "This morning I had a father and a brother. Now all I've got is a sword." "When nobody's listening, lad. Always." "For about a minute, I wanted it to be." / "Don't make a song out of it." The last of these also plants the songs without anyone noticing. All three pass the heat test and the attribution-strip test without effort.
- **Movement III (pp 20–27).** Lot is loud and one-note, as the ledger intends, and he's fun to hate. The p25 confrontation now has real anger and a Merlin who is not sorry. "Did he ever see me?" / "Once. The night you were born. He named you." lands because p2 paid for it. The p26 decision is now chosen rather than pronounced.
- **Movement IV (pp 28–36).** The charge, the rescue and "You're very bad at this" are excellent. The p33 aftermath and p34 "Then why does it feel like this?" give the win its cost. Page 35, "Nobody in the songs was called Kay," is the best single line in the book. It is the appetite turning into pride, enacted through a minstrel rather than explained.
- **Movement V (pp 37–43).** Arthur is hungry and wrong, which is exactly what the volume mandate asks for. "Kings in the songs don't have stewards." The catechism on p42 ("Kay." / "Pellinore." / "Me.") is the moral turn enacted by Arthur's own mouth. Good.
- **Movement VI (pp 44–49).** The lake is a ritual, but the scabbard question gives it a choice, and p48 ("That isn't a fight. That's cheating.") shows the change in Arthur instead of having anyone say it. The cost is that the movement never goes home: the person Arthur wronged is absent, so the lesson is paid to a stranger (Pellinore) and to Merlin, never to Kay. Here the book goes briefly cold where it should be warmest.
- **Movement VII (pp 50–58).** Lively. The cartwheel, the sawing "whenever Kay wasn't looking," Pellinore at the door, Lot's walk-out, "I'll swear first, because I need it most," and Merlin's last line about Kay all work. Page 57 tries to settle the Kay debt with a joke between two other people. It is lovely, but it can't stand in for the scene the book skipped.

**Presence tests:**
- **Logistics and aphorism:** no logistics dialogue anywhere. No aphorism worth blocking. Page 34 "Because you're looking at what it cost. Good." and p51 "Knights always have, and they always will…" are Merlin's own dry voice, not the writer's.
- **Want-from:** passes on every dramatic page.
- **Serene protagonist:** none before p44; Arthur is never serene.
- **Page-turn reasons:** all real.

## 3. Mandatory findings

### 1. Pages 43–50: Arthur never faces Kay. The movement's central wrong has no aftermath, and the book's warmest relationship loses its middle beat.

**The failure.**
- Movement V is built on Arthur defying his brother. Kay forbids it on p37 ("The answer is no"). Arthur sneaks out on p38 "like a boy stealing apples" (p43). He names Kay first when he confesses ("Kay.", p42).
- After that, Kay simply reappears on p50 doing steward's work, as if nothing happened.
- The return to Camelot, Kay finding the king gone, and Arthur bringing home the sword that "chose him" in two pieces are all offscreen.
- The lesson is enacted toward Pellinore ("knights… who aren't afraid to tell me no," p48) and spoken about Kay by Merlin (p57), but never between the brothers.

**What the reader has to repair.** They have to supply Kay's reaction and the reconciliation themselves. They also have to assume that Arthur's change ("Me.") reached the person it was owed to. Both mandates name this case: §4.3 "stay for the consequence," and §3f "any consequential beat missing its aftermath — including the protagonist's — is a finding."

It is also the protagonist's want, unpaid. The ledger gives Arthur's volume want as "from Kay, to be his equal," and Kay's secret want as to be needed. The p57 punchline only works if the reader has seen Arthur actually come back and need Kay.

**Revision.**
- Add one dramatic page after p48 and before the Excalibur chronicle, so the chronicle still sits at the movement break. Renumber everything after it.
- **Setting:** the Camelot gate at dawn, one location. Kay is waiting in the gateway, cloak thrown over his nightshirt, a guard behind him. He has clearly been up all night.
- **Arthur:** he wants Kay's forgiveness without having to ask for it. He doesn't make a speech. He holds out the two halves of the stone sword, hilt first, the way he once handed Kay a sword on p12, and says plainly: "You told me no, and I went anyway. You were right."
- **Kay:** he wants to stay furious and fails. He deflects. Something in his voice, for example: "Of course I was right. I'm always right. It's exhausting." Then he sees the red scabbard: "…What is that?" or "Where did you get *that* one? Don't tell me. A lake."
- **The turn:** Kay takes the broken halves off him and carries them for him, a squire's job reversed. Or Arthur asks, "Will you tell me no again, next time?" and Kay answers "Every day."
- Keep the page text in the 45–80 word dramatic budget.
- This page turns p57's "Kay. He's had years of practice." from a joke into a payoff.

### 2. Page 49 (Chronicle): the answer begins "Yes," which contradicts the story and overstates what the tradition says.

**The failure.** The question is "Why is the new sword called Excalibur, and is it the sword from the stone?" The first word of the answer is "Yes, and storytellers have been mixing them up for eight hundred years." A reader takes "Yes" as "Excalibur is the sword from the stone."

**What the reader has to repair.**
- On p40 the reader watched the stone sword snap, and on p42–43 Arthur carried the halves home. Now the chronicle tells them it is the same sword. They must decide whether the book or the chronicle is wrong, and that is solving the adaptation.
- Field 2 then reverses the "Yes": "some storytellers said it was Excalibur, and others said it was a different sword."
- So the page states, with a certainty nobody has, a claim its own second field takes back. That fails the Mode 4 rule "true, and honest about which truth."
- The question as written also asks something the story already answered (no, the stone sword broke). The question the story actually raised, and the one in the mandate's planned run, is "Are there two magic swords?"

**Revision.**
- Reword the **Question:** to the one the story raised, for example: "Are there two magic swords? And why is the new one called Excalibur?"
- Open the answer with the honest version, for example: "In this story, two — and storytellers have argued about it for eight hundred years."
- Make Field 2 say plainly that some old books make the stone sword and Excalibur one sword, some make them two, and Malory does both. Then say that this book chose two, so that Arthur could lose the first one.
- Keep everything else on the page.

### 3. Page 58 (Chronicle): "the Grail, a holy cup" is credited to Chrétien de Troyes around 1180. It is stated as fact and it is wrong.

**The failure.** Field 2: "Around 1180, a French poet, Chrétien de Troyes, added Camelot, a knight named Lancelot, and the Grail, a holy cup the knights would one day search for."
- Chrétien's *graal* is a mysterious, holy serving dish, unfinished and unexplained.
- It became a cup (the cup of the Last Supper) only with Robert de Boron, around 1200. The script's own p19 already introduces him.

**What the reader has to repair.** Nothing, which is the problem: they will believe it. The Mode 4 rule is "nothing invented is presented as fact," and this page is the one that closes the book's account of who added what.

**Revision.** Change the clause to something like: "…and the Grail — a mysterious, holy dish that later storytellers, starting with Robert de Boron, turned into a cup the knights would one day search for." Or keep the Grail and drop the word "cup." Keep the word count within the 150-word chronicle budget; the page has room.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **p18, how the news spread.** "All London was whispering his name" follows a reveal with only three witnesses. Add one clause, for example: "By evening Sir Ector had told the archbishop, and the archbishop had told everyone."
2. **p21 → p22, from "no" to crowning.** The lords' refusal turns into a coronation offscreen. One caption would close it, for example: "The lords could not shout down all of London. Arthur was crowned."
3. **p45, the arm.** "The arm is hers" while the Lady walks toward them makes a careful reader count arms. Either let Merlin call it "her lake's arm," or drop the possessive: "That arm belongs to the lake. And here comes the lady of it."
4. **p50–p52, the seasons.** The feast is at "the spring feast of Pentecost," and the next prose opens "All that winter… three months." Summer and autumn disappear, and so does Pellinore's near-year of "thinking about it." Something like "It took them till the end of winter" or "most of a year" would fix it. Also consider naming the occasion on p53 ("At the next Pentecost").
5. **p14, mode.** It is tagged spectacle, but the turn is carried by a balloon ("I'm not your father"). It works as written. If the prompt treats it as near-silent, the lettering lanes may be starved. Consider tagging it dramatic. The mode balance (34/9/9, 65/17/17) can absorb that.
6. **p16 → p55, the "Father" line.** "When nobody's listening, lad. Always." asks to be paid off. On p55 Arthur looks at Ector across a crowded table. A single private "Father," whispered when the hall has emptied (p55, or Ector in the doorway on p57), would close the book's first wound.
7. **p53 and p55, seating.** Pellinore is "directly across" from Arthur on p53, and Arthur looks "across the table at Ector" on p55. Settle the seating so both readings hold, for example Ector across and Pellinore across-and-left.
8. **The Lady's promise (p45).** It is never recalled by the end of the volume. One line in p57's caption or Merlin's farewell ("And remember you owe the Lady a gift") would keep the hook alive. It is optional; the empty seat and Merlin's warning already carry the ending.
9. **p36, "the poets of about 1200 gave him mail and a great helm."** It was the artists and the knights of the poets' own day, more than the poems themselves. "The storytellers and painters of about 1200 pictured him in mail and a great helm" is safer.
10. **p17.** "Kay will be my steward for as long as we both live" is said to Kay in the third person. It reads as a vow before Ector as witness. Consider "You'll be my steward…" or staging Arthur turning to Ector as he says it.
11. **Five-word audit.** All 21 short balloons in the audit file pass: each is a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before it, with one visible referent. The p42 trio ("Kay." / "Pellinore." / "Me.") answers three full questions and is the strongest short dialogue in the book. "Then I'm sorry for it." and "I'll think about it." both have single, unambiguous referents.
12. **Ledger.** `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md` still gives the sword breaking on p39 and Excalibur arriving on p45. Update it after the renumbering, as `02-SCRIPT-LOOP.md` §4 requires.
13. **Budgets.** Pages 8 (~100 words, including the stone inscription) and 54 (~96 words) are above 80 words. They are within the ~105 allowance only if their balloon lanes are protected. Flag both in packets. Every other page fits its mode budget. All chronicle pages are 100–135 words and fall at real chapter breaks. Taking every chronicle page out, the story still passes the paraphrase test.
