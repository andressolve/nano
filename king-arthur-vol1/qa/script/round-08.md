## Cold read

A wizard carries a newborn baby down a cliff path in a storm. Two winters later King Uther is dying. He whispers "My Arthur" to Merlin and tells his lords only that he has a son. Then he dies. Nobody finds the son. The lords fight each other for thirteen years. On a muddy farm, a boy grows up as Sir Ector's son and as his loud brother Kay's squire.

They ride to a tournament in London. A sword sits in a stone that no lord can move. Lot of Orkney, big and laughing, calls it one of Merlin's tricks. Kay leaves his sword at the inn, and Arthur pulls the stone sword out just to get his brother a sword. Kay claims it for a minute. Ector makes him swear, and Kay tells the truth. Ector watches Arthur do it again, kneels, and says "I'm not your father." Arthur's line, "This morning I had a father and a brother. Now all I've got is a sword," is where the book got me. Ector asks him to make Kay steward, and the brothers settle it.

The lords laugh at the farm boy. The people of London storm the churchyard shouting for him, and he is crowned. Merlin promises to tell him his father's name that night, then shouts it at Lot in the hall first. Arthur confronts him, and Merlin says he'd do it again. Arthur decides to fight because the people chose him.

At Bedegraine, Arthur charges alone and is unhorsed. Kay rides in for him ("You're very bad at this"). The people's line holds, and the sword blazes. Afterwards, among the wounded, Arthur learns the lesson. Then the songs make him forget it. He sneaks out against Kay's "no" to fight the knight at the fountain. Pellinore breaks the stone sword and nearly kills him, and Merlin puts Pellinore to sleep. Merlin's three questions end with Arthur saying "Me."

At the lake, Arthur promises the Lady anything for Excalibur, and Merlin is alarmed. He picks the scabbard over the sword. He refuses a rematch he can't lose and invites Pellinore to Camelot. At the gate he hands the broken halves to Kay: "You were right." / "Every day."

Knights fight over seats, so Arthur builds a round table. Pellinore comes. Lot comes under truce, is offered Arthur's own chair, and walks out. The knights swear. Kay tries the empty seat and is warned off. Arthur says "Goodnight, Father." Merlin reminds him of the Lady's gift. The seat waits for a knight not yet born.

## Is it alive?

Yes, and I'll say it plainly: this is a good book, and Kay makes it one. Strip the speaker names and every voice is still recognisable:
- **Kay** is fast and sarcastic, and loves Arthur sideways ("It's true. I've seen him fight. It's terrible.").
- **Lot** dares people in every sentence.
- **Pellinore** is slow, formal, and never insults anyone.
- **Merlin** is dry and asks questions he already knows the answers to.
- **Ector** speaks in few, plain, warm words.
- **Arthur** blurts things out.

**Movement by movement:**
- **I (pp 1–7):** atmospheric and economical. The heat sits in Ector's evasion on p5 ("That isn't an answer." / "It's the only one I've got."). The want is clear: Arthur wants Ector to say he can be a knight, and Ector can't say it.
- **II (pp 8–19):** the best run in the book. Kay's grab for the crown is real appetite, and his swearing is real heat. The "Father" thread opened on p16 ("When nobody's listening, lad. Always.") pays off on p58. That is subtext doing structural work.
- **III (pp 20–27):** Lot gives the book a loud, specific antagonist. On p25 each person wants something from the other, right now: Arthur wants an apology, and Merlin refuses it.
- **IV (pp 28–36):** the charge-and-rescue is enacted, not reported. The aftermath page (p34) earns its silence. Pride returns on p35, which sets up Movement V.
- **V (pp 37–43):** Merlin's three questions ending in "Me." are the best-written exchange in the book.
- **VI (pp 44–50):** the lake pages could have been a pageant. They are rescued by the bargain ("you don't even know what she'll want") and by the scabbard choice, which is acted out, not explained. Arthur refusing the rematch on p48 is the turn of the book.
- **VII (pp 51–59):** the table comes out of a fight the reader watches. The heat comes from Lot walking out, Kay going for the empty seat, and the goodnight. Arthur is never calm or above it all before p44, and never preachy after it.

I found no logistics dialogue and no aphorisms in the story pages. Every page has a real reason to turn it.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p25, panel 2, Merlin: "Yes. If those lords had known there was a baby, you'd have been drowned in a well before your first birthday."**

The reader has been told twice that the lords *did* know there was a son:
- On p2, Uther announces it to them.
- On p24, Merlin says "Some of you heard Uther say he had a son."

And on p2 Merlin gave the real danger in different words: "If your lords knew *where*, he wouldn't live a week."

So p25, the one line that justifies the whole hidden-heir premise, now gives a second, different reason. A careful reader has to work out for themselves that "before your first birthday" means the two years before Uther spoke. That is solving the adaptation, on the most important "why" in Movement III.

**Fix:** make it the same danger as p2: "Yes. If those lords had found out where you were, you'd have been drowned in a well before you could walk." Keep "I'd do it again."

**2. p36, Chronicle Field 2: "So why does everyone draw Arthur like the man on the right?"**

This is false, and it contradicts the page before it. p35 shows the songs and the hanging dressing Arthur "in shining steel plate". The popular image of Arthur is the plate knight, which is the ghost outline, not the man on the right in mail and great helm. A reader comparing the image with the field will ask "but they don't. The hanging drew him in plate." That puts a confusion at the centre of the page's one idea. The mandate's Mode 4 rule says every claim must be true, so this is a chronicle accuracy failure.

Field 1 also says the warriors of about 500 "looked like the man on the left", which is more certain than the scant evidence allows.

**Fix:**
- Field 2 should open: "So why is Arthur almost never drawn like the man on the left? Because every storyteller dressed him in the armour of their own time…" The rest can stay as written.
- In Field 1, change "looked like" to "probably looked like".

**3. p59, label under the Winchester table: "Winchester: made 1290, painted 1522"**

The label is more certain than the field above it ("made around 1290") and more certain than the evidence:
- The tree-ring dating gives timbers felled around 1250–1280. The 1290 tournament is a probable occasion, not a recorded one.
- The painting is usually dated 1516–1522, most likely 1522.

Labels are what a reader remembers, so the hedge has to be in the label too. The Chronicle voice is the one that says "nobody knows" when nobody knows.

**Fix:** change the label to "Made about 1290, painted about 1522" (six words, within the label limit). Field 2 already names Winchester.
