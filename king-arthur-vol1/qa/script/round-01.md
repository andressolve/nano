## 2. Cold read

A storm, a wizard, a baby carried down a cliff. A dying king names "my son, Arthur", and nobody has heard of a son. Fifteen years of lords fighting. Then a farmyard: a boy named Arthur polishing his big brother Kay's helmet and teasing him. I had the irony at once (this is the lost son) and enjoyed it. The father dodging "will I be a knight?" by the forge is a good quiet page. They ride to London.

**Stopped (p7):** a history page about Rome. It asks why Britain has no king, but the story had just told me why: Uther died and nobody knew his son. Now there were two answers, and nothing told me which one was the story and which was history.

The sword in the stone, Lot failing, Merlin staring at the boy. **Stopped (p8):** when did this stone appear, and what does it have to do with the tournament? I guessed it was a test for the gathered lords. Then the forgotten sword, the locked inn, the easy pull. This run (pp 9–14) is the best stretch in the book. Kay grabbing the crown ("I'm the king of Britain!") was a real gasp, and Ector's "I'm not your father" hits hard.

**Stopped (pp 15–16):** Kay just tried to steal his brother's crown, and nobody says a word about it. Arthur makes him steward. I wanted Arthur to look at him.

**Stopped (p17):** "the way Ector had". Who is Ector? I worked out it was the father. He had been "Father" for 16 pages and was never named.

The lords laugh, the people roar, Arthur is crowned. **Stopped (p21):** Arthur has spent p17 aching to know who his father was. Now the one man who knows is at his elbow, and he asks something else and accepts "Not today." At the feast Lot threatens him, Merlin blurts "son of Uther", and Lot's exit swallows the reveal. The question the whole volume is built on gets one line, then one more on p24.

**Stopped (p26):** a second history page between Arthur pulling on his mail and the dawn charge.

The battle: Arthur charges alone, Kay saves him ("You're very bad at this", which made me laugh), and then the sword glows and the war is over. **Stopped (p30):** where did the glow come from, and what were all those farmers for?

Aftermath, the songs, "Nobody in the songs was called Kay". Very good. The fountain sequence is strong: Arthur sneaking out, Pellinore, the sword snapping, Merlin's "He's snoring."

**Stopped (pp 42–44):** an arm holds the sword in the middle of the lake. Then a woman walks to the shore. Then Arthur is in a boat taking the sword from a hand in the middle of the lake. Was the arm hers? Where did the boat come from?

Pellinore spared and invited. The Round Table invented by Arthur himself (good). **Stopped (p51):** why has Lot shown up under a truce flag, and how does he know Kay's word "cartwheel"? The oath. The empty seat. Merlin says "I won't always be beside you." Nothing has prepared that, and Arthur doesn't answer. **Stopped (p55):** "Next came Lancelot." So Lancelot is the knight for the empty seat? But p54 said that knight hadn't been born.

**Paraphrase after one read:** a hidden heir pulls the sword, loses his father and gains a kingdom, is laughed at by the lords and chosen by the people, wins a battle, gets proud, breaks his sword, earns a better one, turns down an unfair fight, and builds a table where nobody sits higher than anyone else. It holds. The gaps are around the edges of the big beats, not in the spine.

## 3. Is it alive?

**Movement I (pp 1–7): alive.** The Kay/Arthur banter on p4 gives two voices in four lines. Ector at the forge (p5) is real subtext: a man who knows and won't say. The Uther page is a bit flat (Merlin's opening line exists to set up the scene). The chronicle at the end is the weakest page in the movement.

**Movement II (pp 8–18): the best thing in the script.** Kay's grab for the crown, Ector making him swear on the book, the long pause, "Arthur gave it to me": that is heat, want, and a choice under pressure, all acted out on the page. "I'm not your father" lands. Then the movement drops its own best thread: Kay's lie is never settled between the brothers.

**Movement III (pp 19–26): half alive.** Lot is a great loudmouth ("The king of the hens!"). p20 is a real spectacle turn. But the emotional centre of the whole book, *whose son am I*, is answered as a heckle in a 25% panel. The aftermath gives Merlin a line I can only call a fortune cookie: "Go and happen." Kay's "It's true. I've seen him fight. It's terrible" saves p24.

**Movement IV (pp 27–34): exciting, then hollow at the turn.** The charge, the fall, and Kay's rescue are excellent. The win comes from a glow nobody set up. The farmers who "had chosen him" and would "not come home" never touch the battle. The aftermath pages (31–32) are moving until Merlin starts speaking in maxims. p33 is a lovely page.

**Movement V (pp 35–41): alive, the best movement after II.** p35 is the best dialogue page in the book ("Kings in the songs don't have stewards"). Arthur sneaking out with a song already made up is pure appetite. Pellinore is a fine creation. The sword snapping hurts. Then Merlin ends the scene with a sermon.

**Movement VI (pp 42–47): beautiful pictures, muddled staging, and the moral spoken aloud.** Arthur literally says the book's theme, which the ledger says nobody ever says aloud. Turning down the fight with Pellinore is the right act, but the line explaining it is vague.

**Movement VII (pp 48–55): warm and well built until the last two pages.** Arthur inventing the round table out of a fight over seating is real agency. "I'll swear first, because I need it most" is the best line Arthur has. The ending fades out: an unprepared warning, a silent protagonist, and a chronicle that sets up a wrong idea.

**Is it any good?** Yes, often. Kay, Lot, and Pellinore have real voices. Arthur is hungry and rash, not serene. Most pages have something at stake between the people on them. That makes the failures sharper: the script keeps flinching at its own biggest moments (Kay's betrayal, Arthur's parentage, the battle's turn, the ending), and Merlin, the second lead, speaks almost only in verdicts. This is a strong draft, not yet a book I would hand over and walk away from.

## 4. Mandatory findings

**1. pp 1–2: Merlin's reason for hiding the baby, and the order of events, never reach the page.**
- **Failure:** p1 shows Merlin carrying a newborn away "and told no one where". p2 puts Merlin at Uther's bedside the same night ("hood back now"), and Uther dies. No rendered line says *why* the heir was hidden. The volume mandate says the book will say Merlin carried the baby away "for his safety", but that phrase exists only in the docs. The order is also unclear: did Merlin take the baby away and then come back up the cliff, or is p1 after p2?
- **Reader repair:** the reader has to invent a motive for a wizard who let Britain bleed for fifteen years rather than produce the heir. The reader also has to guess the sequence.
- **Revision:** add a whispered exchange on p2, staged with the lords out of earshot. Uther, gripping Merlin's sleeve: "Is he safe?" Merlin, low: "Safe, and far from here. If your lords knew where, he wouldn't live a week." Add "Earlier that night," to the start of p2's caption, or another time marker, so p1 reads as after the naming or the order is otherwise explicit.

**2. pp 3–17: Arthur's foster father is never named before the story depends on his name.**
- **Failure:** Ector speaks on pp 4, 5, 13–16 and is only ever "Father" on the page. His name first renders on p17 ("the way Ector had") and p21 ("You're the one who took me to Ector").
- **Reader repair:** at p17 the reader has to work out by elimination that "Ector" is the man they knew as Father.
- **Revision:** name him early. In p3 PROSE FIELD 2: "…on a muddy little farm belonging to an old knight called Sir Ector, a boy grew up…". Or on p4 let Kay say it: "Sir Ector of the Mud Farm has news!"

**3. p7: the chronicle competes with the story's answer and never says which part is legend. Its page-turn line is also false.**
- **Failure:** the Question asks "why is Britain full of lords fighting… instead of having one king?" The story already answered that on pp 2–3: Uther died and his heir was lost. The chronicle gives a second, historical answer (Rome left) and never tells the reader that Uther, Merlin, and the hidden heir are legend placed inside that real history. Separately, the page-turn line says the reader "now knows why a sword in a churchyard could decide who rules", but no sword has appeared yet.
- **Reader repair:** a first-time reader can't tell whether Uther is a historical king, or which of the two causes is "true".
- **Revision:** end CHRONICLE FIELD 2 with the hand-off: "Into that real darkness, storytellers later put a king called Uther, a wizard called Merlin, and a lost boy. That part is legend." Rewrite the page-turn line so it is true of this page and p6.

**4. p8: the stone appears from nowhere, with no link to the tournament.**
- **Failure:** the sword, anvil, and inscription are presented as if already known. Nothing says when the stone appeared, who put it there, or that the tournament gathering is the occasion for the lords to try it.
- **Reader repair:** has it been there fifteen years? Is it why everyone came? The reader guesses.
- **Revision:** add a CAPTION to Panel 1: "On Christmas morning, a great stone had appeared in the churchyard with a sword through it. Every lord who came for the tournament tried to pull it out." Lot's "Merlin's tricks" line then has something to push against.

**5. pp 12–16: Kay's betrayal has no aftermath between the brothers.**
- **Failure:** on p12 Kay claims the crown for himself, the boldest move anyone makes in the movement. p13 he confesses under oath. From then on Arthur never reacts to it, and Kay never speaks to Arthur about it. On p16 Arthur swears him in as steward over a silent forearm grip. The mandate (§4.3) says betrayal must get reaction or aftermath.
- **Reader repair:** "Kay lied and nobody cares?" Every reader will want Arthur to look at his brother, and the script looks away.
- **Revision:** give p16 Panel 3 the exchange the grip is standing in for. Kay, not meeting his eyes: "I said it was mine. For about a minute, I wanted it to be." Arthur: "I know. Then Father made you swear, and you told the truth." Kay: "Don't make it a song." Then the grip and the steward oath. If the page gets crowded, give it its own panel or split it; do not cut Ector's request.

**6. pp 17 and 21–24: Arthur's parentage, the engine of the volume, is answered as a heckle.**
- **Failure:** p17 has Arthur aching to know "who his mother was, or his father". On p21, with Merlin at his ear, he asks only "You're the one who took me to Ector. Aren't you?" and accepts "We'll talk. Not today." That talk never happens. The answer comes in public on p23, squeezed into a 25% panel ("King Uther? Uther was my father?") between Merlin's announcement and Lot's war threat. That puts a major revelation and the next big threat on one page, against §4.8. The aftermath on p24 is one line. Arthur never confronts the man who took him from his dying father and let Ector raise him on a lie, though the ledger says this is exactly Merlin's moral weight ("deciding a boy's whole life without asking him"). The mother question from p17 is dropped.
- **Reader repair:** the reader has to supply Arthur's feelings about the single fact he most wanted, and has to accept that the protagonist didn't ask for it.
- **Revision:**
  - **p21:** Arthur asks the real question: "You know who my father was. Tell me." Merlin: "Not here. Not with Lot in the room. Tonight." That gives Merlin a concrete reason to hold back.
  - **Add a page after p23:** the empty hall, Arthur and Merlin alone, with real heat. Arthur: "You took me from my father the night he died. You let Ector lie to me for fifteen years." Merlin: "Yes. If those lords had known there was a baby, they'd have drowned you in the moat. I'd do it again." Arthur: "You didn't even ask him to keep my name." Merlin: "He kept it anyway."
  - **Then p24 as it stands**, with Kay's lines kept.
  - **The mother:** either cut "his mother" from p17, or have Merlin give one unnamed line ("Your mother was the queen. She's alive, and that is a story for another day").

**7. pp 24, 32, 40, 45, 46: Merlin speaks in maxims, and the theme is said aloud.**
Merlin's ledger voice is "dry, quick, impatient, funny; asks questions he already knows the answer to". On the page he mostly hands down verdicts. These lines would work in any wise mentor's mouth, which is the aphorism failure:
- p24: "The sword didn't choose a squire, Arthur. It chose a king who hasn't happened yet. Go and happen."
- p32: "A king who stops looking is a dangerous thing."
- p40: "That sword was made to make you king. It was never going to make you good. That part is your job."
- p45 Arthur: "So the thing that keeps you safe is worth more than the thing that wins." Merlin: "Now you're thinking like a king." The ledger says of exactly this belief: "No one ever says this aloud."
- p46: "I could probably beat you with this sword. But I wouldn't have earned it." The reader can't tell why *this* sword makes the fight unearned; the scabbard's magic is the unstated reason.

**Reader harm:** the scenes stop at a moral instead of a choice. Merlin's want (Arthur alive, then Arthur good) turns into lecturing. Three "fool" scoldings in five pages (40, 41, 45) blur into one note.

**Revisions:**
- **p24:** make Merlin ask instead of pronounce. Merlin: "Did the sword ask whose son you were?" Arthur: "No." Merlin: "Did it ask how many fights you'd won?" Arthur: "No." Merlin: "Then why are you asking?" Arthur: "…Then we fight."
- **p32:** cut the maxim. Keep "Because you're looking at what it cost. Good."
- **p40:** let Arthur convict himself. Merlin: "Who told you not to come?" "Kay." "Who offered to let you walk home?" "Pellinore." "And who was too proud to take it?" Arthur, looking at the broken halves: "Me."
- **p45:** cut Arthur's summary. Let him buckle the scabbard on before he sheathes the sword, and give Merlin one dry, in-character line: "Hm. You may live to be old after all."
- **p46:** name the reason. "With this scabbard on, I can't bleed. That's not a fight, that's cheating. Come to Camelot instead…"

**8. pp 25–27: the chronicle on p26 interrupts the battle sequence.**
- **Failure:** p25 ends with Arthur pulling on his mail before dawn. p27 is that dawn. The chronicle sits between arming and the charge, mid-sequence, against the Mode 4 rule ("never between a turn and its consequence").
- **Reader harm:** the tension of the night before the battle is drained by a museum page at the moment the reader most wants to turn to the battle.
- **Revision:** swap pp 25 and 26. The chronicle follows p24's decision ("Then we fight") and the end of the hall scene. p25's night-before page then runs straight into p27's dawn. Reword the Question to fit the new position: "Was there a real King Arthur? Did he really fight wars?"

**9. pp 26, 34, 55: three chronicle claims are false or stated more firmly than the evidence allows.**
- **p26:** "He names one British leader, Ambrosius." Gildas names several British rulers, including five kings he denounces by name. The sentence also implies Ambrosius led at Badon, which Gildas does not say. Revise to: "He names a British war-leader, Ambrosius, who led the fight back against the Saxons. He never names Arthur."
- **p34:** "Malory, writing in the 1400s, imagined steel plate from head to toe." Malory barely describes armour. Revise to: "Malory lived in the 1400s, among knights in steel plate, and that is how his readers pictured Arthur."
- **p55:** "made around 1290, about seven hundred years after Arthur." From about 500 to 1290 is nearly eight hundred years. "After Arthur" also assumes the man existed, against p26's "Nobody knows." Revise to: "about eight hundred years after a real Arthur could have lived."

**10. pp 27–30: the battle is won by a glow nobody set up, and the people's army never matters.**
- **Failure:** the townspeople who chose Arthur (p20), whose deaths he dreads (p25), and who make up most of his line (p27) do nothing in the battle. It is decided on p30 when the sword "blazed like thirty torches". The sword has shown no such power before, and Arthur chooses nothing. He is rescued by Kay and saved by a special effect.
- **Reader repair:** "Why did they win?" The reader's honest answer is "magic", and the people's sacrifice set up on p25 turns into decoration.
- **Revision:** let the rescue lead to the right choice. On p29, Kay drags Arthur back across the stream *to the line*. On p30, Arthur on foot among the smiths and farmers, shoulder to shoulder, as Lot's riders charge. The spear line holds. Only then, as Arthur raises the sword *in the line rather than ahead of it*, does the blade blaze. Lot's horses rear and his army breaks. If the glow stays, seed it once earlier: on p11 or p19, the blade flares when the people roar.

**11. pp 33–34: the armour chronicle answers a question nobody asked.**
- **Failure:** "Did knights in King Arthur's time really look like the ones in this story?" Nothing on pp 31–33 makes armour stand out, so the page reads as a lesson.
- **Revision:** p33 is about songs making Arthur bigger than he was, so put the seed there. In Panel 2's painted hanging, show the "boy king" in gleaming full plate armour that Arthur has never worn, with Arthur grinning at it. Reword the Question to: "Why does the picture show armour Arthur never wore? What did knights really look like?" Open FIELD 1 from that picture: "The songs made Arthur bigger. So did the pictures."

**12. pp 42–44: the Lady, the arm, and the boat do not add up.**
- **Failure:** p42 shows an arm in white silk in the middle of the lake. p43 shows a woman in white walking across the lake to the shore and making the bargain face to face. p44 shows Arthur in a boat nobody mentioned, in the middle of the lake, taking the sword from a white-sleeved hand, which then sinks.
- **Reader repair:** was the arm hers? Are there two of them? Where did the boat come from?
- **Revision:** make it explicit. On p43, draw a small boat pulled up in the reeds and keep the arm visible behind the Lady. Give her the instruction: "The sword is not mine to carry. Take the boat, and take it from the hand." On p44, show her standing on the water at a distance, watching, while the hand gives up the sword. Alternatively, make the arm hers and drop the boat entirely.

**13. p51: Lot arrives with no reason given, and two of his lines don't fit.**
- **Failure:** Lot, who fled swearing "This isn't over", turns up under a flag of truce with no stated invitation or motive. He says "I'm a king of Orkney", where "the King of Orkney" is meant. He also says "Keep your cartwheel", which is Kay's private joke from p49, a scene Lot was not in.
- **Reader repair:** "Why is Lot here?"
- **Revision:** Lot opens with his motive: "You sent for me under a white flag, boy. I came to see this wonder of yours with my own eyes." Change "a king" to "the King". Change "cartwheel" to his own word, such as "Keep your tabletop, boy."

**14. pp 54–55: the ending gives the protagonist nothing, its warning comes from nowhere, and the last chronicle plants a wrong idea.**
- **Failure:** the final story beat is Merlin's "I won't always be beside you, Arthur." Nothing in 53 pages prepares it, though the ledger makes it Merlin's secret want, and Arthur has no reply. The volume ends with its hero silent. Then p55's last field, "Next came Lancelot", directly follows p54's caption about a knight who "had not even been born". Readers will take Lancelot to be the empty-seat knight, and a later volume would have to undo that.
- **Revision:**
  - Seed Merlin's foreknowledge once, e.g. on p32, after "Yes. Remember that.", add "I may not always be there to remind you."
  - On p54, give Arthur a reply that carries his arc. Arthur: "Then who will tell me when I'm being a fool?" Merlin, nodding at Kay's empty chair: "He's had years of practice."
  - In p55, either cut FIELD 3 or make it unambiguous ("The storytellers weren't finished. They had a great many more knights to add."), so it does not name a figure the reader will pin on the empty seat.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

- **Five-word audit:** all 19 balloons pass. Each is a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before, with one referent. "Father!" (p16) works as embarrassment given "red in the face". "Sleep, Pellinore." (p39) works because p40 confirms Merlin at once, but see the p39 note below.
- **Mode balance:** 65% dramatic, 16% prose, 18% spectacle over 49 story pages. That is light on prose and heavy on spectacle. p44 (Panel 2 plus caption) or a reworked p54 could become illustrated prose without loss.
- **p23:** "Says a wizard who reads dreams!" means nothing on the page. A callback such as "Says the wizard who does tricks with swords!" (p8) would land.
- **p39:** Merlin's ring has never been shown before, so his hand alone may not identify him. Include the staff tip or the edge of the forked beard. One prose clause on p41 saying how Merlin got there ("Merlin had followed him from the gate") would close a small gap.
- **p43:** Merlin, who just said "be very polite", says nothing as Arthur promises an unknown gift. A wince or a grip on Arthur's arm in Panel 3 would plant the debt more firmly.
- **p18:** the label "1136: a great king" is not explained by either field. The sword-in-stone episode survives in the prose redaction of Robert de Boron's *Merlin*, not the fragmentary verse, so "a poem called Merlin" is slightly overcertain. "A story called Merlin, by a French writer" is safer.
- **p47:** the French form was "Escalibor". "Excalibur" is the later English form, so "turned that into Excalibur" compresses two steps. This is acceptable for the register.
- **p48:** "Pentecost" may need nothing, but it is the only church-calendar word in the book.
- **p1:** confirm that `TITLE CARD` is treated as rendered text by `verify.py` and the page prompt. It is not in the script's own list of rendering labels.
- **p51:** Lot's third "This isn't over" is fine as a verbal tic, but it is the third time.
- **Ledger housekeeping:** the ledger puts the sword breaking on p37 and Excalibur from p43. The script has them on p38 and p44.
- **What to protect in revision:** pp 4, 9, 12–14, 29, 33, 35–37, 40 Panel 2, 49, and 52. Do not let revision sand down Kay's voice or Lot's.
