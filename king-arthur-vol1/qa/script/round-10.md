# Script gate, round 10: *King Arthur, Volume I: The Sword and the Table*

**Scope.** I read all 59 pages of `books/king-arthur-vol1/08-FULL-SCRIPT.md` cold, in order, before opening any book document. Then I read `qa/script/audit-round-10.txt` (no blockers, 26 short balloons, one warning that the book ends on a chronicle page), `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md` and `00-VOLUME-MANDATE.md`. I audited every page and every page-to-page transition, including pages approved in earlier rounds. I did not look at the source.

## Cold read

A storm, a cliff, an old man carrying a newborn away. Two winters later King Uther, dying, whispers "my Arthur" to Merlin alone and tells his lords only that he has a son. For thirteen years the lords fight over the crown. Meanwhile, on Sir Ector's muddy farm, a boy polishes his brother Kay's helmet and trades insults with him. Ector won't tell Arthur whether he'll ever be a knight: "you'll be whatever you were born to be."

In London, Lot of Orkney fails at the sword in the stone and laughs it off as one of Merlin's tricks. Merlin looks past him at the farm boy. Kay leaves his sword at the inn and the inn is locked. Arthur pulls the stone sword "as easily as a stick out of snow", only because Kay needs a sword. For about a minute Kay claims it. Ector makes him swear on a book, and Kay tells the truth. Arthur pulls it again; Ector kneels and says he isn't his father. "This morning I had a father and a brother. Now all I've got is a sword." Ector wins him back ("When nobody's listening, lad. Always"), the brothers settle it, and Arthur makes Kay his steward.

The lords laugh at him ("the king of the hens"). The people of London roar "WE WILL HAVE ARTHUR," and the sword flares. He is crowned. At the feast Lot throws back Arthur's gifts and threatens him, and Merlin tells the whole hall whose son he is, breaking his promise that Arthur would hear it first. Arthur confronts him alone. Merlin isn't sorry ("I'd do it again"), and Uther "named you." Arthur asks about his mother and is put off till another night. Then he decides to fight.

At Bedegraine he charges alone and is unhorsed. Kay rescues him ("You're very bad at this"), the townspeople's line holds, and the sword blazes. The aftermath is hollow, and Merlin is glad Arthur feels it. Then come the songs at Camelot, the steel he never wore, the one person they leave out ("Nobody in the songs was called Kay"), and Arthur starting to believe them.

Kay says no to the fountain knight. Arthur goes anyway. Pellinore breaks the stone sword and is about to kill him when Merlin puts him to sleep. Merlin's three questions get three one-word answers, the last one "Me." At the lake Arthur promises the Lady "whatever you ask," over Merlin's alarm. He chooses the scabbard first, turns down a fight he can't lose, and invites Pellinore to Camelot. At the gate he hands Kay the broken sword hilt first, the same way he once handed Kay a sword: "Will you tell me no again, next time?" / "Every day."

At the Pentecost feast the knights fight over seats, and Arthur thinks of a round table. Pellinore is the first to sit. Lot, offered Arthur's own chair, walks out. Arthur swears first "because I need it most." Kay offers to fill the empty chair so it won't look like a refusal, and Merlin warns him off. Ector says "Goodnight, lad." Merlin reminds Arthur he still owes the Lady a gift and that someone else will have to call him a fool: "Kay. He's had years of practice."

**Where I stopped.** Only two places, and neither cost me the story:

1. **p2, last caption.** "Not one of his lords had ever heard of a son" comes right after Uther tells them he has one. I reread it once to land on "before that night."
2. **p26, opening.** Arthur's "But Lot's right about one thing" comes straight off Merlin turning down the mother question. For half a beat I looked for what the "But" was arguing with.

I never had to guess who anyone was, why a scene followed, or what someone wanted.

## Is it alive?

Yes. I would want it read to me, and I wanted the next volume when it ended.

- **Movement I (pp 1–7).** The cold open earns its silence, and p2 has real subtext: the dying king whispers a name he won't say aloud. The farmyard page gives the book its voice. Kay's vanity and Arthur's needling ("They'll hear you coming") pass the attribution-strip test at once. Ector looking into the coals instead of at Arthur (p5) is the best kind of subtext: a man dodging a question he has dreaded for fifteen years.

- **Movement II (pp 8–19).** This is the heart of the book, and it works because it is about family, not the sword. Arthur pulls the sword for Kay without reading the letters, which carries the engine without a word of theme. Kay's minute of theft and his sworn confession give him a moral life. The heat on p13 is plain: will Kay lie on the book in front of his father? "For about a minute, I wanted it to be" / "Don't make a song out of it" is the most human exchange in the book, and it plants the song motif.

- **Movement III (pp 20–27).** Lot's want is plain and specific: never to kneel to a nobody. Every line he speaks is a dare. Merlin breaking his promise and refusing to apologise ("I wasn't going to let him finish") is heat between the two people who matter most at that point. The new mother exchange is good subtext, because Merlin deflects with a joke that is also kindness. It is slightly crowded where it sits (see the nonblocking notes).

- **Movement IV (pp 28–36).** The failed charge, Kay's rescue, and the line holding before the blaze put the miracle after the people, which is exactly right. "Because you're looking at what it cost. Good." is Merlin's voice and nobody else's. The songs page (p35) is the cleverest page in the book: the protagonist's appetite visibly goes bad, and the reader sees it before he does.

- **Movement V (pp 37–43).** Arthur's appetite drives this whole movement: he is grinning at the moon, writing his own song on the way to the fountain. Losing the stone sword hurts. Merlin's three questions are the best use of one-word answers I have seen at this gate, because each word is fully determined and each one lands harder than the last.

- **Movement VI (pp 44–50).** This is the closest the book comes to the pageant the mandate warns about. It gets out of it in three ways:
  - Merlin's alarm turns the promise into a hook.
  - The scabbard choice is acted out, not explained.
  - Arthur refuses Pellinore's fight because the scabbard would make it "cheating," which is the book's moral argument done as character.

  The Lady is the thinnest voice in the book: formal and transactional. That suits a figure the ledger says is "the lake's." The gate scene (p49) is a real payoff and the warmest page in the second half.

- **Movement VII (pp 51–59).** It starts with a knife over a chair, not a speech about equality, and Kay's "That's a cartwheel" undercuts the big idea at the right moment. Lot's exit is earned and in character. Pellinore's "I sat down. I'm not ashamed of it" is the plainest and best line in the movement. Kay's new reason for wanting the empty chair ("An empty chair looks like somebody refused you") ties the chair to Lot's exit and to Kay's loyalty in one line. The ending lands on Kay, which is where the book's heart has been all along.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p26, the mother exchange.** It is placed and joined slightly awkwardly.
   - Panel 1 now holds five balloons from three speakers, including Kay's entrance.
   - Arthur's "But Lot's right about one thing" pivots off Merlin's deflection without any reaction to it.
   - It passes the read-aloud test, because "But" reads as Arthur dropping the subject. It still loses a beat of feeling: a boy refused his mother's name shows nothing.

   If the script is ever reopened for another reason, two options:
   - Let Arthur react before Kay comes in, for example: "Another night, then. You keep owing me nights." Then start the decision in a new panel with "Lot's right about one thing, though."
   - Or give the exchange its own panel.

   For the packet, as it stands: stage the Arthur–Merlin exchange high in panel 1 and Kay's two lines low on his own side (left), and protect the balloon lanes. The page is about 98 words.

2. **p2, last caption.** I paused once at "Not one of his lords had ever heard of a son." If reopened, "Until that night, not one of his lords had ever heard of a son" removes the reread.

3. **p35, location.** Panel 1 is an exterior of Camelot and panel 2 is inside the hall. That is an establishing shot of one place, not a second location. Packet note: make the exterior read as the establishing image, not a separate scene band.

4. **p32, caption.** The caption partly repeats the picture. It earns its place by fixing the order ("The line held. And when…"), so the people come before the miracle. Keep it.

5. **Text density, for the packets.**
   - p25 is about 103 words and p26 about 98, both near the ~105 ceiling, so both need protected balloon lanes.
   - p55 has four panels with Kay and Pellinore paired on the right.

   These are packet staging concerns, not script defects.

6. **Geoffrey's date.** "In 1136" on p50 and p59, and the p19 label, are the conventional date. The book's own Sources line says "c. 1136." That is not a factual error. "About 1136" would match the chronicle voice's honesty if the script is ever reopened.
