## Cold read

On a stormy night an old man named Merlin carries a newborn out of a cliff castle. Two winters later King Uther is dying. He whispers "My Arthur" to Merlin alone and tells his lords only that he has a son. None of them has ever heard of a son. For thirteen years the lords fight over the crown. Meanwhile a boy grows up on Sir Ector's muddy farm as the squire and younger brother of Kay, a newly knighted show-off.

They ride to a New Year tournament in London. A sword stands in a stone that says whoever pulls it out is the true-born king. Lot of Orkney fails and laughs it off as Merlin's trick. Merlin looks straight at the farm boy. Kay leaves his sword at the inn, and Arthur takes the one from the stone because "Kay needs a sword." Kay claims the crown for himself, then tells the truth when Ector makes him swear on a book. Ector kneels and confesses. Arthur breaks: "This morning I had a father and a brother. Now all I've got is a sword." Then they rebuild it: "Can I still call you Father?" / "When nobody's listening, lad. Always." Kay becomes steward.

The lords mock Arthur. The people of London roar for him, and he is crowned. Merlin promises to tell him privately who his father was, then shouts it at Lot in the feast hall. Arthur is furious about the broken promise, and Merlin is not sorry.

Arthur goes to war, charges alone and is unhorsed in a stream. Kay rescues him. The townspeople's spear line holds and the sword blazes. Arthur is shaken by what winning cost, then by spring has believed the songs about himself. Kay forbids him to fight a knight at a forest fountain. Arthur sneaks out anyway, loses to Pellinore, and the sword from the stone breaks. Merlin puts Pellinore to sleep, then questions Arthur until he says "Me."

At a misty lake Arthur promises the Lady anything she asks in return for Excalibur. He chooses the scabbard over the sword, and on the way home turns down a fight he can't lose and invites Pellinore to Camelot. At the gate he hands Kay the broken halves: "You were right." / "Every day."

The knights brawl over seats, so Arthur builds a round table. Lot comes under a white flag, is offered Arthur's own chair, and walks out rather than sit level with a steward and "an old boar." Arthur kneels and swears first, "because I need it most." Kay reaches for the one empty, nameless chair and Merlin warns him off. The book ends on Merlin's "Kay. He's had years of practice" and the empty seat in moonlight. A closing chronicle page tells how storytellers built the legend.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a book I would want read to me.

- **Movement I (pp 1–7):** It opens on an image and a secret. Ector's dodge on p5 ("It's the only one I've got. Go to bed.") is real subtext: he is evading, and the third panel shows what it costs him. Arthur's appetite ("A real one?") is on the page from the start.
- **Movement II (pp 8–19):** The best-built stretch of the book. Kay grabs the crown on p12 and says on p13 "Arthur gave it to me," which is true but holds back what matters. p15 gives the grief room, and p16–17 give the repair room. "Don't make a song out of it" quietly sets up the songs on p35. Strip the names off these pages and every speaker is still clear: Kay's vanity, Ector's few plain words, Arthur's bluntness.
- **Movement III (pp 20–27):** The stakes are concrete: a boy being publicly called nobody's son. Lot is a bully you love to hate ("The king of the hens!"). On p25 there is a real argument with a real want on each side: Arthur wants an apology, and Merlin refuses to give one. "He named you" lands.
- **Movement IV (pp 28–36):** Arthur's charge comes from character, not plot. Kay's rescue is acted out, not reported, and is the funniest and warmest moment yet ("You're very bad at this"). The aftermath on pp 33–34 is honest. Then p35 quietly undoes the lesson, which is the strongest page-turn in the book.
- **Movements V–VI (pp 37–50):** Pride, punishment and growth, acted out rather than explained. Merlin's questions on p42 ("Kay." / "Pellinore." / "Me.") are excellent. The scabbard choice on p47, and its echo on p48 ("That's cheating"), show the change with no moral spoken aloud. p49 pays off the brothers.
- **Movement VII (pp 51–58):** The weakest heat in the book is pp 52–54, which build the table and seat the knights. The heat comes back with Lot on p55, the kneeling oath on p56, and Kay on p57. The ending is warm, funny and open.

**Appetite** runs from p4 to p58. Arthur is never serene before p44, and is not serene after it either.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p25, Arthur's mother.** A boy who just said "Then I'm not anybody's son" learns his father's name and never asks about his mother. The mandate cuts the conception story on purpose, but the silence reads as something left out rather than something kept secret. One exchange would turn it into a secret, for example:
   - Arthur: "And my mother?"
   - Merlin: "That's another night's story, Arthur. One father is enough for tonight."

   The question becomes something the reader wonders about without committing the book to anything.
2. **p3, Prose Field 2.** "A boy grew up who had never heard of King Uther at all" is hard to believe after thirteen years of war over Uther's crown. A suggested alternative with stronger irony: "a boy grew up who had never once wondered about King Uther's lost son."
3. **p57, Kay's motive.** Kay leaving a chair that already carries his name reads as officiousness only if the reader supplies the reason. Half a line would fix it, for example "An empty chair at the king's table looks like somebody refused him. I'll sit in it, then."
4. **p19, Chronicle Field 1.** The 38-word sentence ("The oldest stories about Arthur, and even a book of 1136 that … have battles…") puts 25 words between its subject and its verb. Split it in two for reading aloud.
5. **pp 52–54, heat.** This is the book's lowest-heat run. It is acceptable as breathing room before Lot's walkout. Packets should make sure p52's cartwheel line and Arthur's grin carry the energy of the page.
