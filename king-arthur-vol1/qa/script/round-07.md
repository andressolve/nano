## Cold read

On a stormy night an old man named Merlin carries a newborn out of a castle. Two winters later King Uther is dying. He whispers "My Arthur" to Merlin, but tells his lords only "I have a son," and dies. For thirteen years the lords fight over the crown. Meanwhile a boy grows up on Sir Ector's muddy farm as Ector's son and squire to Ector's real son, Kay, who is cocky and newly knighted. Ector dodges the boy's question about whether he'll ever be a knight.

The family rides to London for a New Year tournament. A sword in a stone has appeared in a churchyard. King Lot fails to pull it and calls it a Merlin trick, and Merlin looks straight past him at Arthur.

Kay forgets his sword at the inn and the inn is locked. Arthur pulls the stone-sword out only because "Kay needs a sword." Kay claims it. Ector makes him swear on a book, and Kay tells the truth. Arthur does it again in front of Ector, and Ector kneels: "I'm not your father." Arthur says, "This morning I had a father and a brother. Now all I've got is a sword." Ector gives him back the name Father "when nobody's listening." The brothers make peace, and Kay is made steward.

The lords mock "the king of the hens." The people of London storm the churchyard, Arthur is crowned, and Merlin promises to tell him his father's name tonight. Instead Merlin announces it to the whole feast to shut Lot up. Arthur confronts him and gets "I'd do it again" and "He named you." Then Arthur decides to fight, for the people who chose him.

At Bedegraine, Arthur charges alone and is unhorsed. Kay rescues him ("You're very bad at this"), the spear line holds, the sword blazes, and Lot runs. The victory looks like a field of empty saddles. Back at Camelot the songs swell Arthur's head. When Kay says no, Arthur sneaks out to fight Pellinore at the fountain, refuses to yield, and breaks the stone-sword. Merlin saves him and convicts him with three questions ("Me."). The Lady of the Lake gives him Excalibur in exchange for a rash promise of "whatever I ask." He chooses the scabbard over the sword, turns down a fight he can't lose, and invites Pellinore to court. He hands Kay the broken halves: "You were right." / "Every day."

Knights brawl over seats, and Arthur imagines a table with no head. Pellinore arrives a year later. Lot refuses to sit "level with nobody." The knights swear the oath. Kay tries to take the empty seat and Merlin warns him off. Merlin tells Arthur he won't always be there and reminds him of the debt to the Lady. The seat waits for someone not yet born.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a book I'd want read aloud, and it has a heart: Arthur and Kay.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** Quiet but charged. The Uther whisper pays off cleanly. On p5, Ector looking into the coals instead of at Arthur ("It's the only one I've got. Go to bed.") is real subtext: a man protecting a lie he loves. Arthur wants things from the first page ("Can I come? … Please, Father.").

**Movement II (pp 8–19).** The best stretch of the book. Arthur takes the sword because "Kay needs a sword," which is exactly the right reason. Kay's grab at the crown, the long pause before "Arthur gave it to me," and Ector kneeling are each felt rather than explained. "Now all I've got is a sword" earns p16's answer.
- Want-from is live on every page. On p13 Ector wants the truth out of Kay. On p16 Arthur wants permission to keep his father.
- The attribution-strip test passes: Kay's "Don't make a song out of it" couldn't come from anyone else.

**Movement III (pp 20–27).** Lot is a pleasure to hate ("The king of the hens!"). The Merlin confrontation on p25 has real heat. Arthur wants an apology, Merlin refuses it, and "He named you" turns anger into grief in one panel. Page 26 turns on a question rather than a speech, and Kay's "which makes it less terrible" keeps it human.

**Movement IV (pp 28–36).** The charge, the fall and Kay's rescue are enacted on the page, not reported. Page 33 lets winning look like loss. On p34, "Because you're looking at what it cost. Good." sits close to aphorism. It survives the attribution-strip test because it is Merlin's curt, approving voice, and it answers a direct question. On p35 the songs leave Kay out, a quiet knife that sets up the next movement.

**Movement V (pp 37–43).** Arthur's appetite drives the whole movement, and it is the right appetite: he wants the song. Pellinore's "Yield, boy. Say the word, and you walk home" makes the pride legible. Merlin's catechism on p42 lets Arthur convict himself, which is far better than a moral. The prose line "He had nearly died for a song" is the best sentence in the book.

**Movement VI (pp 44–50).** The wonder pages are clear and earned. Arthur's promise to the Lady is impulsive, and that is in character. On p47 he buckles on the scabbard without a word of moral. On p48 he refuses the fight as "cheating," a face-saving reason that is also a true one, which is good subtext. Page 49, with the broken sword handed over hilt-first as on p12, is the emotional peak of the volume.

**Movement VII (pp 51–59).** The table is born from a fight, not a pageant. Kay's lines ("That's a cartwheel." "I'll keep my own, then.") stop the solemnity from setting in. Lot's walkout keeps an enemy alive. "I'll swear first, because I need it most" gathers up Arthur's whole arc in eight words.

**On the mandate's specific failure, "the pageant":** Arthur is never serene. He is hungry, rash and funny all the way to p44 and beyond.

**Is it any good?** Yes. The boys will argue about Kay, quote Lot, and want the next volume.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p58: Merlin's closing reminder flattens the best button.** "Kay. He's had years of practice." lands the scene. Then "And remember, you still owe the Lady of the Lake a gift." follows in the same balloon run as a second "remember," so it reads like a checklist. Suggestions:
   - Give it its own beat: Arthur's hand goes to the red scabbard, and Merlin says the Lady line to that gesture.
   - Or move the Lady line before the "fool" exchange, so Kay gets the last word.

2. **p55→p56: Arthur gets no reaction to Lot's refusal.** He said what he wanted ("I'd rather have you at this table than across a valley") and was turned down, then the oath begins. One glance at the empty doorway in p56 panel 1 would cost nothing and would keep Lot alive as a threat into Volume II.

3. **p25 and p55 are at the dramatic ceiling.** They run about 102 and 100 words, in 8 and 6 balloons. The packets must reserve large balloon lanes. Do not trim causal words.

4. **p14 is labelled spectacle but its turn is spoken.** "I'm not your father" is a line of dialogue, with four balloons across two panels. The kneeling image does carry it, and this was decided in earlier rounds. The packet should still treat the balloon lanes as primary.

5. **p50 overstates the "argument."** "Storytellers have argued for eight hundred years about whether they were one sword or two" claims a debate that the medieval texts don't record. They simply differ, and Field 2 already says this truthfully. Suggest: "Storytellers have told it both ways for eight hundred years."

6. **p59 invites a guess about the empty seat.** Naming "a knight named Lancelot," the only knight named on that page, directly after p58's "The knight it was waiting for had not even been born" invites readers to decide the seat is Lancelot's. If Volume II will say otherwise, consider leading Field 2 with Camelot and the Grail and mentioning Lancelot without any link to a seat.

7. **p59: the Winchester label doesn't match the picture.** The table is drawn as it looks today, with the Tudor rose and the painted king from 1522, but labelled "Winchester, about 1290." Suggested label (five words): "Winchester: made 1290, painted 1522."

8. **p25: Arthur never asks about his mother.** Cutting the conception story is the mandate's choice and is correct. Still, a boy who has just learned whose son he is would likely ask, and so would the reader. One Merlin line, if the owner wants it, would close the question without any scandal.

9. **p38→p40: the lances appear from nowhere.** Arthur rides out with no lance, then jousts with one. A packet-level fix: a lance on his saddle on p38, or lances leaning by Pellinore's pavilion on p39.

10. **p12: Kay's public shout has no consequence.** He shouts "I'm the king of Britain!" across a crowded tournament field, and nothing comes of it. A packet-level fix: stage the shout so it plainly reaches Ector rather than the stands.
