## 2. Cold read

I read the script cold, start to finish. In plain words:

A wizard named Merlin carries a newborn out of a castle in a storm. Two years later King Uther is dying. He whispers "my Arthur" to Merlin alone, and tells his lords only "I have a son." Nobody ever finds the son. For thirteen years the lords fight over the crown. Meanwhile a boy grows up on Sir Ector's muddy farm as Ector's son, squire to his loud new-knight brother Kay. At the forge one night Ector dodges Arthur's question about whether he'll ever be a knight, and we know Ector is hiding something.

They go to London for a New Year tournament. A sword in a stone has appeared in a churchyard, and the gold letters on the stone say whoever pulls it out is king. King Lot of Orkney fails and laughs. Merlin looks straight at Arthur. Kay leaves his sword at the inn, the inn is locked, and Arthur pulls the stone sword out "like a stick out of snow" just to help his brother. Kay claims it. Ector makes him swear on a book, and Kay tells the truth. Arthur pulls the sword again, and Ector kneels and says "I'm not your father." Arthur has won a kingdom and lost his family in one breath. Ector gives him back the right to say "Father" when nobody's listening and asks that Kay be made steward. The brothers settle Kay's lie.

The lords refuse a farm boy. The people of London roar "WE WILL HAVE ARTHUR!" He is crowned, and Merlin promises to tell him who his father was that night. Then Lot humiliates him at the feast and Merlin blurts out the secret to the whole hall. Arthur confronts Merlin ("I'd do it again" / "He named you") and decides to fight.

In his first battle Arthur charges alone. Kay saves him, the line of ordinary people holds, and the sword blazes. Arthur wins, and the field doesn't look like winning. Then the songs make him a hero in shining armour, and he starts believing them. Kay tells him no. He sneaks out anyway, loses to Pellinore, and the stone sword snaps. Merlin saves him and walks him, by questions, to "Me." At the lake Arthur promises the Lady "whatever you ask" for Excalibur. He chooses the scabbard over the sword, refuses a fight he can't lose, and invites Pellinore to Camelot. He hands Kay the broken sword: "You were right." / "Every day."

A brawl over seats leads to the Round Table. Pellinore comes, Lot walks out, the knights swear, and one seat stays empty. Merlin warns he won't always be there and reminds Arthur of his debt to the Lady.

**Where I stopped:**
- p25. Merlin justifies his public outburst with "Lot was calling you nobody's son… I wasn't going to let that stand." For a beat I remembered that on p20 Lot said exactly that ("Whose son? Nobody knows!") and Merlin stood silent in the doorway. I read on without rereading, and p26 gives a reason for the earlier silence (the people chose him without knowing his father). Not a blocker; see the observations.
- p49. Both p43 and p49 are dawn, so Arthur has been gone a whole day longer than Kay's "up all night" suggests. I noticed it; I don't think a reader would.
- p53. The staging shows Pellinore "looking in at the half-built table" on the eve of the Pentecost when it is finished and sat at. The picture will contradict the prose.

I never had to reread to understand who someone was, what they wanted, or why the next scene followed.

## 3. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a book I would want read to me, and the boys will argue about Kay.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** The p1 storm image and the p2 whisper give the book a secret before it gives it a plot. That is the right order. On p5 the forge scene is the first real subtext: Ector answering a different question ("you'll be whatever you were born to be") and Arthur calling it out ("That isn't an answer"). On p4 Kay and Arthur have distinct voices from their first exchange; strip the names and you can still tell who's talking. The p7 chronicle is earned by the crumbling walls on p6.

**Movement II (pp 8–19).** This is the heart of the book, and it works. Arthur takes the sword out of loyalty, not ambition ("Kay needs a sword"), which makes the kneeling on p14 land like a blow. pp 15–17 give the grief its full length, and the want-from is sharp on every page:
- p15: Arthur wants Ector to say it isn't true.
- p16: Ector wants Arthur to still be his, and to protect Kay.
- p17: Kay wants not to have to say sorry, and Arthur wants his brother back without making him crawl.

"For about a minute, I wanted it to be" / "Don't make a song out of it" is a real person speaking. "Don't make a song out of it" also quietly sets up the songs on p35.

**Movement III (pp 20–27).** The heat is strong. "The king of the hens!" is cruel, funny, and Lot's alone. The people's roar on p21 is a true spectacle page that carries a turn. p25 is the best confrontation in the book. Arthur wants an apology. Merlin refuses it ("I'd do it again"), then gives him the one thing he actually needed ("He named you"). That is subtext doing the work: the anger turns into "Did he ever see me?" without anyone naming the change. On p26, Kay's "It's terrible… But I'll be next to him, which makes it less terrible" is love disguised as an insult, which is Kay's whole character.

**Movement IV (pp 28–36).** The battle is enacted, not reported. The p30 charge, the fall in the stream and Kay's rescue are all on panel, and the blaze on p32 comes after the people's line holds, so the magic rewards the right thing. The aftermath runs across pp 33–34. Then p35 turns the protagonist's pride into appetite: he laughs at the songs, then listens, then believes them, and "Nobody in the songs was called Kay" is quietly devastating.

**Movement V (pp 37–43).** Kay's p37 refusal is the funniest page in the book ("Kings in the songs don't have stewards"), and it plants the no Arthur will defy. The p38 prose catches a boy's joy at getting away with something. "Kings don't yield" on p41 is pride at its peak, and Merlin's catechism on p42 ("Kay." / "Pellinore." / "Me.") convicts him without a lecture. Arthur is never serene. He is hungry, wrong, and paying for it.

**Movement VI (pp 44–50).** The lake is wonder, and the bargain has a hook in it: Merlin's alarm on p45 makes "whatever you ask" frightening. The scabbard choice on p47 is enacted in silence, and on p48 it pays off as character, not doctrine ("That isn't a fight. That's cheating"). The new p49 is right. It closes the Kay thread the book opened on p37, the hilt-first handoff echoes p12 without saying so, and "Every day" is a good last line for a brother.

**Movement VII (pp 51–59).** This is the coolest movement, and I checked it hardest against the "pageant" failure the volume mandate warns about. It mostly escapes:
- p51 has a knife drawn and a king slamming the table, so it is hot.
- p52 is an idea scene with Kay heckling ("That's a cartwheel").
- p55 has real heat. Lot wants Arthur humiliated; Arthur wants Lot inside the circle. Pellinore's "I sat down. I'm not ashamed of it" turns Lot's dare back on him.
- p56's "I'll swear first, because I need it most" earns the oath through everything Arthur got wrong.
- p58 closes the protagonist's arc in a single exchange. The boy who said "Kings don't yield" now asks "Then who'll tell me when I'm being a fool?" and gets "Kay." That is the book's moral landing, and it is enacted.

The temperature sags a little at p57 (see the observations), but the movement is alive.

**The chronicle pages** are a real addition, not a lecture. Each one answers a question the story pages put in the reader's head: the walls, "is the stone real?", "was Arthur real?", the plate armour in the hanging, two swords, and who made all this up. Each is honest about legend versus history ("Almost certainly not", "Nobody knows", "Historians are still arguing", "This book chose two swords").

## 4. Mandatory findings

NONE.

What I checked:
- **Presence tests** on every dramatic page. No logistics register and no aphorism register. Every voice survives the attribution-strip test.
- **Introductions.** All eight named characters are introduced before the plot depends on them. Uther, Ector, Lot, Pellinore and the Lady are named on first appearance. Merlin is named in the p1 caption, and his "is Merlin" identity is re-anchored on p8 through Lot's line.
- **Causal chain.** No step is missing. The gifts on p23 and the white flag on p55 are stated in dialogue at the point of need.
- **Five-word audit.** All 24 short balloons are either direct answers to a complete question in the same exchange or exclamations with a single visible referent. None carries plot logic on its own.
- **Page architecture.** Every page has one mode, one dominant panel, one turn and one location, and every text inventory is within budget.
- **Chronicle rules.** Six pages, 10% of the book, never two in a row, each at a movement break. With all six removed, the story still passes the paraphrase test. None spoils a later turn, none carries a causal step, and no character explains history.
- **Facts.** Every chronicle claim I checked is accurate or properly hedged: Roman Britain and 410, Gildas c. 540 naming Ambrosius and Badon but not Arthur, the *Historia Brittonum*'s twelve battles c. 830, de Boron's stone c. 1200, Caledfwlch / Caliburnus / Escalibor, Malory using both sword versions, Wace 1155, Chrétien's Camelot, Lancelot and the Grail (correctly called a dish), Malory in prison c. 1470, and the Winchester table c. 1290.
- **Mode balance** over the 53 story pages is 66 / 17 / 17. That is within tolerance, and nine prose pages is healthy.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

None of these blocks approval. Each is cheap at the script gate if the owner chooses to take it. **Any edit reopens the gate for a fresh critic.**

1. **p53, staging (continuity).** "Pellinore… looking in at the half-built table" contradicts the prose ("most of a year… three hundred days… On the eve of the next Pentecost") and the page-turn line ("the table is finished"). The image will show an unfinished table the night before it is sat at. **Fix:** "looking in at the finished table, bare and waiting."

2. **p49, time (continuity).** p43 is dawn as they leave the forest, p48 is "day" at the fountain, and p49 is dawn again at the gate. Kay has been waiting more than a day, not one night. Also, the guard could not know where Arthur went. **Fix:** make p49 "dusk" and give Kay "The guard says you rode out alone at midnight. He didn't have to tell me where." That keeps Kay sardonic and fixes the inference.

3. **p20 against p25 (motive).** Merlin's stated reason on p25 ("I wasn't going to let that stand") sits awkwardly with his silence on p20, when Lot says the same thing. **Optional fix:** on p25, Merlin adds "In the churchyard I let the people choose you without a father. At your own table, I wouldn't let Lot take one from you."

4. **p55, voice and heat.** Lot insults Kay by name ("a farm boy's steward") and Kay, who "always has the last word", says nothing. Pellinore's answer is good and should stay. A one-line Kay jab before it would add heat and keep his voice consistent, e.g. "This steward knocked you off a battlefield."

5. **p57, temperature.** This is the quietest dramatic page in the book. The empty-seat mystery hooks, but nobody wants anything from anybody. Consider letting Kay want the seat, half-joking: "Then I'll sit in it. Somebody should." That makes Merlin's warning land on a person rather than on the table.

6. **p56, oath wording.** "I'll swear first" followed by "We swear…" switches person mid-oath. **Fix:** "I swear never to be cruel…", then the knights' "WE SWEAR!" answers him.

7. **p19, honesty of register.** "the history of 1136 that first made him a great king" calls Geoffrey's mostly invented book "the history." **Fix:** "a book of 1136 that called itself a history, and first made him a great king."

8. **p45, panel 3.** Three speakers (Arthur, Merlin, the Lady) share one small panel. That is a balloon-ownership risk at the page gate. The packet should stage Arthur and the Lady left and right with Merlin behind Arthur on his side, or carry the Lady's last line in its own beat.

9. **p59, ending on a chronicle page.** This is the audit's warning, and I confirm the story's last turn lands before it: the arc closes on p58 with "Then who'll tell me when I'm being a fool?" / "Kay." The p59 question, though, is the weakest-earned of the six, because the empty seat raises "who is it for?", not "who made this up?" It reads acceptably as an afterword. If the owner wants the book's final image to be the story's, the alternative is to move p59 before p51, at the VI/VII break, and merge it with p50. I don't recommend that; the snowball works as a send-off.

10. **p14, spectacle with three balloons (23 words).** The round 3 decision stands, and the kneeling image carries the turn. The packet must protect both balloon lanes.

11. **p1, page-turn line.** It says "a king's baby", but the page never says king; p2 does. This is harmless, but the line should be true of its own page. Suggest "someone has carried a baby away in the night, and the man who did it isn't telling."
