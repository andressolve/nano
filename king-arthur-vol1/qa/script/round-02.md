## Cold read

In a storm, an old man named Merlin carries a newborn away from a castle. Two winters later King Uther is dying. He whispers to Merlin to ask whether his son is safe, then tells his lords, out loud, "My son, Arthur, will be king after me." The lords have never heard of a son, nobody finds him, and they fight over the crown for thirteen years. On a muddy farm, Sir Ector's younger boy Arthur polishes his big brother Kay's helmet and teases him. Kay has just been knighted. A tournament is called in London. At night by the forge, Arthur asks whether he'll ever be a knight. Ector looks into the coals and says, "you'll be whatever you were born to be." They ride to London. A chronicle page explains the Roman walls and the end of Roman Britain.

In a churchyard there is a sword in a stone, with gold letters promising the crown to whoever pulls it out. King Lot fails and calls it Merlin's trick. Merlin, in the doorway, looks at Arthur. Kay forgets his sword. The inn is locked, so Arthur pulls the stone sword out "as easily as a stick out of snow." Kay claims it for a minute, then tells the truth when Ector makes him swear on a book. Arthur pulls it again, and Ector kneels and says he isn't Arthur's father. The brothers settle the lie and Arthur promises to make Kay his steward. He spends a night alone in the church. A chronicle page explains that the stone first appears around 1200.

The lords mock him ("the king of the hens"), the people of London roar for him, and the sword flares. He is crowned, and Merlin promises to tell him "Tonight" who his father was. At the feast Lot throws back Arthur's gifts. Merlin then tells the whole hall that Arthur is Uther's son, and Arthur confronts him afterwards ("I'd do it again"; "He named you"). Arthur is afraid he can't fight, Kay jokes, Merlin asks him questions, and Arthur decides: "Then we fight." A chronicle page covers Gildas, Badon, and whether Arthur was real.

At Bedegraine Arthur charges alone, falls, and is pulled out by Kay. The townspeople's spear line holds and the sword blazes. Afterwards Arthur is shaken among the wounded, and Merlin tells him to remember how close it was. At Camelot the songs leave out Kay, and Arthur begins to believe them. A chronicle page covers armour. Arthur sneaks out to fight the fountain knight, Pellinore, breaks the stone sword, and is saved only because Merlin puts Pellinore to sleep. Merlin's questions make Arthur say "Me." At the lake an arm holds up Excalibur. Arthur promises the Lady "whatever you ask" and takes the sword. Merlin tells him the scabbard matters more than the sword. On the way home Arthur turns down a fight with Pellinore and invites him to court. A chronicle page covers the names of the sword. The knights fight over seats, Arthur invents a round table, Pellinore sits first, Lot walks out, the knights swear their oath, and one seat stays empty. Merlin says he won't always be there: "Kay. He's had years of practice." A last chronicle page shows the legend growing like a snowball.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p2 → p4.** Uther says "Arthur" to his lords, and two pages later a farm boy is called Arthur. I kept waiting for some lord to make the connection. None ever does.
- **p19.** Lot asks "Whose son? Nobody knows!" But Lot heard Uther name the heir "Arthur," and a boy called Arthur has just pulled the sword.
- **p21 → p23.** Merlin says the name is so dangerous that lords would cut Arthur's throat for it. A few hours later he shouts it to all of them. I reread p21 to check I hadn't misread "Not here."
- **p25.** "Did the sword ask whose son you were?" The stone says "TRUE-BORN KING," so yes, it did. And "Then why are you asking?" Arthur hadn't asked anything. I reread the page.
- **p44.** Merlin says "That is the Lady of the Lake" while the arm is out on the water. Then a woman walks toward them and the arm is still there. Whose arm is it?
- **p44 → p55.** I kept waiting for someone to bring up the promise Merlin grabbed Arthur's arm to stop. Nobody does.
- **p35.** "Malory lived in the 1400s." Who is Malory? Nobody tells me until p48.
- **p16 → end.** Ector says "You'll always have us, lad, if you want us," and Arthur never answers him. Ector sits at the feast on p22 and then disappears from the book.

## Is it alive?

Yes, mostly, and in places it is very good. This is a real round-2 script: the round-1 repairs work and the voice holds.

**Movement I (pp 1–7): alive.** The p4 banter ("They'll hear you coming") sets up the brothers in two lines. p5 is the best quiet page in the book. Ector looks into the coals, "That isn't an answer," "It's the only one I've got," and then he sits there unhappy in panel 3. That is subtext doing its job. The one flaw is p2, which hands the lords a name the rest of the book needs them not to have (Finding 1).

**Movement II (pp 8–18): the strongest stretch.** Kay's minute of wanting the crown, the long pause before "Arthur gave it to me," Ector kneeling with "I'm not your father," "Now all I've got is a sword," and Kay's "Don't make a song out of it" (which also sets up the songs later). Every page has a want and a cost. I felt it. What's missing is Arthur's answer to Ector (Finding 3).

**Movement III (pp 19–26): hot at the start, wobbly in the middle.** "The king of the hens" and the people's roar are great. p24 is now the emotional centre of the book ("Did he ever see me?" / "Once. The night you were born. He named you."). But Merlin's reasons stop adding up between p19, p21 and p23 (Finding 2). And p25, the page where Arthur decides to go to war, rests on a Merlin quiz that doesn't hold together. Kay's two jokes are the only live lines on that page (Finding 4).

**Movement IV (pp 27–35): alive.** The night before the battle in prose, the lone charge, Kay's "Somebody had to. You're very bad at this," the line holding, and the noon field that "doesn't look like winning." p34's "Nobody in the songs was called Kay" is the best sentence of narration in the book. Arthur keeps his appetite throughout.

**Movement V (pp 36–42): the best movement.** "Kings in the songs don't have stewards." Arthur's joy riding out alone at night, the sword snapping, "He's snoring," and Merlin's three questions ending in "Me." That last exchange is enacted, funny and painful at once. I'd read it to anyone.

**Movement VI (pp 43–48): beautiful, and one thread is dropped.** The scabbard test on p46 is excellent, and "Hm. You may live to be old after all" is pure Merlin. But the promise to the Lady, which is the most dangerous thing Arthur does in the movement, goes unremarked by everyone, including Merlin, who saw it coming (Finding 6). p47's "That's cheating" is slightly neat, but it is Arthur's voice and his choice, so it passes.

**Movement VII (pp 49–56): warm, funny, and a strong ending.** "That's a cartwheel." "The same as me. I sat down. I'm not ashamed of it." "I'll swear first, because I need it most." "Kay. He's had years of practice." The ending works on feeling. Its weaknesses are that Lot's dialogue repeats ("This isn't over" for the third time) and that Ector has vanished from the family the book is ending on.

**Attribution-strip test:** passes. Kay (sarcastic, always has the last word), Lot (every sentence a dare), Merlin (dry, answers with questions), Pellinore (slow, formal) and Ector (plain, warm) can all be told apart with the names covered. I found no logistics dialogue. The closest thing to an aphorism is p25 panel 3 (Finding 4).

**Heat:** present on almost every dramatic page. The sharpest heat is between the brothers (pp 12–16, 30, 36, 55).

**Appetite:** Arthur is hungry and never serene before p44. The mandate's main risk is avoided.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p2 (and its effects on p3, p15, p19 and p23): Uther names "Arthur" in front of the lords, which breaks every later page that depends on nobody knowing who the boy is.**

- **The failure.** p2 panel 3: Uther, "lifts his head toward the lords," says "My son, Arthur, will be king after me." The caption adds "Not one of his lords had ever heard of a son." So every lord in Britain now knows the heir's name.
- **What the reader has to repair.**
  - p19: Lot asks "Who is he? Whose son? Nobody knows!" about a boy named Arthur who has just pulled a sword that proclaims the "TRUE-BORN KING."
  - p15: Ector, a knight who took a baby from Merlin, says he "never asked" whose son it was, while the lost heir's name is the very name he gave the boy.
  - p23: Merlin's reveal is staged as a shock to the hall.
  - A ten-year-old who noticed the name on p2 has to invent a reason why nobody else did.
- **The ledger agrees with the fix.** Uther's voice is given as a whisper: "My son… Arthur."
- **Prescription.** Move the name into the whisper in panel 2. For example:
  - UTHER: "Is he safe? My son? My Arthur?"
  - MERLIN: "Safe, and far from here…"

  Change panel 3's public line to "I have a son. He will be king after me. He has my blessing."

  The reader now knows the name and the lords don't. That makes p4's "Kay! Arthur!" a jolt of recognition for the reader, keeps Lot's "whose son?" honest, explains Ector's ignorance, makes p23 a true revelation, and matches p24's "He named you." In p23 panel 1, Merlin can add "Some of you heard Uther name his son that night."

**2. pp 19, 21, 23 and 24: Merlin reverses himself with no stated motive.**

- **The failure.**
  - p19: Lot asks in public "Whose son? Nobody knows!" and Merlin, who knows, says nothing.
  - p21: Merlin refuses to say the name in church: "Not here. Half these lords would cut your throat for that name. Tonight, I swear it."
  - p23: Hours later he shouts it to Lot and every lord in the hall.
  - p24: Arthur confronts him ("I heard it from you shouting at Lot"). Merlin defends hiding the baby fifteen years ago ("I'd do it again") but never explains why he broke his own warning tonight.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The reader has to invent Merlin's reason. Was it a mistake? A plan? Did Lot's threat change the danger? The only adult who is supposed to know what he's doing acts against his own stated logic, and the page that confronts him answers a different question.
- **Prescription.**
  - On p21, change Merlin's reason so it doesn't contradict p23. The point is that Arthur should hear it privately first: "Not here, with a church full of ears. You should hear it before they do. Tonight, I swear it." That also makes p23's "before Arthur heard it himself" a broken promise, which gives p24 more heat.
  - In p24 panel 1 or 2, have Arthur raise it ("You told me they'd cut my throat for that name!") and give Merlin his reason in his own voice: "They meant to cut it anyway. Now every lord in that hall knows whose throat it is." If space is tight, split p24's three beats across two panels. Do not cut "I'd do it again" or "He named you."

**3. p16: Arthur never answers Ector's "You'll always have us, lad, if you want us," and Ector then disappears from the book.**

- **The failure.**
  - The book's emotional engine is a boy who "loses the only father he ever had." p15 lands that loss, and p17's prose keeps it going.
  - On p16, Ector makes the one offer that could heal it. Arthur's next words answer only Ector's request about Kay.
  - Ector is seen once more, at the p22 feast, with no line. He is absent from the battle, Camelot, the Table and the ending.
  - p24 has Arthur accuse Merlin of making Ector "raise me on a lie," and nothing follows from that either.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The reader has to decide alone whether Arthur still thinks of Ector as his father. That is the most consequential relationship in Movement II, and its aftermath is missing.
- **Prescription.**
  - In p16 panel 1, give Arthur a reply before Ector's request. For example, ARTHUR: "I do want you. Can I still call you Father?" / ECTOR: "When there's nobody listening, lad." Keep Ector's steward line after it. If p16 gets crowded, split the brothers' scene onto its own page; length is never a reason to compress.
  - Then pay it off once more. Put Ector visibly at the Round Table on p51 or p53. Or give him one beat on p53, where Arthur's "I'll swear first, because I need it most" could be said looking at him.

**4. p25: the quiz that drives Arthur's decision contradicts the book and doesn't make sense as dialogue.**

- **The failure.**
  - "Did the sword ask whose son you were before it came out of the stone?" The stone's gold letters, shown on p8 and p11, say "TRUE-BORN KING." The sword did ask, and p23–24 have just told Arthur the answer. The question undercuts Merlin's own reveal from minutes earlier.
  - "Then why are you asking?" fails anti-terse rule 1 ("give important questions an object"). Arthur hasn't asked anything; he said he's never been in a real fight. A listener would say "asking what?"
  - The page uses the same device (Merlin asks, Arthur answers in one word) that p41 uses far better with "Kay." / "Pellinore." / "Me." Using it here first blunts p41.
  - The page carries the book's go-to-war decision, and that decision currently rests on rhetoric rather than on want.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The reader has to reread the inscription, work out what Merlin meant, and invent the missing question.
- **Prescription.** Rebuild panels 2–3 around the people who chose Arthur, who are what p27 makes him think about and what p31 pays off. For example:
  - MERLIN: "When they shouted your name in the snow, did anyone ask how many fights you'd won?"
  - ARTHUR: "No. They didn't even know my father's name."
  - MERLIN: "Then don't you ask it either. Lot is coming for them as well as for you."
  - ARTHUR: "Then we fight him. All of us together."

  This gives Merlin a want from Arthur right now (stop doubting, commit) and gives Arthur a reason (protect the people). It plants "the line" before p29 and saves the one-word-answer device for p41. Keep both of Kay's lines.

**5. pp 34–35: the chronicle question is raised only by an in-story picture that the chronicle's answer doesn't explain, and Malory is named without being introduced.**

- **The failure.**
  - p35's Question asks "Why does the picture in the hall show armour Arthur never wore?" On p34 that is raised only by a panel note ("shining steel plate armour that Arthur has never worn"). The prose is about the songs leaving out Kay. A reader looking at a 60% panel has no words telling them the armour is wrong.
  - Worse, a reader who does notice gets an answer that doesn't fit. Field 2 says storytellers "dressed him in the armour of their own time." But the hanging on p34 was made in Arthur's own time, inside the story, so the answer cannot explain it.
  - Field 2 also says "Malory lived in the 1400s…" Malory has never been named or explained. He is first identified on p48.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The reader has to raise the question themselves, accept an answer that doesn't fit the picture they saw, and guess who Malory is.
- **Prescription.**
  - Put the exaggeration into the songs in words. Add to p34 Prose Field 2: "In the songs, the boy king wore shining steel from head to foot, which he had never owned, charged alone into a whole army…" The hanging illustrates the song, so it now reads as the song's exaggeration rather than a historical puzzle.
  - Reword p35's Question to "In the songs Arthur wore shining armour. What did knights in his time really look like?"
  - In Field 2, introduce Malory: "Sir Thomas Malory, who wrote the most famous book of Arthur stories, lived in the 1400s…"

**6. pp 44–46: the promise to the Lady, which Merlin tried to stop, has no aftermath.**

- **The failure.**
  - p44: "It is yours, if you will give me a gift one day. Whatever I ask." Arthur: "Then I promise. Whatever you ask." Merlin "has gripped his arm, too late." The staging tells the reader this is dangerous.
  - Then nobody mentions it again. Not Merlin on p46, where he lectures about the scabbard instead. Not Arthur. Not p55, where the book collects its open threads.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The reader has to decide whether the promise matters. Merlin's silence suggests it doesn't, which wastes the best hook in Movement VI, and the volume closes without it.
- **Prescription.** Give Merlin his alarm in words where it happens. In p44 panel 3, after the Lady's line, add MERLIN (low, gripping his arm): "Anything, Arthur. You've promised her anything at all, and you don't even know what she'll want." Or put it on p46 before the scabbard question, with Arthur's reply showing he heard it. Optionally echo it once on p55.

**7. p56: one false claim and one unexplained term on the last page.**

- **The failure.**
  - Field 1: "In 1136, Geoffrey of Monmouth made Arthur a great king with Merlin beside him." In Geoffrey, Merlin serves earlier kings and helps bring about Arthur's birth, then drops out before Arthur reigns. Merlin as Arthur's counsellor is a later addition (Robert de Boron and the French prose tradition). The chronicle's own rule is "nothing invented is presented as fact."
  - Field 2: "Chrétien de Troyes, added Camelot, the Grail, and a knight named Lancelot." Nothing in the book has said what the Grail is.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The reader trusts a wrong fact on the page meant to teach them how to trust these pages, and has to guess at a noun.
- **Prescription.**
  - Field 1: "In 1136, Geoffrey of Monmouth made Arthur a great king, and brought in a wizard named Merlin."
  - Optionally give the Merlin-as-adviser idea its true source, if it stays inside the 170-word ceiling (currently about 132).
  - Field 2: "…added Camelot, a knight named Lancelot, and the Grail, a holy cup his knights would one day search for."
  - Update the Sources line to match.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p3 location line.** "Britain, over fifteen years" contradicts the prose's "thirteen years." It doesn't render, but correct it for the page contract.
- **p8 Lot's line.** "It's one of Merlin's tricks, that wizard who does tricks with swords" sounds planted for the p23 callback. Better: "Ha! It's one of Merlin's tricks. That old wizard loves a trick with a sword." p23's "Says the wizard who does tricks with swords!" still lands.
- **p19 Merlin's silence.** Once Finding 2 is fixed, consider keeping Merlin visible but silent in a doorway, as on p8, so his choice to wait reads as a choice.
- **p31 and p52, Lot's repetition.** "This isn't over" appears twice in one p31 balloon and again on p52. Vary p52 to something new and specific, for example "When you need a real king, boy, send to Orkney."
- **p33 the wounded smith.** He is a prop. One line from him ("Did we hold, my lord?") would put a face on what the victory cost and make Merlin's "Good" earn its place.
- **p44 Merlin's "That is the Lady of the Lake."** "That" points at the arm in p43, then a woman walks in. Either stage her approach in panel 1, or have Merlin say "The arm is hers. Here she comes. Be polite. Be very polite."
- **p47 Arthur's invitation.** "I need knights who don't yield" praises the trait that nearly killed Arthur on p40–41. Consider "I need knights who are stronger than me, and who aren't afraid to tell me no."
- **p48 chronicle Question.** "Are there two magic swords?" The story shows two clearly, so a reader who has never read the source isn't confused. The confusion only exists for people who already know the legend. The question the story actually raises is "Why is it called Excalibur?", which the Lady's naming prompts and Field 1 answers. Rewording would also keep the page to one idea, with the Malory mix-up as its second beat.
- **p49 Pentecost.** "Pentecost, at Camelot." means nothing to these readers. Use "At the spring feast of Pentecost, at Camelot."
- **p50 → p51 time gap.** Building the table takes time and no caption marks it. This is also the best place to bring the mode balance closer to target. The book is currently 68% dramatic, 16% prose and 16% spectacle over the story pages, against roughly 60/20/13. A new illustrated-prose page could cover the building of the table over the winter: carpenters, Merlin watching, Pellinore arriving at the gate.
- **p52 "old bear."** Lot calls Pellinore "an old bear," but Lot is the one with the bearskin and bear banner, and Pellinore's sign is a white boar. "An old boar who sleeps in a tent" fits, and is funnier.
- **Arthur's mother.** He never asks about her. The volume mandate deliberately cuts the conception story, and nothing in the plot needs her, so this is acceptable. Some readers will notice.
- **p56 as the last page.** The audit warns that the book ends on a chronicle page. I checked: the story's last turn lands on p55 (Merlin's farewell, Kay as the one who'll tell Arthur when he's a fool, the empty-seat caption). p56 works as a coda. Keep p55 as written.
- **Five-word audit.** I checked all 25 short balloons. Each is a complete thought or an answer to the line directly before it, with one possible referent, except p25 "Then why are you asking?" (Finding 4). "Who?" on p54 and "Kings in the songs don't." on p36 are clear from the line before.
- **Text budgets.** Every page is within budget. The heaviest are p52 at about 91 words across three panels, and the chronicles at 110–138 words, under the 170 ceiling.
- **Locations.** No page has more than two.
- **Chronicle placement.** All six sit at movement breaks, none are consecutive, and none is on page 1.
- **Chronicle facts (pp 7, 18, 26, 35, 48).** I checked them and found them sound apart from Finding 7.
- **Paraphrase test without the chronicles.** The story still passes with every chronicle page removed.
