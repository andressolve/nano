# Cold read: canonical pages 1–59

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands on its own. I did not need documentation to understand who the central characters were, why Arthur became king, why Lot opposed him, how Arthur learned the limits of solitary heroics, why Excalibur replaced the sword from the stone, or what problem the Round Table was meant to solve.

## Retell

On a stormy night, the wizard Merlin secretly carries King Uther's newborn son away from the castle. Two winters later, Uther is dying. His lords are already thinking about the succession, but Merlin reassures him that his son is safe and far away. Uther publicly declares that he has a son who should rule after him, then dies. The lords fail to find the child, each lord wants the crown, and Britain falls into disorder and war.

Fifteen years after the baby was hidden, Arthur has grown up on Sir Ector's farm without knowing his birth. He works as squire to Ector's older son, Kay, a newly made knight. Arthur wants to become a knight too, but Ector evades his questions about the future. The family travels to London for a great tournament at which all the lords of Britain hope to become king.

On Christmas morning, a sword has appeared through a stone in a churchyard. Gold letters say that whoever can draw it is the true king of Britain. King Lot of Orkney, the strongest northern lord, cannot move it, nor can anyone else. During the tournament Kay discovers that his own sword is missing and sends Arthur back to the inn. The inn is locked, so Arthur remembers the unused sword in the churchyard, pulls it free without effort, and brings it to Kay.

Kay briefly claims the sword and announces that he is king, but Sir Ector makes him swear to the truth. Kay admits that Arthur gave it to him. Arthur replaces the sword and draws it again in front of them. Ector and Kay kneel. Ector then tells Arthur that Merlin brought him to the farm as a baby and ordered Ector to raise him in secret. Arthur is hurt and disoriented: in one morning he seems to have lost both father and brother just as he has gained a crown. Ector assures him that they are still his family, and Arthur promises that Kay will be his steward for life.

Arthur spends a sleepless night wondering who he is and why an old wizard took him from his father. In the morning, the people of London acclaim him, and he is crowned after promising justice and protection. He confronts Merlin, who postpones the explanation until that night. Before Merlin can give it privately, Lot arrives at Arthur's feast, returns Arthur's gifts contemptuously, calls him an unknown farm boy, and threatens him with war. Merlin announces publicly that Arthur is Uther's son. Lot refuses to believe him and marches out to fight.

Arthur angrily accuses Merlin of breaking his promise to let Arthur hear the truth first. Merlin explains that secrecy saved Arthur as a baby and that he revealed the truth publicly only after Lot began turning the whole hall against him. Arthur learns that Uther saw him once on the night he was born and named him. Merlin will not yet tell him about his mother. Arthur admits that Lot is right about one thing: he is still only an inexperienced squire. Kay nevertheless stands beside him, and Arthur resolves to fight with the people who have chosen him.

Arthur's army is far smaller than Lot's. On the morning of battle at Bedegraine, Arthur charges out ahead of his line, is unhorsed in the stream, and is nearly killed. Kay rides into the enemy formation to save him and pulls him back. Arthur then holds the line among his ordinary soldiers. When he raises the sword, it shines like fire, heartening his force and frightening Lot's. Lot retreats, and Arthur wins his first battle, but many are dead and Kay is badly wounded. Merlin makes Arthur look at that cost and remember that his reckless solitary charge almost killed him.

Arthur establishes his court at Camelot. Songs quickly turn the battle into a cleaner legend in which Arthur fought magnificently alone and Kay disappears. Arthur begins to believe the songs. When Kay reports that a knight beside a forest fountain has defeated three of Arthur's knights, Arthur insists on challenging him personally. Kay refuses permission, so Arthur secretly rides out at night, pleased to be free of advisers and responsibilities.

The challenger is King Pellinore, who does not care that Arthur is king. In their fight, the sword from the stone breaks. Arthur refuses to yield even while disarmed, and Pellinore is about to kill him when Merlin magically puts Pellinore to sleep. Arthur is furious that Merlin used magic on a brave opponent, but Merlin points out that Arthur ignored Kay, rejected Pellinore's offer to let him leave, and was too proud to accept mercy. Arthur walks away ashamed, carrying the broken sword.

Merlin takes Arthur to a lake and says there is another sword there, held by someone who does not give it to just anyone. A white-sleeved arm rises from the water holding a sword. The Lady of the Lake appears and offers Arthur Excalibur in return for an unspecified gift she will ask of him one day. Arthur promises before asking what the gift will be and takes the sword from the mysterious hand. Merlin then asks whether the sword or its scabbard is more valuable. Arthur chooses the sword, and Merlin corrects him: the scabbard prevents its wearer from losing blood and is worth far more. Arthur keeps it close. On the way home he declines a rematch with Pellinore, honestly admits the scabbard would make the fight unfair, and invites Pellinore to Camelot instead. Pellinore says he will consider it. Arthur returns to Kay with the broken sword and, this time, acknowledges that Kay's warning was right.

At the next Pentecost feast, Arthur finds his knights fighting over precedence: each wants the seat nearest the king and considers the low end of the table an insult. Arthur stops the quarrel and proposes a table with no head or foot, where everyone—including the king—is equally distant from the centre. Camelot's carpenters spend nearly a year building the enormous round table inside the hall. When it is ready, Pellinore arrives and takes the first seat; each knight's name appears in gold at his place.

Arthur invites Lot to join under a white flag and even offers him Arthur's own chair. Lot refuses to sit level with farmers' sons and departs, but Pellinore openly identifies himself as level with Arthur and unashamed of it. Arthur is the first to swear the fellowship's oath: never to be cruel, to show mercy when asked, to protect those who cannot protect themselves, and never to fight for a bad cause, money, or pride. The knights swear with him.

One unnamed chair remains empty. Kay assumes it is for someone who refused Arthur, but Merlin says it is reserved for someone who has not yet come; anyone else who sits there will suffer terribly. Later, Arthur says goodnight to Ector and still calls him Father. Merlin reminds Arthur that he owes the Lady of the Lake a gift and promises to stay beside him; Kay jokes that Merlin has had years of practice making a fool of Arthur. The empty seat waits for a knight who has not yet been born.

The historical interludes frame this as a legend assembled over centuries rather than literal history. They explain Roman Britain's collapse, the uncertain evidence for a real Arthur, the late arrival of the sword-in-the-stone story, the way armour changes in retellings, the separate literary histories of the stone sword and Excalibur, and the gradual addition of Camelot, Lancelot, the Grail, and the Round Table.

## Cast

- **Arthur:** A slim, freckled blond boy of about fifteen. He begins in ragged brown farm clothes, then wears mail and a blue surcoat as king. He is earnest, brave, impulsive, proud, compassionate, and inexperienced. His story role is the hidden heir who must learn that deserving a crown is different from merely being chosen for it.
- **Kay:** Arthur's older foster brother, a lean young knight with dark hair and a moustache, usually marked by a yellow-and-black surcoat. He has a sharp tongue and sometimes wants glory, but he tells the truth when pressed, protects Arthur at real cost, and becomes his steward and practical counterweight.
- **Sir Ector:** Arthur's heavyset, balding foster father, usually in a red tunic and brown cloak. He is the old knight who raised Arthur in secret, insists on Kay's honesty, gives Arthur emotional security after revealing the adoption, and remains the father Arthur recognizes.
- **Merlin:** A gaunt, very old man with wild white hair, a long white beard, dark green-black robes, and a twisted staff. He is a wizard, Arthur's secret protector, and an unsparing teacher. He manipulates timing and information, but the story shows why: he kept the infant alive, stops Pellinore from killing Arthur, and repeatedly forces Arthur to see consequences.
- **King Uther:** Arthur's biological father, an elderly grey-haired king seen dying in a richly furnished bed. His hidden son and disputed succession create the main political crisis.
- **King Lot of Orkney:** A huge, broad red-haired warrior with braided beard, crown, black armour, and a great fur cloak. He is the most powerful rival lord, contemptuous of Arthur's unknown upbringing and of equality with common people. He remains the principal political and military antagonist.
- **King Pellinore:** A massive, older grey-haired and grey-bearded knight in heavy armour and a battered green surcoat bearing a white boar. He first serves as the unbeatable fountain challenger, defeats Arthur fairly, and later becomes the first knight to join the Round Table, proving that Arthur has learned from the encounter.
- **The Lady of the Lake:** A pale, grave young woman with very long black hair and a white silk gown. She is the supernatural owner or guardian of Excalibur and gives it to Arthur in exchange for a future favor that remains unpaid.
- **The people and knights of Britain:** The ordinary Londoners choose Arthur enthusiastically, while the lords are divided. Farmers, bakers, ordinary soldiers, and a few knights form Arthur's first army. Their support gives him legitimacy, and their deaths make his choices matter.

## Causal chain

1. Uther has a hidden son and entrusts him to Merlin, who places him with Ector so rival lords cannot kill him.
2. Uther dies without the boy publicly present, so Britain fragments and the lords compete for the crown.
3. The sword in the stone creates a test that rank and force cannot settle.
4. Kay's missing tournament sword and the locked inn send Arthur to the churchyard, where his innocent practical need leads him to pass the test.
5. Ector compels Kay to tell the truth, exposing Arthur as the true drawer and triggering the revelation that Ector is his foster father.
6. Public acclaim and the sword make Arthur king, but Lot's contempt for an unknown farm boy forces Merlin to reveal Arthur's parentage and leads to war.
7. Arthur's insecurity and inexperience make him charge alone; Kay's rescue saves him, and Arthur wins only after returning to his people and holding the line with them.
8. Songs erase that dependence and inflate Arthur's solo heroism, tempting him to believe a false version of his own victory.
9. That pride makes Arthur ignore Kay and fight Pellinore alone; his first sword breaks, and Merlin has to save his life.
10. The defeat teaches Arthur to accept correction. The Lady's bargain gives him Excalibur and the protective scabbard, while Merlin's question teaches him to value preservation over display.
11. Arthur declines an unfair rematch and invites Pellinore into fellowship, turning a stronger opponent into a possible ally.
12. The knights' quarrel over seats reveals the same pride and hierarchy that divide Britain. Arthur answers with the Round Table and a shared moral oath.
13. Pellinore's arrival validates that answer, while Lot's refusal preserves the conflict between Arthur's ideal of earned fellowship and Lot's ideal of inherited rank.
14. The Lady's unpaid gift, Lot's continuing hostility, Merlin's withheld account of Arthur's mother, and the dangerous empty seat carry the story forward.

## Backtracking, rereading, or guesses

None required. Kay's false claim to have drawn the sword on page 12 reads as a deliberate lie and is exposed immediately on page 13, so it did not create lasting confusion. The transition from the battle to the fountain episode, the replacement of the broken stone sword by Excalibur, and the year-long construction of the Round Table were all explicit.

I did not misidentify any recurring character. Arthur, Kay, Ector, Merlin, Lot, and Pellinore remained visually and functionally distinct throughout.

## Text and attribution at reading pace

No text or speaker attribution failed. The dialogue order was clear, and narration supplied time jumps and location changes before they could become confusing. The history pages are denser and temporarily pause the adventure, but their purpose and separation from the legendary narrative are unmistakable; they did not require outside knowledge.

## What I expect next

I expect the Round Table to attract more named knights and turn Arthur's personal coming-of-age story into a larger fellowship story. The empty, dangerous seat feels like the strongest immediate promise: the future knight destined for it will probably be central to a later generation and to the Grail material mentioned by the history page. I also expect Lot to remain an enemy, perhaps exploiting class resentment or Arthur's youth after losing the first battle.

The two intimate unresolved threads are Arthur's mother and his bargain with the Lady of the Lake. Merlin's refusal to discuss Arthur's mother feels intentionally postponed, and the Lady's unspecified gift is a clear debt that will return when refusing it would hurt. I also expect Arthur's main internal struggle to continue: songs will keep offering him the glorious solitary hero he wants to be, while Kay, Merlin, Pellinore, Ector, and the Round Table keep teaching him to rule through truth, restraint, and fellowship.
