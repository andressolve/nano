# Cold read: canonical pages 1–50

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands cleanly on the proofs alone. I understood who Arthur was, why his identity had been hidden, how the sword made him king, why Lot opposed him, what Arthur learned in his first battle, and how his pride led him to lose the first sword and receive Excalibur. No page caused material confusion, and no lettering or attribution failure interrupted the read.

## Retell of the story so far

During a storm, Merlin secretly carries a newborn away from King Uther's castle. Two winters later, with Uther dying and his lords already thinking about succession, Merlin makes him acknowledge that his hidden son Arthur should be king. Uther dies that night. Because nobody knows where the son is, the lords fight over the crown while Britain falls into disorder.

Arthur grows up on Sir Ector's farm without knowing his birth. He is fifteen, treated as Ector's son, and serves his older foster brother Kay, a newly made knight. When the household goes to a great New Year tournament in London, Arthur hopes that he too might one day become a knight. Ector promises that he will, but is privately troubled because Arthur is the only son he has left at home.

On Christmas morning a sword appears in a stone in a churchyard, with words declaring that whoever can draw it is Britain's rightful king. The gathered lords all fail. During the tournament, Kay discovers that his own sword is missing and sends Arthur to fetch it. The inn is locked, so Arthur takes the unattended sword from the stone, thinking only that Kay needs one. Kay first presents it as his own claim to kingship, but Ector tests him under oath; Kay admits Arthur gave it to him. Arthur explains what happened and repeats the feat. Ector and Kay kneel, forcing Arthur to understand that Ector is not his birth father. Ector tells him that Merlin brought him as a baby and asked Ector to raise him in secret. Arthur grieves the sudden loss of the identity and family he thought he had, but Ector assures him that they remain his family. Arthur makes Kay his lifelong steward.

The people of London acclaim Arthur, and he is crowned. He presses Merlin for the truth about his father. Before Merlin can tell him privately, King Lot arrives at Arthur's feast, rejects him as an unknown farm boy, and threatens war. Merlin publicly reveals that Arthur is Uther's son. Lot dismisses this as a wizard's trick and leaves to fight.

Arthur faces Lot with a much smaller army of ordinary people and a few knights. Frightened that they chose him and may die for him, he puts on his own mail like a squire. At Bedegraine he charges before his line. Kay follows, rescues him from encirclement, and returns him to the formation. Arthur then holds the line with his people and raises the sword, which shines like a beacon. Lot's larger force breaks off, leaving Arthur victorious but confronted by the wounded and dead his victory cost. Merlin makes him look at that cost and remember that he may not always have Kay there to correct a reckless decision.

Arthur establishes his court at Camelot. Songs rapidly turn the battle into a legend in which Arthur single-handedly defeats an army and his sword shines like the sun. He begins to believe the songs. When Kay reports a knight at a forest fountain who challenges every passerby, Arthur insists on going himself. Kay refuses to let him, so Arthur slips away alone at night.

The challenger is King Pellinore, an older and formidable knight. Arthur declares himself king and accepts a fair fight. Pellinore breaks Arthur's sword and offers him a chance to yield, but Arthur refuses because he thinks kings cannot yield. As Pellinore is about to kill him, Merlin intervenes with a sleeping spell. Arthur angrily thinks Merlin has killed an honorable opponent, then learns Pellinore is only asleep. Merlin forces Arthur to admit that Kay warned him, Pellinore offered mercy, and Arthur's pride caused the danger.

Merlin leads Arthur to a lake and promises another sword, provided Arthur respects the person who keeps it. A white-clad arm raises a sheathed sword from the water, and the Lady of the Lake offers Arthur Excalibur in exchange for a future gift of her choosing. Arthur promises before learning what she will ask. He retrieves the sword by boat. Merlin then asks whether sword or scabbard is more valuable; Arthur chooses the sword, and Merlin explains that the scabbard prevents its wearer from losing blood and is therefore worth far more. Arthur declines an immediate rematch with Pellinore because that protection would make the fight unfair, and instead invites him to Camelot as a knight strong enough to tell the king no. Back at Camelot, Arthur admits to Kay that Kay was right and gives him the broken sword. Their affectionate argument resumes, with Kay promising to tell him no every day.

Between story episodes, the book explains that post-Roman Britain fractured into competing kingdoms; that the historical Arthur is uncertain and appears gradually in later accounts; that the sword-in-the-stone episode was added centuries afterward; that storytellers dressed Arthur in the armor of their own eras; and that the legend has sometimes treated the stone sword and Excalibur as one weapon and sometimes as two. This version clearly chooses two.

## Cast

- **Arthur:** A slight, freckled blond boy of fifteen, first in patched brown farm clothes and later in mail with a blue surcoat and crown. Uther's hidden son, Ector's foster son, Kay's foster brother, former squire, and newly chosen king. He is brave, loyal, impulsive, proud, and still learning that courage does not excuse recklessness.
- **Merlin:** A gaunt, very old man with wild white hair, a long white beard, ragged dark robes, a gnarled staff, and a green ring. He is the wizard who hid Arthur, engineered his survival and recognition, advises him, rescues him when necessary, and teaches by withholding answers until Arthur is ready to hear them.
- **Sir Ector:** A heavyset, balding older man in rust-red clothes, chain sleeves, and a brown cloak. An old knight and Arthur's loving foster father, he raises Arthur in secrecy and is the first to kneel once Arthur repeats the sword test.
- **Sir Kay:** A tall, lean young man with dark hair and a narrow mustache, usually in mail and a black-and-yellow quartered surcoat. Arthur's foster brother, former master, protector, truth-teller, and appointed steward. His sharp tongue masks dependable loyalty.
- **King Uther:** A frail, dying old king with grey-white hair and beard, shown in a red-and-gold bed. Arthur's birth father, who gives the hidden son his blessing as heir just before death.
- **King Lot:** A huge red-haired warrior with braided beard, crown, dark scale armor, black fur, and gold ornaments. A powerful northern lord and the first major rival to reject Arthur's claim, mock his upbringing, and take an army against him.
- **King Pellinore:** A broad, grey-haired and grey-bearded veteran in battered dark plate and a ragged green surcoat bearing a pale animal. He is the fountain knight who defeats Arthur fairly, nearly kills him after Arthur refuses mercy, and may become an ally after Arthur recognizes his strength and honesty.
- **The Lady of the Lake:** A pale, black-haired woman in flowing white silk who rises or stands upon the lake. She is the supernatural keeper of Excalibur and gives it to Arthur in return for an unspecified future gift.
- **The people who follow Arthur:** Mostly farmers, bakers, ordinary soldiers, and a small number of knights. Their choice makes Arthur king in practice, and their suffering turns victory from a song into a responsibility.

## Causal chain

1. Merlin hides Uther's newborn son because rival lords would kill him.
2. Uther dies before the secret heir can be publicly established, so the lords compete for power and Britain remains divided.
3. Arthur grows up safely but ignorant of his identity in Ector's household.
4. Kay needs a sword at the tournament, and the locked inn sends Arthur looking elsewhere.
5. Arthur innocently draws the churchyard sword because he is thinking about Kay rather than the crown.
6. Ector's oath and Arthur's repeated draw expose Kay's false claim and confirm Arthur as the chosen king.
7. Lot rejects an unknown young ruler; Merlin's public disclosure of Arthur's parentage answers the question but does not overcome Lot's ambition.
8. Lot's invasion forces Arthur to lead before he is experienced.
9. Arthur charges alone, Kay saves him, and Arthur wins only after returning to his people and holding the line with them.
10. The victory songs erase the cost and Kay's role, feeding Arthur's pride.
11. Arthur evades Kay to imitate the solitary king in the songs and accepts Pellinore's challenge.
12. Pellinore breaks the stone sword; Arthur's refusal to yield forces Merlin to save him.
13. Merlin's rebuke makes Arthur confront the chain of warnings and mercies he ignored.
14. The Lady of the Lake supplies Excalibur and its protective scabbard in exchange for a future obligation.
15. Arthur's refusal to exploit the scabbard in a rematch, his invitation to Pellinore, and his admission to Kay show that he has begun to learn from the failure.

## Backtracking, rereading, or guesses

None. I did not need to return to an earlier page to identify a speaker, character, location, or cause. The arm in white silk on page 44 is intentionally mysterious for one page; page 45 immediately identifies its owner as the Lady of the Lake, so this read as a reveal rather than a gap.

The historical interludes on pages 7, 19, 27, 36, and 50 temporarily pause the plot, but their purpose and distinction from the legend are clear. I did not mistake them for story events.

## Text and attribution at reading pace

No text failed. All narration, inset labels, inscriptions, sound effects, and dialogue were readable at the supplied 600 × 900 size. I could attribute every speech balloon at reading pace, including Merlin's partially off-panel command on page 41 and the exchange among Arthur, Merlin, and Kay on page 49.

## What I expect next

I expect Pellinore to accept Arthur's invitation and become one of the strong knights around him. Arthur will probably seek more knights who can challenge his judgment rather than flatter him, leading toward the table promised by the title. Lot remains an unresolved enemy and may return with allies. The Lady of the Lake's unspecified future request is an obvious debt that will come due. Most of all, I expect Arthur's next stage to test whether he can keep the lesson of Bedegraine and the fountain: a king must listen, share danger without chasing it, and value the people and protections around him as much as the shining weapon in his hand.
