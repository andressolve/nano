## Page intent

## PAGE 49

A dramatic page at the gate of Camelot at dusk, torches being lit. Kay is in the gateway, unshaven and rumpled, plainly awake all night, and complains that he hates waiting. Arthur gets down and holds out the two halves of the sword from the stone to Kay, hilt first, the way he once handed Kay a sword at the tournament, and says Kay was right. Kay answers that he is always right and it's exhausting, takes the halves to carry for him, and then notices the red scabbard. Arthur asks him to keep telling him no; Kay says every day. The brothers own the page, and the handing-over is its heart. The dominant turn: the brothers are square again, and Arthur has a new sword nobody at court has seen. What stays subordinate: Merlin, the horses, and the castle gate.

## Exact owner-controlled script

## PAGE 49 · *dramatic*

**Location:** the gate of Camelot, dusk.

**Panel 1 — 35%.** Dusk at the castle gate, torches being lit. Kay stands in
the gateway, unshaven, his steward's black rumpled, a torch in his hand; he
has clearly not slept since last night. Arthur rides in, Merlin behind him, the red
scabbard at Arthur's hip. Kay on the left, Arthur on the right.

> **KAY:** "The guard woke in time to see your horse's tail. Merlin went after you and told me to wait. I hate waiting."

**Panel 2 — dominant, 40%.** Arthur, down from his horse, holds out the two
halves of the sword from the stone to Kay, hilt first, the way he once
handed Kay a sword at the tournament.

> **ARTHUR:** "You told me no, and I went anyway. You were right."
>
> **KAY:** "Of course I was right. I'm always right. It's exhausting."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Kay takes the broken halves and tucks them under his
arm to carry them for him, then frowns at the red scabbard.

> **KAY:** "And where did you get that one?"
>
> **ARTHUR:** "A lake. Will you tell me no again, next time?"
>
> **KAY:** "Every day."

### C1 — Transcription
Every listed string reads exactly from the 600 × 900 proof, once, in causal order, owned by the right mouth by tail and placement, with no extra text; a string that does not read is blocking, a string that reads is not to be measured. Spacing around a dash, curly against straight quotes, a hyphen for a dash, a comma present or missing inside a line, and a regional spelling variant are nonblocking; the words and their order must match. A line owned by placement (directly under its speaker's previous balloon) is owned even if its tail wanders.

### C2 — The handing-over owns the page
The eye finds Arthur holding out the broken sword to Kay; the page is dramatic. If the gate arrival or the horses dominate instead, the apology loses its weight: blocking.

### C3 — Attribution
Kay complains about waiting; Arthur says "You were right"; Kay answers that he is always right; Kay asks about the scabbard; Arthur says "A lake…"; Kay ends with "Every day." Each must hang from its own speaker's mouth by tail or placement. If Arthur's admission reads as Kay's, or "Every day." reads as Arthur's, the reconciliation inverts: blocking.

### C4 — Identity
Kay: tall, black-haired, sharp-faced young man with a thin moustache, in steward's black with yellow, rumpled. Arthur: blond, round-faced boy in blue. If the brothers can be confused, it is blocking. Merlin, if visible, is silent.

### C5 — Object state
The sword from the stone is in exactly two pieces, dull iron, never whole; Kay ends holding them. Excalibur stays in the red scabbard on Arthur's hip. A whole old sword, or the two swords confused, is blocking.

### C6 — Focal integrity
Arthur's two hands holding out the halves and Kay's receiving hands are cleanly drawn. A broken face or extra hand on the brothers is blocking.

### C7 — Kay's steward state
Kay appears in his steward's black: a plain black tunic with a yellow border, no mail and no black-and-yellow surcoat. Kay in his old mail and quartered surcoat is blocking: it sends him back to the tournament and battle pages just when his place at Camelot should read. The exact width of the yellow border or whether the keys show is nonblocking.
