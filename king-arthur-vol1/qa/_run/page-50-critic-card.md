## Page intent

## PAGE 50

A chronicle page: the reader steps out of the story onto a vellum page of an old chronicle, in ink and watercolour. The question it opens with is the one the story just raised: two magic swords? The answer: yes, in this story, and the famous one's name is far older than the stone, changing from tongue to tongue until it became Excalibur. Old books told the stone sword and Excalibur as one sword or as two, and this book chose two so that Arthur could lose the first. The dominant image is a family tree of names growing up from a sword, one branch tangling with a little drawing of the stone and anvil. The dominant turn: whatever its name, the king now has his sword, and a hall full of knights to keep in order. What stays subordinate: decoration; no story character appears.

## Exact owner-controlled script

## PAGE 50 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Why does Arthur get a second magic sword, and where does the name Excalibur come from?

**Sources:** *Culhwch and Olwen* (Welsh, c. 1100) — Caledfwlch; Geoffrey of Monmouth, *History of the Kings of Britain* (c. 1136) — Caliburnus; Old French romances (late 12th–13th c.) — Escalibor; the Vulgate *Merlin* identifies the stone sword with Excalibur; Malory, *Le Morte d'Arthur* I.9 calls the stone sword Excalibur, breaks Arthur's sword against Pellinore (I.23), and has the Lady give Excalibur (I.25).

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A chronicle page on ivory vellum. The dominant
image is a family tree of names drawn in brown ink: at its root, a long
sword painted in watercolour lying across the page; branches growing up from
it, each ending in a small round frame holding one name; one branch crosses
over and tangles with a little drawing of the stone and anvil. An illuminated
initial begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(top of the page)*
> "Two magic swords? In this story, yes: the sword from the stone, and Excalibur. Arthur's famous sword is older than his stone. In a Welsh tale from around 1100, it is called Caledfwlch. Geoffrey of Monmouth, writing in 1136, called it Caliburnus. French poets made it Escalibor, and English storytellers made that Excalibur."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(bottom of the page)*
> "When the sword in the stone came along, some old books made it the same sword as Excalibur, and some made them two; storytellers have told it both ways for eight hundred years. Sir Thomas Malory does both! This book chose two swords, so that Arthur could lose the first one."

> **LABEL** *(in the lowest frame)*
> "Caledfwlch, about 1100"

> **LABEL** *(in the middle frame)*
> "Caliburnus, 1136"

> **LABEL** *(in the top frame)*
> "Excalibur"

**Why you turn the page:** whatever its name, the king now has his sword, and a hall full of knights to keep in order.

## Materiality threshold

The critic is a release gate, not a defect collector. A visible issue is
blocking only when it materially harms the reader event, exact text or
attribution, named or focal identity, consequential continuity or object state,
focal generation integrity, or the dominant dramatic relationship. Every

### C1 — Transcription
Every listed string reads exactly from the 600 × 900 proof, once, in reading order, with no extra text; each label sits in its own frame of the tree, Caledfwlch lowest and Excalibur at the top. A string that does not read is blocking; a string that reads is not to be measured. Spacing around a dash, curly against straight quotes, a hyphen for a dash, a comma present or missing inside a line, and a regional spelling variant are nonblocking; the words and their order must match. The illuminated initial is the T of "Two", not an extra letter.

### C2 — Every claim reads as written
Each name, date, and claim in the fields and labels reads as scripted. A wrong date, a misspelled sword-name that changes the name, or a label in the wrong frame misleads the reader about history: blocking.

### C3 — The Chronicle register
The page is visibly a vellum chronicle page: ivory vellum, ruled margin, brown ink and watercolour wash, a red-and-blue illuminated initial. An oil-painted scene, a story character, a balloon, or a caption box means the reader has not stepped out of the story: blocking.

### C4 — The dominant image
A family tree of names grows up from a sword, with one branch tangling with a small stone and anvil. If the image is missing or the names float free of it, the idea loses its picture: blocking. Ornament detail is nonblocking.

### C5 — No invented text
No extra names, empty frames filled with pseudo-writing, or lettering on the stone. Invented legible words are blocking; faint illegible texture in the margin is not.
