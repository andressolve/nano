### Register — *Greenwood Romance*

Greenwood Romance. Golden-age adventure illustration in oil, in the tradition of the great book illustrators of a century ago: opaque oil paint on canvas board with visible, confident brushwork, a warm raw-umber underpainting showing through, thick impasto in the highlights, and strong simple shapes that read from across a room. Earth and forest: raw umber, burnt sienna, yellow ochre, oxide red, sap green, oak brown, iron grey, bone white, snow-light blue, and deep Prussian-blue nights; one deep ultramarine reserved for the young king. Light like a stage: low winter sun, torchlight and firelight with real cast shadows, moonlight in silver and blue, white mist. Materials painted with weight: riveted mail rings, quilted linen, rough wool, oak beams, wet grey stone, iron, leather, horsehair, snow, still water. Faces painted with character and weather, never pretty. Not smooth prestige-oil realism. No glossy concept-art surfaces, no airbrushed skin, no photographic rendering, no anime proportions, no children's-book softness, no plastic 3D render, no fantasy-game armour, no glowing magic effects except where named. No signature, monogram, artist's mark, or watermark in any corner.

Colour belongs to people: Arthur deep ultramarine blue (and nobody else wears it), Kay black and yellow, Ector russet, Lot black bearskin and gold torc with copper-red braids, Pellinore faded green with a white boar on dented black armour, Merlin dark grey-green, the Lady of the Lake white samite, Uther red and gold with a red dragon. ### The lettering law — construction numbers for the builder

Outer safe margin 64 px on every side; no essential text below 72 px from the bottom edge; minimum panel gutter 24 px; no balloon, caption, or prose field crosses a panel border. Speech lettering 44–50 px tall, short replies 48–54 px, **never below the 40 px floor**; comfortable balloon width 240–390 px; at most about 24 words in one balloon; warm ivory balloon fill, restrained dark painted outline; tails end in open space beside the speaker's mouth; silent figures get no balloon and no tail fragment. ### Hierarchy and staging

One dominant image plainly owns the page; the share named in the prompt is a construction target, and the generator should build a clear hierarchy rather than a stack of equal bands. When the page's turn is a quiet beat (an answer, a sentence, a face changing) and another panel carries action or a crowd, the quiet panel is still the largest image on the page by far, close and tall, and the busy panel is a shallow strip seen from far back with small figures; this generator favors action tableaux and must be told otherwise. At most two small reaction insets unless causality needs a strip. A clear top-to-bottom reading path. The first speaker in a panel stands on the LEFT; opposed speakers stand on opposite sides with each balloon on its owner's side; in a three-line exchange, A upper on A's side, B middle on B's side, A's second line lower on A's side; a reply is never readable before the line that prompts it and sits clearly lower than it, never level. Every balloon's tail ends beside its own speaker's mouth; a tail into open space, into a crowd, or toward an animal is a defect. Every visible speaker's mouth is visible and its tail corridor is clear of hands, props, and bystanders. Any exchange of two short lines in one panel where either line could plausibly be spoken by either character (a question and its answer, a check and its confirmation, a one-word address) is staged as two single-speaker beats, the questioner alone with mouth visible and the balloon beside that mouth, then the answerer alone the same way, as stacked strips or a strip cut into two panels read left to right; a shared two-shot with crossing tails is the most common attribution failure on this generator. A focal action uses exactly two hands, named. Consequential objects that the story counts (five of something, three of something) are drawn in a spaced row so the reader can count them, and the prompt says "count them". Sound cues render the exact letters given, with no added punctuation or extra words; the run length of a repeated letter is not a defect. Words painted on a story object (a banner, a plaque, a sign) are object text: they must read exactly as scripted, and the same object seen in two panels may carry them twice. Nothing else is lettered: no laugh lines, no invented captions, no labels. Vertical accumulation for time, consequence, descent, isolation, aftermath. Unnamed figures never carry a complete reserved identity stack: background lords and knights are middle-aged, brown- or dark-haired with short beards, in muted surcoats (brown, rust, grey, dull green, dull red); never a blond boy, never a forked white beard, never copper-red braids, never a bald clean-shaven old man, never black-and-yellow, never deep blue, never white samite. Townspeople wear browns and greys. Churchmen are in white with small faces. **War deaths are real and never drawn:** no blood, no wound, no body, no severed anything; a blow lands between panels, a rider falls out of frame, the cost is empty saddles, dropped shields, and the faces of the living. The two swords are never confused: the sword from the stone is plain, dull iron-grey, a dark iron cross-guard and round iron pommel, and after it breaks it is never seen whole; Excalibur is longer and bright, with a gold-wired grip and one green stone in the cross-guard, in a dark-red leather scabbard chased with gold, worn at Arthur's hip from the lake onward. Merlin's magic is never a glow; the only magical light in the book is the sword's blaze in battle and its brief flare when the people roar.

### Chronicle pages (Mode 4) — a different medium, same lettering law

A page whose mode is *chronicle* is not a story page. It is **a page from a chronicle**: the whole canvas is warm ivory vellum with a ruled brown-ink margin, drawn in iron-gall brown ink with fine pen hatching and transparent watercolour wash, with one illuminated initial painted in vermilion red and lapis blue with a touch of raised gold leaf. No oil paint, no full-bleed painted scene, no story character, no balloon, no caption box. The illuminated initial is the first letter of the first word of CHRONICLE FIELD 1, made large and decorated, with the rest of that word following it in the field; it is never an extra letter. CHRONICLE FIELD text is lettered in the prose serif (36–42 px, 38–52 characters a line, left-aligned, calm ragged edge) in dark brown ink directly on the plain vellum, with generous space around it and nothing drawn behind it. LABEL strings are short hand-lettered ink labels sitting beside their map point, date, or object, at the small-label level, never smaller than about 32 px. The one dominant image (a map, a museum study of an object, a timeline, two figures as costume studies) owns the page; the fields sit on plain vellum beside or below it. The reader must know at a glance that they have stepped out of the story.
