## Page intent

## PAGE 45

A dramatic page. Merlin warns Arthur to be very polite, and the Lady of the Lake walks toward them across the water as if it were a floor: tall, still, black hair to her knees, white samite. She owns the page. She names Arthur as Uther's son, names the sword Excalibur, and offers it for a gift to be named later, whatever she asks. Arthur promises at once, and Merlin grabs his arm too late, appalled that the boy has promised anything at all without knowing what. The dominant turn: Arthur has made a promise he doesn't understand, and the sword is waiting. The reader turns because she has told him to take the boat. What stays subordinate: the small boat in the reeds and the far arm still holding the sword.

## Exact owner-controlled script

## PAGE 45 · *dramatic*

**Location:** the lakeshore.

**Panel 1 — 25%.** On the shore, Merlin leans close to Arthur. Merlin left,
Arthur right. A small wooden boat is drawn up in the reeds beside them.

> **MERLIN:** "That arm belongs to the lake. And here comes the Lady of the Lake herself. Be polite. Be very polite."

**Panel 2 — dominant, 50%.** Walking toward them across the surface of the
lake as if it were a floor, the LADY OF THE LAKE: tall and still, a long
pale face, calm grey eyes, very long straight black hair wet to her knees,
a long plain gown of white samite, a thin silver circlet. Ripples spread
from her bare feet. Far behind her, out in the middle of the lake, the arm in
white silk still holds up the sword.

> **LADY OF THE LAKE:** "Arthur, son of Uther. That sword is Excalibur. It is yours, if you will give me a gift one day. Whatever I ask."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Arthur, face to face with her at the water's edge;
Merlin, behind him, has gripped his arm, too late.

> **ARTHUR:** "Then I promise. Whatever you ask."
>
> **MERLIN:** "Anything, Arthur? You've promised her anything at all, and you don't even know what she'll want."
>
> **LADY OF THE LAKE:** "Take the boat, and take the sword from the hand."

**Why you turn the page:** Arthur has made a promise he doesn't understand, and the sword is waiting.

## Materiality threshold

The critic is a release gate, not a defect collector. A visible issue is
blocking only when it materially harms the reader event, exact text or
attribution, named or focal identity, consequential continuity or object state,
focal generation integrity, or the dominant dramatic relationship. Every
correction replaces the complete page. Minor background, cosmetic, tiny-prop,
geometry, colour, texture, and prompt-fidelity observations are nonblocking.
Omit them. Numeric panel shares and lettering sizes are builder construction
guidance, never critic measurements.

Each numbered criterion is also its stable retry signature. Cite it only when
the reader harm and redraw justification are concrete.

A lettered speaker name, label, or tag anywhere on the page is extra text and
blocks under the transcription criterion, whatever else reads correctly:
finished pages carry dialogue, captions, prose, and sound cues only.
A sound cue whose letters and order match the script reads correctly; the run
length of a repeated letter in it is nonblocking and never counted.

## Numbered blocking criteria

## Page 45 — card

### C1 — Transcription
Every listed string reads exactly from the 600 × 900 proof, once, in causal order, owned by the right mouth by tail and placement, with no extra text; a string that does not read is blocking, a string that reads is not to be measured. Spacing around a dash, curly against straight quotes, a hyphen for a dash, a comma present or missing inside a line, and a regional spelling variant are nonblocking; the words and their order must match. A line owned by placement (it names its addressee, or sits directly under its speaker's previous balloon) is owned even if its tail wanders.

### C2 — The Lady owns the page
The Lady walking toward us on the water is the image the eye finds first and holds longest; the page is dramatic. If the shore exchange or the far arm dominates, her offer loses its weight: blocking.

### C3 — Attribution in the last exchange
Arthur's promise must hang from Arthur, Merlin's alarmed question from Merlin, and "Take the boat…" from the Lady, read in that order. Merlin's line naming Arthur cannot belong to Arthur. If Arthur's promise reads as Merlin's, or the Lady's command reads before Merlin's alarm, the bargain's logic breaks: blocking.

### C4 — Identity
The Lady: tall, still, long pale face, very long black hair, white gown, silver circlet. Arthur: a blond boy in blue. Merlin: a thin stooped old man with a long forked white beard and staff. Any of them unrecognisable, or a second woman, is blocking.

### C5 — Object state and world
The sword is still far off, sheathed, in the arm's hand; Arthur holds no new sword; the boat is on the shore. The lake stays cold silver and white. A sword already in Arthur's hands breaks the next page: blocking.

### C6 — Focal integrity
The Lady's face and Merlin's grip on Arthur's arm are cleanly drawn. A broken face or a tangled extra hand on the focal figures is blocking; background reeds and water texture are not.
