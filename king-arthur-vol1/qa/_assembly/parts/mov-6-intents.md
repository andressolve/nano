## PAGE 44

A spectacle page. Arthur and Merlin come out of the forest onto the shore of a lake that looks like another world: still water, white mist, silver hills, no warmth anywhere. Out of the very middle of the lake rises a woman's arm in white silk, holding a long bright sword straight up in a dark-red scabbard. The arm and the sword own the page; Arthur and Merlin are tiny on the shore, and Arthur's blue is the only warm colour in all that grey. The dominant turn: the other sword is right there. The reader turns to find out who is holding it. What stays subordinate: the reeds, the far hills, and the two small figures, who give the scale and nothing more; the broken sword is not part of this picture.

## PAGE 45

A dramatic page. Merlin warns Arthur to be very polite, and the Lady of the Lake walks toward them across the water as if it were a floor: tall, still, black hair to her knees, white samite. She owns the page. She names Arthur as Uther's son, names the sword Excalibur, and offers it for a gift to be named later, whatever she asks. Arthur promises at once, and Merlin grabs his arm too late, appalled that the boy has promised anything at all without knowing what. The dominant turn: Arthur has made a promise he doesn't understand, and the sword is waiting. The reader turns because she has told him to take the boat. What stays subordinate: the small boat in the reeds and the far arm still holding the sword.

## PAGE 46

A spectacle page, almost silent. Arthur stands in the small wooden boat in the middle of the misty lake and reaches up with both hands to take the sword and its dark-red scabbard from the white-sleeved hand; far off, the Lady stands on the water, small and white, watching. Then the empty hand lets go and sinks into the still water. Arthur owns the page in the moment of taking. The dominant turn: Arthur has Excalibur. The reader turns to see what he does with it. What stays subordinate: the boat, the ripples, and the Lady, who is a distant witness; nobody speaks, and the broken sword plays no part here.

## PAGE 47

A dramatic page on the lakeshore. Arthur has drawn Excalibur and holds it up in pure delight; Merlin asks which he likes better, the sword or the scabbard, and Arthur picks the sword without a thought. Merlin calls him a fool: whoever wears the scabbard loses no blood. Then, slowly, Arthur lays the bright sword on the grass and buckles the empty scabbard on first, looking at it, while Merlin raises one eyebrow and allows that he may live to be old. Arthur's change of mind owns the page, and the joke is Merlin's. The dominant turn: Arthur has chosen the thing that keeps him safe, and on the way home he will have to choose again. What stays subordinate: the lake behind them, which is only a cold backdrop now.

## PAGE 48

A dramatic page in the fountain clearing by daylight. Pellinore, awake and enormous, blocks the road with his sword planted point-down and offers to try the new sword. Arthur's hand goes to Excalibur's hilt, then deliberately leaves it and rests on the scabbard: with that on, he cannot bleed, so a fight would be cheating. Then he holds out his open hand and invites Pellinore to Camelot, because he needs knights strong enough to tell him no. Pellinore stares at the hand, suspicious, and says he'll think about it. Arthur owns the page; the dominant turn: Arthur turned down a fight he couldn't lose, and asked his enemy to join him. The reader turns to see whether Pellinore comes. What stays subordinate: Merlin, silent behind Arthur, and the pavilion.

## PAGE 49

A dramatic page at the gate of Camelot at dusk, torches being lit. Kay is in the gateway, unshaven and rumpled, plainly awake all night, and complains that he hates waiting. Arthur gets down and holds out the two halves of the sword from the stone to Kay, hilt first, the way he once handed Kay a sword at the tournament, and says Kay was right. Kay answers that he is always right and it's exhausting, takes the halves to carry for him, and then notices the red scabbard. Arthur asks him to keep telling him no; Kay says every day. The brothers own the page, and the handing-over is its heart. The dominant turn: the brothers are square again, and Arthur has a new sword nobody at court has seen. What stays subordinate: Merlin, the horses, and the castle gate.

## PAGE 50

A chronicle page: the reader steps out of the story onto a vellum page of an old chronicle, in ink and watercolour. The question it opens with is the one the story just raised: two magic swords? The answer: yes, in this story, and the famous one's name is far older than the stone, changing from tongue to tongue until it became Excalibur. Old books told the stone sword and Excalibur as one sword or as two, and this book chose two so that Arthur could lose the first. The dominant image is a family tree of names growing up from a sword, one branch tangling with a little drawing of the stone and anvil. The dominant turn: whatever its name, the king now has his sword, and a hall full of knights to keep in order. What stays subordinate: decoration; no story character appears.
