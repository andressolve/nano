## PAGE 37

Dramatic. Morning in Camelot's hall. Kay, now the king's steward in black with a yellow border, brings news: a knight has pitched his tent beside a fountain in the forest, fights everyone who passes, and has knocked three of Arthur's knights into the water. Arthur, leaning forward in his chair, wants to ride out and fight him himself. Kay refuses flatly, because kings send knights; Arthur answers with the songs; Kay has the last word and walks away. Kay owns the talk, and Arthur owns the ending: he goes quiet with his jaw set. The one dominant turn: Arthur has been told no, and he isn't going to listen. The reader turns to see what he does about it. The hall is a plain daylight setting; the news, the refusal, and Arthur's set jaw carry the page.

## PAGE 38

Illustrated prose. Night in the great oak forest. Arthur has slipped out of the sleeping castle alone, and he rides his grey horse down a moonlit road in mail and his blue surcoat, his helmet hanging from the saddle, grinning up at the moon. The prose lets the reader feel his happiness and hear the pride under it: no steward, no wizard, only the moon and a sword that beat an army, and a song about tonight already made up in his head. Below, far ahead through the trees, firelight glows beside a glint of water. The one dominant turn: he's reached the fountain, and someone is waiting there. Arthur's joy owns the page; the fire stays small and distant, a promise rather than a person.

## PAGE 39

Dramatic. A moonlit clearing with an old stone fountain, a battered green-and-white pavilion, and a dented shield hanging from an oak. King Pellinore rises from his fire, the biggest man in the book, both hands on a huge sword planted point-down, and states his rule: nobody passes without fighting him. Arthur, on horseback, plays his crown. Pellinore names himself, says he has never yielded to any man, and settles his great helm on his head; Arthur answers with one eager word. Pellinore's size and calm own the page, and Arthur's eagerness is the spark. The one dominant turn: the boy king is about to fight the biggest man he has ever seen. The reader turns for the fight.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic, nearly silent. The joust: two riders crash together and both lances shatter. Then the two men on foot by the pool, Arthur smaller and attacking, Pellinore planted. Then the moment that owns the page: the sword from the stone meets Pellinore's huge blade and snaps in two, the broken half spinning away through the moonlight toward the pool, and Arthur's face goes blank with disbelief as he cries out. The one dominant turn: the sword that made Arthur king is broken, and he has nothing left to fight with. The joust and the circling are quick lead-ins; the break and Arthur's face are what the reader must see. Nobody is wounded.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Arthur lies on his back in the wet grass, the broken hilt still in his fist, with Pellinore's sword point at his throat. Pellinore offers him his life for one word; Arthur refuses, because kings don't yield. Pellinore, sorry for it, lifts the sword in both hands. Then, out of the dark behind him, come a long hand with a silver ring, two fingers pointing, the tip of a crooked staff, and the end of a forked white beard, and a single quiet command. The giant topples like a felled oak, his eyes closed. The one dominant turn: Merlin has stopped the killing blow, and Arthur doesn't yet know how. The pinned boy owns the page; Merlin is only a hand and a voice; no blow lands.

## PAGE 42

Dramatic. Arthur scrambles up in a fury, sure that Merlin has killed the bravest knight he ever fought with a spell; Merlin, walking into the firelight, tells him the giant is only asleep and snoring. Then Arthur sinks to his knees holding the two halves of the sword from the stone, and Merlin, standing over him, convicts him with three questions: who told you not to come, who offered to let you walk home, who was too proud to take it. Arthur answers each, and the last answer is himself. The kneeling boy with the broken sword owns the page. The one dominant turn: Arthur has lost the sword from the stone, and he knows whose fault it is. Pellinore stays a sleeping shape in the background.

## PAGE 43

Illustrated prose. Dawn mist at the forest's edge. Arthur walks out leading his horse, the two halves of the broken sword held against his chest; Merlin walks a few steps ahead with his staff; behind them, Pellinore's fire is dying in the trees. The prose carries Arthur's shame plainly: Merlin's following him was the only reason he is alive, he nearly died for a song, and the sword that chose him lies broken because he crept out. Then Merlin, half turned, says there is another sword, and ahead of them white mist lies over open water. The one dominant turn: there's another sword, and it's somewhere in that mist. The walk owns the page; the mist is the hook.
