Dramatic. At the Pentecost feast at Camelot the knights are on their feet along a long table, shoving and shouting over which seats sit nearest the king, and Kay, now the harried steward, rushes to Arthur to say it is the seats again. Down the table a knight has drawn a knife on another. Then the page belongs to Arthur: on his feet at the head of the table, he brings Excalibur's pommel down on the boards so hard the cups jump, and every knight freezes while he forbids anyone to bleed in his hall over a chair. The dominant turn is the king stopping the fight, but not the reason for it. The reader turns to see how he will fix the reason. The quarrelling crowd and the knife stay small and subordinate; the blow and Arthur's face own the page.

## PAGE 52

Dramatic. Late that night in the empty hall, Arthur, Merlin and Kay stand by the hearth. Arthur says the knights fight over who sits closest to him, and Merlin answers that knights always will at a table with a head and a foot. Then Arthur looks up at a round shield hanging above the hearth and the idea dawns on his face: a table with no head. Kay scoffs that it is a cartwheel. Arthur, grinning now, turns to Merlin and explains it, and the old wizard admits it has never been tried. The dominant turn is Arthur deciding to build the table; the page belongs to his face catching the idea from the shield. Kay's joke is a sideline, and the furniture of the hall stays plain and subordinate.

## PAGE 53

Illustrated prose. The great hall becomes a carpenters' workshop for most of a year: sawdust, trestles, curved oak sections of an enormous round tabletop, and Arthur himself, sleeves rolled up, holding one end of a long curved plank while Merlin watches from the hearth bench, chin on his staff. The prose tells the size of the work and gives the reader a smile about Kay and Merlin. Then, on a late-spring evening, the hall doors open and Pellinore stands there, huge and dusty from the road, helmet under his arm, looking in at the finished table, bare and waiting. The dominant turn is that the table is finished and the knights are about to sit down at it. The carpenters stay anonymous and the tools stay background.

## PAGE 54

Spectacle. Seen from high in the rafters, the finished round table fills the hall, and knights are taking their seats all around it. In front of each seated knight a name glows in gold on the rim, too small and far away to read. Arthur sits at one place on the ring, no higher than anyone, the only figure in blue; Ector beams among the knights; across the table Pellinore, the biggest man there, sits down with a hand on his heart. One chair is empty, with no gold before it. The caption says the names appeared and that Pellinore sat first. The dominant turn is the table full of names, except for one place. The individual knights stay small; the ring of gold and the single dark gap own the page.

## PAGE 55

Dramatic. King Lot appears in the hall doors under a white flag of truce, two lords behind him, to see the boy's wonder for himself. Arthur, risen, holds out his own chair to him and says he would rather have Lot at this table than across a valley. Lot sneers at sitting level with a farm boy's steward and an old boar who sleeps in a tent; across the table Kay fires back that this steward chased him off a battlefield, and Pellinore says simply that he sat down and is not ashamed. Lot turns on his heel and walks out, refusing to sit level with anybody. The dominant turn is Lot walking out while the knights who stayed are about to swear. The page belongs to the offer and the refusal; the lords behind Lot stay silent background.

## PAGE 56

Dramatic. All the knights stand around the round table holding their swords upright by the blades so the hilts make crosses. Arthur alone kneels at his place, Excalibur held up the same way, and swears first because he needs it most: never to be cruel, to give mercy to anyone who asks, to protect anyone who cannot protect himself, never to fight for a wrong cause. Behind him the doors Lot walked out of stand open on the dark. Ector, Kay, Pellinore and the others listen, and Ector's eyes are wet; then every knight answers together. The dominant turn is that the fellowship is sworn. The page belongs to the kneeling boy king and the faces answering him; the open doors stay quiet background, and nobody draws a sword against anyone.

## PAGE 57

Dramatic. Kay points across the table at the one chair with no name in front of it and offers to sit in it himself, so it will not look as if someone refused Arthur. The page belongs to the empty chair, tall and plain in a pool of light from a high window, with Merlin standing behind it, his hand on its back, telling Kay there will be no name on it yet, because it is kept for someone who has not come. Arthur asks who. Merlin tells the whole table they will know him when he comes, and that anyone else who sits in it will be very sorry. Kay, half-risen, sits back down fast with both hands up. The dominant turn is the table waiting for someone nobody knows. Kay's retreat is comedy, kept small.

## PAGE 58

Dramatic. Late at night Ector, candle in hand, leaves the hall for bed and looks back; Arthur, by the hearth, wishes his father goodnight and is answered. Then the page opens into its dominant image: the great round table in moonlight, Excalibur laid on it in its red scabbard, the empty chair lit by a shaft of moonlight, and, small by the dying hearth, Merlin and Arthur side by side. Merlin reminds Arthur that he still owes the Lady of the Lake a gift and that he will not always be beside him; Arthur asks who will tell him when he is being a fool, and Merlin answers, Kay. Last, the empty chair alone, and a caption: the knight it waits for has not been born. The dominant turn is that every thread is a story still to come.

## PAGE 59

Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from a chronicle, which asks who made up the story of King Arthur and whether the Round Table is real. The answer is the dominant image: a snowball rolling down a long hill and growing, a small date flag at each step where a storyteller added something, from Geoffrey of Monmouth's king and wizard, through Wace's round table and Chrétien's Camelot, Lancelot and Grail, down to Malory's one enormous book. At the bottom of the hill hangs the real Winchester Round Table, made long after any real Arthur could have lived. The dominant turn is that the story is not over: the storytellers had many more knights to add. No story character appears; the vellum, ink and wash register owns the page, and the snowball carries the idea.
