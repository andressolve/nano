# Page intents

## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The book opens on a storm: a black castle on a sea cliff, one window lit, rain in sheets and waves breaking far below. Down a narrow cliff path comes one tall, thin, hooded old man with a crooked staff, carrying a bundle under his cloak; the only warm light on the page is his lantern on that bundle, where a tiny hand reaches out of the cloth. The title sits in the storm sky; one caption names Merlin and says he told no one where he went. The dominant turn: someone has carried a baby away in the night, and the man who did it isn't telling. Merlin stays small and faceless; the storm and the little hand own the page. The reader turns to learn whose baby it is.

## PAGE 2

Dramatic. Two winters later, King Uther lies dying in a dark stone chamber under a red-dragon coverlet, his lords crowding in the shadows at the foot of the bed. Merlin, standing over him, tells him to name an heir. Then the private moment: Uther pulls Merlin close and whispers about his son, Arthur, and Merlin answers that the boy is safe and hidden because the lords would kill him. Last, Uther lifts his head and tells the lords only that he has a son, and the closing caption says he died and none of them had ever heard of one. The dominant turn: the king has a son named Arthur, and only the wizard knows it. The whisper is the secret; the lords stay hungry shapes in the dark. The reader turns to find the boy.

## PAGE 3

Illustrated prose. The prose tells thirteen years in two fields: nobody found Uther's son, the lords fought over the crown, the fields went to weeds, Britain had a hundred swords and no king. The large image is a winter landscape from a hill, two armies clashing at a ford under different banners, a village burning, crows over empty fields, every figure small and faceless. Below and far off, the smaller image: a quiet little farm, smoke from its chimney, a boy carrying two buckets across the yard. The second field names Sir Ector's farm and a boy who never wondered about the lost son. The dominant turn: the lost heir is out there somewhere, and the page has just shown us a boy. War stays distant; the boy stays small and untouched. The reader turns to meet him.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. In Sir Ector's muddy farmyard, Arthur, a fair-haired farm boy in a patched brown tunic, polishes the shining new helmet of his brother Kay, newly knighted and showing off in black-and-yellow. Kay orders him about; Arthur teases back that Kay hasn't stopped talking since they knighted him. Then Ector strides through the gate waving a letter: a great tournament in London at New Year. Kay cheers; Arthur begs to come as Kay's squire; Ector says they'll all go, someone has to carry Kay's sword. The dominant turn: the farm boy is going to London. The brothers' teasing is warm, not cruel, and owns the top of the page; the letter is the hinge. The reader turns because the journey has begun.

## PAGE 5

Dramatic. Night in the forge shed, the fire down to red coals. Arthur, knees pulled up on a grain sack, asks Ector whether he will ever be a real knight. Ector looks into the coals instead of at him and promises only that Arthur will be whatever he was born to be. Arthur says that isn't an answer; Ector says it's the only one he has and sends him to bed. Last, Ector alone at the dying fire, big hands around his cup, looking at the door the boy went through, unhappy. The dominant turn: Ector knows something about Arthur, and he isn't saying. The first image, the two of them in the firelight, owns the page; the empty-doorway close is the quiet aftershock. The reader turns carrying a secret.

## PAGE 6

Illustrated prose. Snow falling on the road into London. Small on the road below ride Ector and Kay, with Arthur behind on a pony carrying Kay's sword and shield; ahead, filling the page, the city: an enormous crumbling Roman wall of grey stone banded with red brick, a great gate, and inside a crowd of roofs, towers, smoke and banners, riders and carts pouring through. The prose tells the six-day ride and how much bigger London is than anything Arthur imagined. Below, a close image of Arthur on his pony, snow in his hair, mouth open, and a last line: every lord in Britain wants to be king. The dominant turn: a farm boy has just ridden into a city full of men who all want the same crown. The city owns the page; the riders stay small.

## PAGE 7

Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from an old chronicle: warm ivory vellum, brown ink, watercolour wash, one red-and-lapis illuminated initial. The dominant image is a hand-drawn map of Britain about the year 500, Roman roads as straight red lines meeting at London, Hadrian's Wall across the north, green western hills, small oared ships crossing from the east. The first field opens with the page's question, who built the old walls and why Britain has no king, and answers it: four hundred years of Rome, then the soldiers left. The second field says Britain broke apart, new peoples came, hardly anyone wrote it down, and storytellers later put Uther, Merlin and a lost boy into that darkness. The dominant turn: the walls are real, the lost boy is legend, and the boy is about to ride through those walls. No story character appears.

## PAGE 8

Dramatic page. The great stone stands in a snowy London churchyard with a sword driven through an anvil and gold letters promising the crown to whoever pulls it out. King Lot, the strongest lord in the north, strains at the hilt and fails, then laughs it off as one of Merlin's tricks. Kay explains to Arthur who Lot is, both of them small at the edge of the crowd. The page belongs to the stone and its promise, but its turn is the last beat: Merlin, in the church doorway, answers Lot that the sword is waiting for someone else, and he is looking past Lot straight at the farm boy. The reader turns because the wizard has picked out Arthur. The crowd, the church, and the snow stay subordinate; nobody but Merlin should seem to notice Arthur.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic page. On the bright snowy tournament field, Kay, in his new mail and black-and-yellow surcoat, reaches for his sword before riding out and finds his scabbard empty. Arthur, holding the horse, reminds him he left it at the inn because it spoiled the look of his cloak. Kay, desperate not to be laughed at in front of every lord in Britain, grabs Arthur by the shoulders and sends him running; Arthur promises to be back before the trumpets. The page ends on Arthur sprinting flat out through the gate into the city. The one turn is that Arthur is racing the trumpets back to the inn. Kay's panic owns the dialogue; the tents, stands, and crowds stay a bright backdrop, and the gate is only a place to run through.

## PAGE 10

Illustrated-prose page. Arthur reaches the inn and finds it locked; the whole city has gone to the tournament, and the street is completely empty. He hammers on the door until his fists hurt, thinking of Kay being laughed at. Then his face changes as he looks up the street at the church tower over the roofs: there is a sword in the churchyard that nobody is using. The prose carries his thinking; the pictures carry the empty street and the moment the idea arrives. The turn is that Arthur is going to take the sword from the stone without knowing what that means. He does not think about the gold letters at all, and the page must not make him look solemn or chosen, only a boy solving a problem for his brother.

## PAGE 11

Spectacle page, one image. The churchyard is empty in falling snow. Arthur, alone, one foot on the stone, has drawn the sword straight up out of the anvil in a single easy pull, his body still in the motion, more surprised than strong. A shaft of pale winter light falls through the clouds onto the blade and the stone; snow flies off the anvil; the gold letters on the stone catch the light. One short caption tells how easily it came. The turn is that he has done the thing no lord in Britain could do, and nobody saw him. The emptiness of the churchyard is part of the event. The light is weather, not magic, and the boy must not look triumphant or heroic.

## PAGE 12

Dramatic page. Back at the tournament field, out of breath, Arthur hands Kay the sword hilt first, explaining that the inn was locked. Kay stares at the blade, the colour draining from his face, and asks where it came from; Arthur answers simply that it came from the stone and nobody was using it. Then Kay turns his back on his brother, holds the sword high, and shouts across the field to their father, Ector, turning in the crowd, that he is king of Britain. The dominant image is Kay's claim, with Arthur small and baffled behind him. The turn is that Kay is claiming the crown for himself. The crowd and the stands stay distant; Kay's shout is aimed at Ector, not at the spectators.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic page. In the churchyard, before the stone with its now-empty anvil, Ector holds a big leather-bound church book and makes Kay lay his hand on it and swear how he got the sword. After a long pause and a look at his brother, Kay tells the truth: Arthur gave it to him. Ector turns to Arthur, who, worried now, explains that he pulled it out because the inn was locked and Kay needed a sword, and asks if that was wrong. Ector tells him to put it back and pull it out again where he can see. The page is Ector's: grim, careful, testing. The turn is that Ector is going to watch Arthur do it. Kay's honesty under oath matters; the book is a prop, not a focus.

## PAGE 14

Spectacle page with a spoken turn. Arthur stands on the stone with the sword drawn a second time, holding it up, astonished at himself. Below him in the snow, Ector and Kay are down on their knees, heads bowed. Arthur begs them to get up; Ector refuses, calls him "my lord", and tells him he is not his father. Then a close view of Arthur's face, the sword forgotten in his hand, and one word: "What?" The kneeling image owns the page. The turn is that the man Arthur has called Father all his life has just told him he isn't. The stone and the snow are setting; the kneeling men and the boy's face are the event.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic page. Ector, still kneeling, takes Arthur's free hand in both of his and tells him plainly how Merlin brought a baby to his door fifteen years ago and told him never to ask whose he was, and he never asked. Arthur, very still, concludes he is nobody's son. Then he sits down hard on the edge of the stone with the sword across his knees, crying the way a boy cries when he is trying not to, while Kay, behind him, looks away. The dominant image is Arthur on the stone. The turn is that Arthur has won a kingdom and lost his family in one breath, and someone has to answer him. Grief owns the page; the sword lies across his knees as a burden, not a trophy.

## PAGE 16

Dramatic page. Ector gets up off his knees and puts his big hand on the back of Arthur's neck, the way a father does, and tells him he will always have them. Arthur asks if he can still call him Father; Ector answers, "When nobody's listening, lad. Always." Then Ector glances across at Kay, standing apart and red in the face, and asks the new king one thing: make Kay his steward, because no man alive will fight harder for him. Arthur, watching Kay, agrees, but says first he needs to talk to him. The dominant image is the father's hand and the boy's question. The turn is that Arthur still has a father, and now has to face the brother who tried to take the crown.

## PAGE 17

Dramatic page. The brothers face each other in the snow by the stone. Kay, unable to meet Arthur's eyes, admits that for about a minute he wanted the sword to be his. Arthur says he knows, and that Kay told the truth when it counted. Kay, embarrassed, tells him not to make a song of it. Then Arthur grips Kay's forearm in the knight's grip and swears Kay will be his steward as long as they both live; Kay, after a moment, grips back and looks at him at last, while Ector watches from behind. The dominant image is the grip. The turn is that the brothers are still brothers, and now the rest of Britain has to hear. Ector stays a quiet witness in the background.

## PAGE 18

Illustrated-prose page. Night, inside the cold dark church, one candle. Arthur sits alone on a bench with the sword across his knees, looking down at it, very small under a high window of snow-light. The prose tells what the day has done: this morning he was a squire who knew exactly who he was; by evening all London was whispering his name, and he does not know who his father was or why a wizard carried him off in the dark. A closer view of his hand on the blade carries the last thought, that tomorrow he must show the lords, and he does not think they will kneel. The turn is that tomorrow the lords decide what to do with him. Loneliness owns the page; the church stays plain and dark.

## PAGE 19

Chronicle page. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from a chronicle. Its question is the one the story has just raised: did the sword in the stone really happen? The one dominant image is a long timeline drawn as an unrolled scroll running down the page from about the year 500 to about 1500, almost all of it empty, with a small question mark and a tiny warrior near the top, a mark for a book of 1136 halfway down, and a small painting of the stone far down at about 1200. The fields explain that the oldest Arthur stories have no stone and that a French writer invented it around 1200. The turn is that the stone may be legend, but in the story the lords are about to see it. No story character appears.

## PAGE 20

Dramatic. The churchyard is packed with lords in furs and mail, and Arthur, still in his patched brown farm tunic, holds the sword up at the stone, drawn for the third time. The lords' faces show anger, not wonder. King Lot owns the page: he sneers that it is the wizard's trick, demands to know whose son the boy is, and calls him a squire from a pigsty. Arthur, red to the ears, shouts back that it was a good farm, and Lot turns it into a joke for the lords: the king of the hens. The dominant turn: the lords are laughing him out of the churchyard. Merlin watches from the church doorway and says nothing. The reader turns because the boy has done the impossible thing three times and the powerful men are still saying no.

## PAGE 21

Spectacle. The churchyard gate has burst open and the people of London pour in: smiths in leather aprons, farmers, bakers, washerwomen, boys on their fathers' shoulders, fists up, mouths open, an ocean of ordinary faces filling most of the page. High above them, small, the lords turn round in shock, and Arthur stands on the stone staring at the crowd. As the roar goes up, the sword in his hand flares with a sudden white light, and he looks down at it, startled. The page belongs to the people and their shout. The dominant turn: the lords said no, and the people said yes. A single caption tells us the people had been listening. The reader turns because the crowd has just chosen a king the lords refused.

## PAGE 22

Dramatic. Two moments in the great church. First, the crowning: in a tall stone church full of candles and crowds, Arthur kneels before the altar in a new deep-blue surcoat, a thin gold crown just set on his head by churchmen in white, his hand on the sword laid on the altar, and he swears to be a true king and protect all of them. A few lords at the front stand with arms folded, unsmiling. Then, close, the crown a little too big and tipped, sweat on his lip, Arthur turns on Merlin at his side: you know who my father was, tell me. Merlin promises: not here, tonight. The dominant turn: Arthur is king, and tonight he'll learn whose son he is. The reader turns waiting for that night.

## PAGE 23

Dramatic. The crowning feast in a long torchlit hall, tables full. At the high table sit Arthur in blue with his thin crown, Kay and Ector beside him, Merlin behind. The doors at the far end are flung open and King Lot strides in with his lords, an open chest at his feet spilling gold cups and silver onto the floor. Arthur, eager, welcomes him and asks if his gifts arrived. Lot owns the rest of the page: he kicks the chest so the cups roll, says they don't take presents from a boy nobody has heard of, and then, grinning, hand on his sword hilt, offers a gift of his own: a sharp sword, coming for Arthur's neck. The dominant turn: the most powerful lord in Britain has just threatened the king at his own feast.

## PAGE 24

Dramatic. The hall has gone silent. Merlin has stepped forward from behind the high table, staff raised, and he owns the page: he tells Lot to be quiet and listen, and announces to everyone that this boy is the son of King Uther, and that he was there when Uther said he had a son. Lot stands in the middle of the floor, facing him. Behind Merlin, Arthur is half-risen from his chair, hearing it at the same moment as everyone else. Then Lot, at the doors, laughs it off, mocks the wizard's sword tricks, tells the boy to come down from his chair, and leaves with his lords, promising the battlefield. The dominant turn: the whole hall has just heard whose son Arthur is, before Arthur heard it himself. The reader turns to see what Arthur does with that.

## PAGE 25

Dramatic. After the feast, the hall is empty, the torches low, cups knocked over. Arthur, crown pulled off and gripped in his fist, faces Merlin across the high table and accuses him: you swore I'd hear it first. Merlin explains why he kept quiet in the churchyard and why he spoke at the feast. Arthur's anger deepens: you took me from my father and let Ector raise me on a lie. Merlin, not sorry, leaning on his staff and meeting his eyes, says the lords would have drowned him in a well, and he would do it again. That exchange is the heart of the page. Then the anger goes out of Arthur and he asks, quietly, whether his father ever saw him. Once, the night he was born; he named him. The dominant turn: Arthur has his father's name now, and a war to fight for it.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. Still the empty hall. Arthur has sat down at the high table, crown in his lap, hands shaking. He asks about his mother, and Merlin puts it off for another night. Arthur admits Lot is right about one thing: he is a squire who has never been in a real fight. Kay, just come in and leaning on the table, agrees that Arthur's fighting is terrible, and then says he will be next to him, which makes it less terrible. The heart of the page is Merlin leaning down close, tapping the crown in Arthur's lap: when the people shouted your name in the snow, did anyone ask how many fights you'd won? No. Then don't ask it yourself; Lot is coming for them too. Arthur looks up at Merlin, then at Kay: then we fight him, all of us, together. The dominant turn: the boy has decided to go to war.

## PAGE 27

Chronicle. The book steps out of the story onto a page of warm ivory vellum to answer a question the reader has been holding: was there a real King Arthur, and did he really fight wars like this? The dominant image is two old manuscripts painted side by side as museum studies, a long empty gap between them crossed by a thin ruled line, and below them a small ink drawing of a lonely green hill with a question mark over it. One field tells what the monk Gildas wrote around 540, a great battle at Mount Badon and no Arthur named; the other tells what a book written nearly three hundred years later says, twelve battles won by Arthur. Short labels name each manuscript and the hill nobody can find. The dominant turn: real or not, in the story Arthur has to get through the night before his first battle.

## PAGE 28

Illustrated prose. The night before the first battle, Arthur sits awake in the mouth of a small tent above a dark valley. On his side burn a handful of campfires where smiths, farmers, and bakers' boys sit with spears and pitchforks; across the valley King Lot's fires burn in rows over the hill, far more of them. The prose carries what he is thinking: these people chose him, and some will not come home. Then, in grey light before dawn, he pulls on his own mail with nobody to help him. Arthur owns the page, and he is alone in it. The dominant turn: the battle is at dawn. The reader turns to see it begin. The tents, the weapons, and the far enemy stay subordinate to the boy awake in the tent; the scale of the two camps is felt, not counted.

## PAGE 29

Spectacle. Dawn at Bedegraine: a forest valley with frost on the grass and mist along a stream. On the far slope stands Lot's army, a wall of mailed riders under many banners with his black bear banner in the middle. On the near slope stands Arthur's army, a thin line of a few knights and a great many townspeople with spears and pitchforks, the low sun behind them. At its front, small but clear, Arthur sits a grey horse in his blue surcoat, sword drawn, with Kay in black and yellow beside him on a black horse. The page belongs to the valley and the mismatch between the two armies. The dominant turn: two armies are about to collide, and one of them is mostly farmers. The reader turns to see the collision. No single face carries this page, and no fighting has started yet.

## PAGE 30

Dramatic. Arthur raises his sword, shouts for the army to follow, and charges; Kay grabs for his reins, misses, and shouts at him to wait. From high above, the dominant image shows the mistake: one small blue rider galloping across the stream far ahead of his own army, straight into Lot's riders, who close around him like water. Then his horse goes down in the stream with a great splash, and Arthur is thrown, still holding the sword. Arthur owns the page through his own recklessness, and Kay's warning is the voice he ignored. The dominant turn: the king is on the ground in the middle of the enemy, alone. The reader turns to find out whether he survives. Nobody is hurt on the page, and the horse's fall is a splash, not an injury.

## PAGE 31

Dramatic. Arthur stands in the shallow stream with his sword up while enemy riders and spears close in around him. Then Kay bursts into the ring on his black horse, shouting who he is and that this is his brother. Kay hauls Arthur up behind his saddle and wheels back toward their own people, scolding him; Arthur answers that Kay came alone too, and Kay gets the last, dry word. Kay owns the page: the jealous brother rides into an army for the king. The dominant turn: Arthur is back with his own people, and King Lot is coming for him. The reader turns to see Lot's charge. The enemy riders stay faceless and subordinate. No blow lands on the page, and the brothers' exchange is warm and funny under the danger.

## PAGE 32

Dramatic. Arthur stands on foot in the front rank among smiths and farmers, their spears braced, as Lot charges down at him, mocking him as a farm boy. Arthur shouts to his people to hold. The charge hits and the line does not break, and in the middle of it, among his people, Arthur raises the sword from the stone and it blazes with white light. Lot's horse rears, his riders shield their eyes, and his army turns and runs while Lot shouts back that this isn't over. Arthur owns the page, and the blazing sword raised among ordinary people is its heart. The dominant turn: Lot's army has broken, and Arthur has won. The reader turns to see what winning looks like. The rout stays small and distant, and no one falls on the page.

## PAGE 33

Spectacle. At noon under a grey sky the mist is gone and the valley shows what the battle cost: riderless horses standing with their heads down, dropped shields, broken spears, torn banners in the stream, crows on the far hill, and townspeople helping the wounded walk. In the middle, small, Arthur stands alone with his helmet in his hand and his blond hair blowing, looking at all of it. No bodies and no blood are shown; the cost is in the empty things and in the living. Arthur owns the page by being alone in the middle of it. The dominant turn: he has won, and it doesn't look like winning. The reader turns to find out what Arthur makes of it. The wreckage is quiet aftermath, not horror, and nothing in it competes with the lone figure.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. Arthur kneels in the grass, holding a water-skin to the mouth of a wounded young smith, while Merlin leans on his staff above them. Arthur asks why winning feels like this, and Merlin says it is because he is looking at what it cost, and that this is good. Then, quietly and without looking up, Arthur admits he charged alone and would be dead if Kay hadn't come. Merlin tells him to remember it, because he may not always be there to remind him. Arthur and Merlin share the page, and its weight is Arthur's quiet confession. The dominant turn: Arthur has learned the lesson, and Merlin is afraid he won't keep it. The reader turns to see whether the lesson holds. The wounded smith is cared for and shows no wound.

## PAGE 35

Illustrated prose. Arthur makes his court at Camelot, a pale castle on a green hill above a river, where knights ride up the road to serve the king who beat Lot. That establishes the place. The dominant image is inside the hall at night: a minstrel with a harp sings by the great hearth to a crowd of knights, and the painted hanging behind him shows a boy king in shining plate charging alone with a blazing sword, armour Arthur has never owned. At the edge of the firelight Arthur leans on a pillar, listening and smiling. The prose says that nobody in the songs was called Kay, and that Arthur began to believe them. Arthur's smile owns the page. The dominant turn: Arthur has forgotten the lesson. The reader turns because something bad is coming. The minstrel and the crowd stay subordinate to Arthur.

## PAGE 36

Chronicle. The story stops and the reader steps onto a page of an old chronicle. The page opens with the question: in the songs Arthur wore shining armour, so what did warriors in his time really look like? The one dominant image is two warriors painted side by side as costume studies: on the left a warrior of about 500 in a long mail shirt, round wooden shield, spear, and Roman-style ridge helmet; on the right a knight of about 1200, as this book draws Arthur, in mail, a red surcoat, and a great helm; behind him, faint, the ghost outline of a knight in plate from Malory's own century. The fields explain why every age dressed Arthur in its own armour. The dominant turn: back in the story, the boy king in mail is about to go looking for trouble. No story character appears; the chronicle owns the page.

## PAGE 37

Dramatic. Morning in Camelot's hall. Kay, now the king's steward in black with a yellow border, brings news: a knight has pitched his tent beside a fountain in the forest, fights everyone who passes, and has knocked three of Arthur's knights into the water. Arthur, leaning forward in his chair, wants to ride out and fight him himself. Kay refuses flatly, because kings send knights; Arthur answers with the songs; Kay has the last word and walks away. Kay owns the talk, and Arthur owns the ending: he goes quiet with his jaw set. The one dominant turn: Arthur has been told no, and he isn't going to listen. The reader turns to see what he does about it. The hall is a plain daylight setting; the news, the refusal, and Arthur's set jaw carry the page.

## PAGE 38

Illustrated prose. Night in the great oak forest. Arthur has slipped out of the sleeping castle alone, and he rides his grey horse down a moonlit road in mail and his blue surcoat, his helmet hanging from the saddle, grinning up at the moon. The prose lets the reader feel his happiness and hear the pride under it: no steward, no wizard, only the moon and a sword that beat an army, and a song about tonight already made up in his head. Below, far ahead through the trees, firelight glows beside a glint of water. The one dominant turn: he's reached the fountain, and someone is waiting there. Arthur's joy owns the page; the fire stays small and distant, a promise rather than a person.

## PAGE 39

Dramatic. A moonlit clearing with an old stone fountain, a battered green-and-white pavilion, and a dented shield hanging from an oak. King Pellinore rises from his fire, the biggest man in the book, both hands on a huge sword planted point-down, and states his rule: nobody passes without fighting him. Arthur, on horseback, plays his crown. Pellinore names himself, says he has never yielded to any man, and settles his great helm on his head; Arthur answers with one eager word. Pellinore's size and calm own the page, and Arthur's eagerness is the spark. The one dominant turn: the boy king is about to fight the biggest man he has ever seen. The reader turns for the fight.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic, nearly silent. The joust: two riders crash together and both lances shatter. Then the two men on foot by the pool, Arthur smaller and attacking, Pellinore planted. Then the moment that owns the page: the sword from the stone meets Pellinore's huge blade and snaps in two, the broken half spinning away through the moonlight toward the pool, and Arthur's face goes blank with disbelief as he cries out. The one dominant turn: the sword that made Arthur king is broken, and he has nothing left to fight with. The joust and the circling are quick lead-ins; the break and Arthur's face are what the reader must see. Nobody is wounded.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Arthur lies on his back in the wet grass, the broken hilt still in his fist, with Pellinore's sword point at his throat. Pellinore offers him his life for one word; Arthur refuses, because kings don't yield. Pellinore, sorry for it, lifts the sword in both hands. Then, out of the dark behind him, come a long hand with a silver ring, two fingers pointing, the tip of a crooked staff, and the end of a forked white beard, and a single quiet command. The giant topples like a felled oak, his eyes closed. The one dominant turn: Merlin has stopped the killing blow, and Arthur doesn't yet know how. The pinned boy owns the page; Merlin is only a hand and a voice; no blow lands.

## PAGE 42

Dramatic. Arthur scrambles up in a fury, sure that Merlin has killed the bravest knight he ever fought with a spell; Merlin, walking into the firelight, tells him the giant is only asleep and snoring. Then Arthur sinks to his knees holding the two halves of the sword from the stone, and Merlin, standing over him, convicts him with three questions: who told you not to come, who offered to let you walk home, who was too proud to take it. Arthur answers each, and the last answer is himself. The kneeling boy with the broken sword owns the page. The one dominant turn: Arthur has lost the sword from the stone, and he knows whose fault it is. Pellinore stays a sleeping shape in the background.

## PAGE 43

Illustrated prose. Dawn mist at the forest's edge. Arthur walks out leading his horse, the two halves of the broken sword held against his chest; Merlin walks a few steps ahead with his staff; behind them, Pellinore's fire is dying in the trees. The prose carries Arthur's shame plainly: Merlin's following him was the only reason he is alive, he nearly died for a song, and the sword that chose him lies broken because he crept out. Then Merlin, half turned, says there is another sword, and ahead of them white mist lies over open water. The one dominant turn: there's another sword, and it's somewhere in that mist. The walk owns the page; the mist is the hook.

## PAGE 44

A spectacle page. Arthur and Merlin come out of the forest onto the shore of a lake that looks like another world: still water, white mist, silver hills, no warmth anywhere. Out of the very middle of the lake rises a woman's arm in white silk, holding a long bright sword straight up in a dark-red scabbard. The arm and the sword own the page; Arthur and Merlin are tiny on the shore, and Arthur's blue is the only warm colour in all that grey. The dominant turn: the other sword is right there. The reader turns to find out who is holding it. What stays subordinate: the reeds, the far hills, and the two small figures, who give the scale and nothing more; the broken sword is not part of this picture.

## PAGE 45

A dramatic page. Merlin warns Arthur to be very polite, and the Lady of the Lake walks toward them across the water as if it were a floor: tall, still, black hair to her knees, white samite. She owns the page. She names Arthur as Uther's son, names the sword Excalibur, and offers it for a gift to be named later, whatever she asks. Arthur promises at once, and Merlin grabs his arm too late, appalled that the boy has promised anything at all without knowing what. The dominant turn: Arthur has made a promise he doesn't understand, and the sword is waiting. The reader turns because she has told him to take the boat. What stays subordinate: the small boat in the reeds and the far arm still holding the sword.

## PAGE 46

A spectacle page, almost silent. Arthur stands in the small wooden boat in the middle of the misty lake and reaches up with both hands to take the sword and its dark-red scabbard from the white-sleeved hand; far off, the Lady stands on the water, small and white, watching. Then the empty hand lets go and sinks into the still water. Arthur owns the page in the moment of taking. The dominant turn: Arthur has Excalibur. The reader turns to see what he does with it. What stays subordinate: the boat, the ripples, and the Lady, who is a distant witness; nobody speaks, and the broken sword plays no part here.

## PAGE 47

A dramatic page on the lakeshore. Arthur has drawn Excalibur and holds it up in pure delight; Merlin asks which he likes better, the sword or the scabbard, and Arthur picks the sword without a thought. Merlin calls him a fool: whoever wears the scabbard loses no blood. Then, slowly, Arthur lays the bright sword on the grass and buckles the empty scabbard on first, looking at it, while Merlin raises one eyebrow and allows that he may live to be old. Arthur's change of mind owns the page, and the joke is Merlin's. The dominant turn: Arthur has chosen the thing that keeps him safe, and on the way home he will have to choose again. What stays subordinate: the lake behind them, which is only a cold backdrop now.

## PAGE 48

A dramatic page in the fountain clearing by daylight. Pellinore, awake and enormous, blocks the road with his sword planted point-down and offers to try the new sword. Arthur's hand goes to Excalibur's hilt, then deliberately leaves it and rests on the scabbard: with that on, he cannot bleed, so a fight would be cheating. Then he holds out his open hand and invites Pellinore to Camelot, because he needs knights strong enough to tell him no. Pellinore stares at the hand, suspicious, and says he'll think about it. Arthur owns the page; the dominant turn: Arthur turned down a fight he couldn't lose, and asked his enemy to join him. The reader turns to see whether Pellinore comes. What stays subordinate: Merlin, silent behind Arthur, and the pavilion.

## PAGE 49

A dramatic page at the gate of Camelot at dusk, torches being lit. Kay is in the gateway, unshaven and rumpled, plainly awake all night, and complains that he hates waiting. Arthur gets down and holds out the two halves of the sword from the stone to Kay, hilt first, the way he once handed Kay a sword at the tournament, and says Kay was right. Kay answers that he is always right and it's exhausting, takes the halves to carry for him, and then notices the red scabbard. Arthur asks him to keep telling him no; Kay says every day. The brothers own the page, and the handing-over is its heart. The dominant turn: the brothers are square again, and Arthur has a new sword nobody at court has seen. What stays subordinate: Merlin, the horses, and the castle gate.

## PAGE 50

A chronicle page: the reader steps out of the story onto a vellum page of an old chronicle, in ink and watercolour. The question it opens with is the one the story just raised: two magic swords? The answer: yes, in this story, and the famous one's name is far older than the stone, changing from tongue to tongue until it became Excalibur. Old books told the stone sword and Excalibur as one sword or as two, and this book chose two so that Arthur could lose the first. The dominant image is a family tree of names growing up from a sword, one branch tangling with a little drawing of the stone and anvil. The dominant turn: whatever its name, the king now has his sword, and a hall full of knights to keep in order. What stays subordinate: decoration; no story character appears.

## PAGE 51

Dramatic. At the Pentecost feast at Camelot the knights are on their feet along a long table, shoving and shouting over which seats sit nearest the king, and Kay, now the harried steward, rushes to Arthur to say it is the seats again. Down the table a knight has drawn a knife on another. Then the page belongs to Arthur: on his feet at the head of the table, he brings Excalibur's pommel down on the boards so hard the cups jump, and every knight freezes while he forbids anyone to bleed in his hall over a chair. The dominant turn is the king stopping the fight, but not the reason for it. The reader turns to see how he will fix the reason. The quarrelling crowd and the knife stay small and subordinate; the blow and Arthur's face own the page.

## PAGE 52

Dramatic. Late that night in the empty hall, Arthur, Merlin and Kay stand by the hearth. Arthur, grinning now, turns to Merlin and explains it, and the old wizard admits it has never been tried. The dominant turn is Arthur deciding to build the table; the page belongs to his face catching the idea from the shield. ## PAGE 53

Illustrated prose. ## PAGE 55

Dramatic. King Lot appears in the hall doors under a white flag of truce, two lords behind him, to see the boy's wonder for himself. Dramatic. All the knights stand around the round table holding their swords upright by the blades so the hilts make crosses. Dramatic. Kay points across the table at the one chair with no name in front of it and offers to sit in it himself, so it will not look as if someone refused Arthur. Chronicle. The reader steps out of the story onto a page from a chronicle, which asks who made up the story of King Arthur and whether the Round Table is real.
