# Full Script — King Arthur, Volume I: The Sword and the Table

**Owner-controlled.** Production roles never edit this file. No compensation
number, generation note, or critic suggestion is ever written into it.

**How to read this document.** Each page is written as a scene first. The spec
under it is three lines and no more: mode (in the header), location, and why
the reader turns the page. Panel notes are staging, not metadata. **If the
metadata for a page is longer than the page's dramatized content, the story is
not being written.** Chronicle pages (Mode 4) add two non-rendering lines:
the reader's **Question** and the **Sources**.

**Text discipline.** Every string between quotation marks in a speaker,
`PROSE FIELD`, `CHRONICLE FIELD`, `LABEL`, `CAPTION`, `SOUND`, or
printed-object line renders exactly as written. Nothing else in this document
renders. `tools/script_audit.py` and `tools/verify.py` parse this format, so
keep it exact.

# MOVEMENT I — THE HIDDEN KING · pp 1–7

---

## PAGE 1 · *spectacle*

**Location:** the cliff path below a castle, night, storm.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 100%.** A black castle on a sea cliff, high in the top
third, one window lit. Rain in slanting sheets, waves bursting white on the
rocks far below. Down a narrow switchback path cut into the cliff comes one
tall, thin, stooped figure in a dark hooded robe — MERLIN, seen small, his
face hidden — leaning on a crooked staff taller than himself, holding a
bundle against his chest under his cloak. A small lantern hangs from the
staff and throws the only warm light on the page, onto the bundle: a tiny
hand reaching out of the cloth.

> **TITLE CARD** *(top, over the storm sky, above the castle)*
> "KING ARTHUR
>
> The Sword and the Table"

> **CAPTION** *(lower left, over dark rock)*
> "On a night of storm, an old man named Merlin carried a newborn baby away from the castle, and told no one where."

**Why you turn the page:** someone has carried a baby away in the night, and the man who did it isn't telling.

## PAGE 2 · *dramatic*

**Location:** King Uther's chamber, night.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** A dark stone chamber lit by one brazier. A great
bed with a red-and-gold coverlet embroidered with a red dragon. In it, KING
UTHER: gaunt, hollow-cheeked, long thin grey hair on the pillow, a long grey
moustache and no beard. Crowded at the foot of the bed in shadow, a dozen
lords in furs and mail, leaning in, hungry and anxious. At the bedside,
standing over the king, MERLIN, hood back now: tall, very thin, a long
hooked nose, wild white hair, a long forked white beard, leaning on his
staff.

> **CAPTION** *(top left, over the dark wall)*
> "Two winters later, King Uther lay dying."
>
> **MERLIN:** "Your lords are frightened, Uther. They want to know who will be king when you are gone. Tell them."

**Panel 2 — 30%.** Close on the bed. Uther grips Merlin's sleeve and pulls
him down; Merlin bends so close that his beard touches the pillow. The lords
behind are out of focus, out of earshot. Uther on the left, Merlin on the
right.

> **UTHER:** "Is he safe? My son? My Arthur?"
>
> **MERLIN:** "Safe, and far from here. If your lords knew where, he wouldn't live a week."

**Panel 3 — 20%.** Uther, with the last of his strength, lifts his head
toward the lords.

> **UTHER:** "I have a son. He will be king after me. He has my blessing."
>
> **CAPTION** *(across the bottom of the panel)*
> "King Uther died that night. Not one of his lords had ever heard of a son."

**Why you turn the page:** the king has a son named Arthur, and only the wizard knows it.

## PAGE 3 · *illustrated prose*

**Location:** Britain, over thirteen years.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A wide winter landscape seen from a hill:
two lords' armies clashing at a river ford under different banners, a
village burning behind them, crows over empty fields gone to weeds. Small,
far, nobody's face.

**Panel 2 — 35%.** Far below and far away, in the lower right, a small
timber-and-stone farm in a muddy yard, smoke curling from its chimney, a
boy carrying two buckets across the yard. Peaceful. Untouched.

> **PROSE FIELD 1** *(top of the page, over pale winter sky)*
> "Nobody ever found King Uther's son. Merlin would not say where he was, and after a while the lords stopped asking. Each of them wanted the crown for himself.
>
> For thirteen years they fought over it, lord against lord and castle against castle, while the fields went to weeds and the roads filled with robbers. Britain had a hundred swords and no king."

> **PROSE FIELD 2** *(lower left, over the plain winter field beside the farm)*
> "And far from all of it, on a muddy little farm that belonged to an old knight called Sir Ector, a boy grew up who had never once wondered about King Uther's lost son."

**Why you turn the page:** the lost heir is out there somewhere, and the page has just shown us a boy.

## PAGE 4 · *dramatic*

**Location:** Sir Ector's farmyard, autumn.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 50%.** A muddy farmyard with a forge glowing in an

> **KAY:** "Polish it properly, squire. A knight's helmet should shine so bright it blinds his enemies."
>
> **ARTHUR:** "Your enemies won't need to see you, Kay. They'll hear you coming. You haven't stopped talking since they knighted you."

**Panel 2 — 25%.** Through the gate strides SIR ECTOR: short, broad,
barrel-bodied, bald on top with a grey fringe, round ruddy clean-shaven face,
a russet tunic, waving a letter with a red seal.

> **ECTOR:** "Kay! Arthur! A rider from London. There's to be a great tournament at New Year, and every knight in Britain is called."

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Kay punches the air on the left. Arthur, on the right,
jumps up, the helmet forgotten in his hands, looking at Ector.

> **KAY:** "My first tournament!"
>
> **ARTHUR:** "Can I come? As Kay's squire? Please, Father."
>
> **ECTOR:** "We'll all go. Somebody has to carry your brother's sword."

**Why you turn the page:** the farm boy is going to London.

**Panel 3 — 25%.** Arthur has gone. Ector sits alone at the dying fire, his

## PAGE 7 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Who built those old walls, and why is Britain full of lords fighting each other instead of having one king?

**Sources:** Roman rule of Britain AD 43–c. 410 (Zosimus on the letter of Honorius, c. 410); the Roman walls of Londinium (c. AD 200); Hadrian's Wall (AD 122); Gildas, *De Excidio Britanniae* (c. 540) on the coming of the Saxons; Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* (731) for Angles, Saxons, and Jutes.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 70%.** A page of a chronicle on warm ivory vellum with
a ruled margin. The dominant image is a hand-drawn ink-and-watercolour map of
the island of Britain about the year 500: Roman roads as straight red lines
meeting at London; Hadrian's Wall drawn across the north; the western hills
shaded green; small oared ships crossing from the east.
A red-and-lapis illuminated initial with a touch of gold begins the first
field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(left of the map, on plain vellum)*
> "Who built those old walls, and why does Britain have no king? Here is something the story doesn't tell you. For almost four hundred years, Britain was part of the Roman Empire. The Romans built its roads, its towns, and London's walls. Then, around the year 410, Rome was in trouble at home. The soldiers left Britain, and they never came back."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(below the map, on plain vellum)*
> "Without Rome, Britain broke into small kingdoms, each fighting the others. Across the sea came new people, the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, first to raid and then to stay. Hardly anyone wrote down what happened next. Into that real darkness, storytellers later put a king called Uther, a wizard called Merlin, and a lost boy. That part is legend."

> **LABEL** *(on the map, along the red lines)*
> "Roman roads"

> **LABEL** *(on the map, across the north)*
> "Hadrian's Wall"

> **LABEL** *(on the map, at the meeting of the roads)*
> "London"

> **LABEL** *(on the map, beside the ships)*
> "Saxon ships"

**Why you turn the page:** the reader knows now that the crumbling walls are real and the lost boy is legend, and the boy is about to ride through those walls.

## PAGE 8 · *dramatic*

**Location:** the churchyard in London, snow.

**Panel 1 — dominant, 55%.** A snowy churchyard in front of a squat stone
church. In the middle, a great grey block of stone with an iron anvil on top,
and driven down through the anvil into the stone, a long plain sword with a
dark iron cross-guard and a round pommel. Letters of gold are cut into the
front of the stone. A ring of lords and townspeople watches. At the stone,
straining at the hilt with both hands, his face red: KING LOT OF ORKNEY, big
and heavy-shouldered, a long copper-red beard in two braids with iron rings,
a black bearskin cloak, a gold torc at his neck. At the far left edge of the
crowd, small: Ector, Kay, and Arthur, watching.

> **CAPTION** *(top left, over the church wall)*
> "On Christmas morning, a great stone had appeared in the churchyard with a sword through it. Every lord who came for the tournament had tried to pull it out."

> **PRINTED STONE** *(the gold letters on the stone, large and plain)*
> "WHOEVER PULLS THIS SWORD OUT OF THIS STONE IS THE TRUE-BORN KING OF ALL BRITAIN"

**Panel 2 — 15%.** Kay, leaning to Arthur, both on the left.

> **KAY:** "That's King Lot of Orkney. The strongest lord in the north. If he can't move it, nobody can."

**Panel 3 — 15%.** Lot lets go of the hilt and laughs, arms wide to the
crowd.

> **LOT:** "Ha! It's one of Merlin's tricks. That old wizard loves a trick with a sword. There's no man alive who could pull that out."

**Panel 4 — 15%.** In the church doorway, MERLIN, leaning on his staff. He
is not looking at Lot. He is looking past him, at the edge of the crowd,
where Arthur is.

> **MERLIN:** "There is one, King Lot. The sword is waiting for him, not for you."

**Why you turn the page:** the wizard is looking straight at the farm boy.

## PAGE 50 · *chronicle*

**Location:** the chronicle frame.

**Question:** Why does Arthur get a second magic sword, and where does the name Excalibur come from?

**Sources:** *Culhwch and Olwen* (Welsh, c. 1100) — Caledfwlch; Geoffrey of Monmouth, *History of the Kings of Britain* (c. 1136) — Caliburnus; Old French romances (late 12th–13th c.) — Escalibor; the Vulgate *Merlin* identifies the stone sword with Excalibur; Malory, *Le Morte d'Arthur* I.9 calls the stone sword Excalibur, breaks Arthur's sword against Pellinore (I.23), and has the Lady give Excalibur (I.25).

**Panel 1 — dominant, 65%.** A chronicle page on ivory vellum. The dominant
image is a family tree of names drawn in brown ink: at its root, a long
sword painted in watercolour lying across the page; branches growing up from
it, each ending in a small round frame holding one name; one branch crosses
over and tangles with a little drawing of the stone and anvil. An illuminated
initial begins the first field.

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 1** *(top of the page)*
> "Two magic swords? In this story, yes: the sword from the stone, and Excalibur. Arthur's famous sword is older than his stone. In a Welsh tale from around 1100, it is called Caledfwlch. Geoffrey of Monmouth, writing in 1136, called it Caliburnus. French poets made it Escalibor, and English storytellers made that Excalibur."

> **CHRONICLE FIELD 2** *(bottom of the page)*
> "When the sword in the stone came along, some old books made it the same sword as Excalibur, and some made them two; storytellers have told it both ways for eight hundred years. Sir Thomas Malory does both! This book chose two swords, so that Arthur could lose the first one."

> **LABEL** *(in the lowest frame)*
> "Caledfwlch, about 1100"

> **LABEL** *(in the middle frame)*
> "Caliburnus, 1136"

> **LABEL** *(in the top frame)*
> "Excalibur"

**Why you turn the page:** whatever its name, the king now has his sword, and a hall full of knights to keep in order.

## PAGE 59 · *chronicle*
