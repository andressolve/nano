# Volume Architecture — 54 pages

Britain in the age of legend, drawn as the storytellers of about 1200 imagined
it. About sixteen years, with one jump of fifteen in the first movement and a
few months after that. Four places matter: Sir Ector's farm, London and its
churchyard, the forest with its fountain and its lake, and Camelot.

Story pages are numbered with the book; the six Chronicle pages (C) sit at the
chapter breaks and are part of the page count.

---

## I — THE HIDDEN KING · pp 1–6

**Emotional entry:** secrecy. **Exit:** London's walls in the snow, and a boy
who has never been anywhere.

A storm, a cliff, an old man carrying a newborn bundle down a path to the
sea. Then a dying king in a dark hall, lords crowding his bed
demanding to know who rules after him, and the old man, Merlin, at his
shoulder: *Name him.* Uther names his son Arthur, gives him his blessing, and
dies, and not one lord in the room has ever heard of a son. Fifteen years pass
in a page: the lords fight each other for a crown that nobody can claim, and
the land goes hungry. On a farm far from all of it, a boy of fifteen polishes
his big brother's brand-new armour. Kay was knighted yesterday and won't let
anyone forget it; Arthur is his squire and would give anything to be what Kay
is. A rider brings news of a great tournament in London at New Year. Ector
says the family will go. That night Arthur asks the question he has never
asked out loud — *will I ever be a knight?* — and Ector, who knows exactly who
Arthur is, gives him an answer that is true and isn't the truth. The family
rides through the snow to London.

**The turn:** a king names an heir nobody has seen; fifteen years later the
heir is a squire who doesn't know it.
**Page-turn out:** they arrive in the biggest city Arthur has ever seen, which
has no king.

**Chronicle 1 · p 7 — "Why doesn't Britain have a king?"** The Romans ruled
Britain for almost four hundred years and left around 410; their roads and
walls stayed and crumbled; the island broke into small kingdoms; Saxons,
Angles, and Jutes crossed the sea to raid and then to settle. Map of Britain
about 500 AD with the Roman roads, the British kingdoms in the west, and the
newcomers' landings in the east. Honest about how little anyone wrote down.

## II — THE SWORD IN THE STONE · pp 8–16

**Emotional entry:** wonder. **Exit:** a king alone at night with a sword,
who was somebody's son this morning.

In a churchyard, a block of stone with an iron anvil on it and a sword driven
through the anvil into the stone, and golden letters: whoever pulls it out is
the true-born king. Lord after lord has heaved at it. King Lot of Orkney, the
proudest, tries in front of everyone, fails, and laughs it off as one of
Merlin's tricks — Merlin, watching, says it will wait for the right hand. On
tournament morning Kay finds he has left his sword at their lodging and sends
Arthur running for it. The inn is locked; everyone is at the tournament.
Arthur, desperate that his brother will be shamed, remembers the sword in the
churchyard. Alone, he pulls it out as if it were stuck in snow. He brings it
to Kay. Kay knows that sword. He goes white — and then shouts to his father
that *he* is king. Ector takes both boys back to the stone and makes Kay swear
how he got it. Kay, to his credit, tells the truth. Ector makes Arthur put the
sword back and draw it again. He does. Ector and Kay kneel. Arthur, horrified,
begs his father to get up — and Ector tells him he is not his father. Merlin
brought him as a baby; nobody knew whose. Arthur's grief: he has gained a
kingdom and lost his family in the same breath. Ector asks one thing of the
new king: make Kay his steward, for Kay's sake. Arthur promises, *as long as
we both live.* That night Arthur sits alone with the sword.

**The turn:** the moment Arthur becomes king is the moment Ector kneels and
stops being his father.
**Page-turn out:** in the morning he has to tell the lords of Britain.

**Chronicle 2 · p 17 — "Did the sword in the stone really happen?"** The
oldest stories of Arthur have no sword in a stone. It first appears in a poem
by Robert de Boron, around 1200 — about seven hundred years after any real
Arthur could have lived. A timeline drawn as a scroll, mostly empty, with the
stone arriving very late. The idea of a sign that chooses the king, and why
storytellers liked it.

## III — THE KINGS WHO LAUGHED · pp 18–24

**Emotional entry:** humiliation. **Exit:** a boy king the night before a
battle he could lose.

Before the lords of Britain, Arthur draws the sword again. They refuse him
anyway: a farm squire nobody has heard of, who was brought by a wizard. Lot
says it for all of them. Then the crowd outside — the farmers, the smiths, the
ordinary people — begins to shout: *We will have Arthur!* They will have no
other king. He is crowned, too young for the crown, sweating under it, and
swears to be a true king to them, and means it, and is terrified. At the
feast, Lot and the kings ride in. Arthur, trying to be a king, sends them
gifts. They send them back with a message: the only gift they'll give him is a
sword between his neck and his shoulders. Merlin stands up and tells the hall
whose son Arthur is. Lot laughs at a wizard's word and declares war: come
down from that chair, boy, or be pulled down. Afterwards, alone with Merlin
and Kay, Arthur shakes. They're right, he says; he's a squire. Merlin: the
sword didn't choose a squire, it chose a king who hasn't happened yet. Arthur
decides to fight. The night before battle, he can't sleep.

**The turn:** the people choose him and the kings refuse him; there will be
war.
**Page-turn out:** dawn over two armies.

**Chronicle 3 · p 25 — "Was there a real King Arthur?"** Nobody knows.
Around 540 a monk named Gildas wrote about a great British victory over the
Saxons at a place called Mount Badon — and named no Arthur. Nearly three
hundred years later, a book called the *History of the Britons* lists twelve
battles won by a war-leader called Arthur, Badon among them. That's all. The
page shows both manuscripts as museum studies, side by side, and the gap
between them.

## IV — THE FIRST BATTLE · pp 26–32

**Emotional entry:** fear. **Exit:** a king who believes the songs about him.

Dawn in a forest valley: Lot's banners on one side, Arthur's army of
townsmen and a few knights on the other. Arthur charges first and too far,
alone, with Kay shouting at him to wait. He is unhorsed and surrounded. Kay
rides in alone and gets him out, roaring that he is the king's steward. Back
in the saddle, Arthur meets Lot himself, and when he raises the sword from the
stone it blazes with light like thirty torches, and Lot's men break and run.
Lot, retreating, swears it isn't over. Then the field after: empty saddles,
dropped shields, the wounded. Arthur, with Merlin, asks why winning feels like
this. Arthur makes his court at Camelot, a castle on a hill with
a river below it. And the songs begin — about the boy king whose sword
shines — and by the end of the winter Arthur has started to believe them.

**The turn:** Arthur wins his first battle, because his brother saved him, and
learns the wrong lesson.
**Page-turn out:** a king who thinks his sword makes him unbeatable.

**Chronicle 4 · p 33 — "What did knights really look like?"** Two warriors
side by side. Left: a British war-leader of about 500 AD as archaeology shows
him — mail shirt if he was rich, round shield, spear, a helmet like the ones
found in graves. Right: the knight of the stories, in the armour of the people
telling them, centuries later. Every age has dressed Arthur in its own
armour; this book dresses him the way the storytellers of about 1200 did.

## V — THE KNIGHT AT THE FOUNTAIN · pp 34–40

**Emotional entry:** pride. **Exit:** a boy walking out of a forest carrying
half a sword.

Kay brings news to Camelot: a champion has set up his tent beside a fountain
in the forest and knocked three of their knights into the water. Arthur wants
to go himself; Kay says a king doesn't. That night Arthur slips out alone,
proud and happy, riding through the moonlit forest. At the fountain is King
Pellinore: older, enormous, battered, no villain, a champion who has never
yielded. *Then you'll fight like a king.* They joust; lances shatter; they
fight on foot; and the sword from the stone breaks in half. Pellinore stands
over Arthur: yield or die. Arthur won't yield. Merlin is suddenly there, and
Pellinore falls into an enchanted sleep. Arthur thinks he's dead and is
furious — and Merlin tells him Pellinore is asleep, and a better man than
Arthur was today. The sword from the stone made you king, says Merlin. It was
never going to make you good. Arthur walks out of the forest holding the
broken half. He nearly died for a song. Merlin tells him there's another
sword, and the one who keeps it won't give it to a fool.

**The turn:** the sword that made him king breaks, because of his own pride.
**Page-turn out:** a lake in the mist.

## VI — THE LADY OF THE LAKE · pp 41–45

**Emotional entry:** awe. **Exit:** Arthur turning down a fight.

A wide lake in mist, and out of the middle an arm in white silk, holding up a
sword. The Lady of the Lake walks on the water to the shore. She will give him
the sword if he promises her a gift one day, whatever she asks. He promises.
He rows out and takes sword and scabbard from the hand, and the hand sinks.
On the shore Merlin asks which he likes better, the sword or the scabbard.
The sword, obviously. *Then you're a fool:* whoever wears the scabbard loses
no blood, however he's wounded; it's worth ten swords; keep it close. Arthur,
slowly, understands the lesson isn't about the scabbard. On the way home they
pass the fountain. Pellinore is awake, and he challenges Arthur again — and
Arthur, with a new sword he could finally beat him with, doesn't fight. He
invites Pellinore to Camelot: *I need men who don't yield.*

**The turn:** Arthur gets the better sword and chooses not to use it.
**Page-turn out:** a king who needs knights who won't fight for the wrong
reasons — and doesn't yet know how to make them.

**Chronicle 5 · p 46 — "Wait — are there two magic swords?"** Yes, and the
storytellers mixed them up for centuries. Arthur's sword is in the oldest
Welsh tales as *Caledfwlch*; Geoffrey of Monmouth, about 1136, made it
*Caliburnus*; the French made it *Excalibur*. The sword in the stone came
later still, and some storytellers say it *was* Excalibur. A family tree of a
sword's names.

## VII — THE ROUND TABLE · pp 47–53

**Emotional entry:** chaos. **Exit:** an empty seat.

Camelot's hall at Pentecost, and the knights are fighting over seats — who
sits nearest the king, who sits at the bottom — and someone draws a knife.
Arthur bangs Excalibur's pommel on the long table and stops it. Then his idea,
argued out with Merlin and the grumbling knights: a table with no head and no
foot. Round. Nobody above anybody, the king included. It is built: huge, oak,
and as each knight takes his seat his name appears on it in gold. Pellinore
rides in and sits, the first champion of the new table. Lot rides in under a
truce; Arthur offers him a seat, equal to everyone's. *Equal? To a farm boy's
squires?* Lot walks out: this isn't over. The knights swear the oath, with
Arthur kneeling first: never to be cruel, to give mercy to anyone who asks,
to protect anyone who can't protect himself, and never to fight for a wrong
cause, for money or for pride. And then Kay asks the question: what about that
seat? One seat has no name. Merlin: it's for someone who hasn't come yet; and
whoever sits there who shouldn't, won't stand up again. And, quietly, to
Arthur alone: *I won't always be beside you.* Last story page: the hall at
night, the table, Excalibur laid on it, and the empty seat.

**The turn:** the king who wanted to be above everyone builds a table where
nobody is.
**Page-turn out:** who is the empty seat for?

**Chronicle 6 · p 54 — "Who told this story first?"** The snowball: Gildas
(about 540, no Arthur) → the *History of the Britons* (about 830, twelve
battles) → Geoffrey of Monmouth (1136, a king, Merlin, the sword
*Caliburnus*) → Wace (1155, the Round Table) → Chrétien de Troyes (1170s–80s,
Camelot, Lancelot, the Grail) → Robert de Boron (about 1200, the sword in the
stone) → Sir Thomas Malory (1470, all of it in one book, written in prison).
The real Round Table hanging in Winchester: made about 1290, painted for King
Henry VIII — seven hundred years after Arthur. And the storytellers weren't
finished: *next came Lancelot.*

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
Ector's confession (p 14) and Arthur's grief (p 15) are separate pages, and
the aftermath (p 16) is a third. The battle's cost (p 30) gets its own page
before the songs. The sword breaking (p 37) and Merlin's rescue (p 38) are
separate pages. Every consequential beat gets its aftermath before the plot
moves — including the protagonist's.

**One dominant turn per page.** Every page in `07-PAGE-CONTRACT.md` names the
one thing that changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence
comes back here, not to the prompt.

**Chronicle placement.** Every Chronicle page follows a page that lands a
movement's turn and precedes a page that opens a new movement. None sits
between a turn and its consequence: there is no Chronicle between V and VI,
because the broken sword's consequence is the lake.
