Light and Darkness, Named

A Wizard of Earthsea — after Ursula K. Le Guin

Ged summoned a shadow out of pride, and spent years running from what he had loosed — north to grim Osskil, south to the dragon of Pendor, out past the last charted island. It cost him four scars, a stolen friend, and nearly his own name. What he learned at the world's dry edge is the heart of Earthsea: you cannot destroy your own darkness. You can only turn, and name it, and be whole. One quiz stands between you and the end — it tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why did Ogion teach Ged so little magic at first?
Right — Ogion wasn't withholding magic, he was trying to teach the one thing that comes before it: silence, patience, and respect for the Balance. Ged, hungry to prove himself, read the quiet as emptiness — and left for Roke still not understanding it.
Not quite. Ogion didn't doubt Ged's gift, save spells for Roke, or lack the time. He taught so little because real power starts with listening and patience and respect for the Balance — a lesson Ged wasn't ready to hear yet.
2. Why did summoning the spirit of the dead Elfarran loose the shadow?
Right — this is the book's central law. Real magic isn't a trick of words; every act shifts the Balance, and calling a spirit back from death is one of the greatest disturbances there is. Ged did it to humiliate Jasper, not because it was needed — and pride tore the opening the shadow came through.
Not quite. It wasn't a trapped book, a mispronounced word, or working alone. Summoning the dead tears at the Balance the world rests on — and Ged did it for pride, not need. That rent is what let the shadow through.
3. Why did Ged refuse the Stone of Terrenon's offer to name the shadow hunting him?
Right — Ged saw the shape of the trap just in time. An Old Power like the Stone doesn't grant favors; it enslaves whoever takes what it offers. The whole court had been herding him there, and the "gift" of a name was the last turn of the snare.
Not quite. Serret never warned him the Stone lied, and Ged did NOT yet know the shadow's name. He refused because Old Powers like the Stone don't serve — they use. Taking its offer would have made him its servant.
4. Why did Ogion's advice — turn around and hunt the hunter — change everything?
Right — the shadow was never stronger than Ged in strength alone; it was stronger in the game of fear. Running, Ged fed it his own terror. Turning to hunt it, he took the one thing it needed from him back — and the balance of the chase flipped.
Not quite. Ogion gave no new spell, no secret name, and didn't organize a group hunt. The advice mattered because fear was what fed the shadow's power. By choosing to hunt instead of flee, Ged took that power back.
5. Why could Ged defeat the shadow only by speaking his own name?
Right — this is the whole point of the book. The shadow was never an outside enemy; it was the part of Ged he'd loosed and then run from. You can't kill your own shadow. You can only stop running, name it as yourself, and become whole.
Not quite. It wasn't a generic rule about true names, or a sailor's charm. The shadow was the dark half of Ged himself — and a person can't destroy their own shadow. Only by claiming it as his own name could Ged become whole.
