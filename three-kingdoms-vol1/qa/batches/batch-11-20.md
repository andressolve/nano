# Sequence gate — canonical pages 11–20

## Verdict

APPROVED

## Uninterrupted visible sequence

The brothers and their growing volunteer force reach the largest rebel camp and prepare to attack at dawn, but a midnight grass fire reveals that someone else has acted first. The fire drives the rebels into imperial cavalry, leaving Zhang Fei furious that their battle has been stolen. At dawn Cao Cao claims the tactic, learns that Liu Bei's men are unpaid villagers, and promises to remember him; Zhang Fei likes Cao Cao immediately, while Guan Yu distrusts his quick smile.

Weeks later the brothers see an imperial general about to be overwhelmed and charge in without asking whose man he is. They save Dong Zhuo, who loses interest as soon as he learns they have no office or rank and dismisses them with a bag of rice. Zhang Fei wants to attack him, but Liu Bei restrains him because killing a general in imperial armor would make them rebels; Zhang Fei sees how deeply Liu Bei shares his anger.

The rebellion ends, decorated generals take the rewards, and the brothers and their farmers return home with nothing. Five years pass. After the old emperor dies and the court turns murderous, Dong Zhuo marches into the capital claiming he will restore order. He rides up the emperor's stairs, deposes the heir, installs the heir's nine-year-old brother, and places himself beside the throne. Yuan Shao openly challenges him and leaves alive with his hand on his sword.

At a banquet that night, another lord repeats the challenge, but Dong Zhuo backs down when he notices the unbeatable Lü Bu standing behind the man. Cao Cao identifies Lü Bu as the lord's adopted son, and Dong Zhuo decides he wants him. Dong Zhuo then brings Lü Bu the peerless Red Hare, gives him the horse, belittles what Lü Bu's adoptive father has provided, and offers gold armor and a new father-son bond. Lü Bu defends the man who raised him, but does not reject the offer.

## Script and sequence judgment

The material story, captions, dialogue, and speaker attribution match the script across the full run. The brothers, Cao Cao, Dong Zhuo, Yuan Shao, and Lü Bu remain legible and distinct when they recur. The brothers' characteristic weapons, Dong Zhuo's purple-and-gold court presence, the imperial and rebel forces, Red Hare, and the progression from battlefield to road, capital, throne hall, banquet, and stable remain consequentially continuous across page turns.

The focal chain is intact: the brothers' stolen victory and unrewarded rescue establish Dong Zhuo's character before the five-year jump; his seizure of the court then leads directly through Yuan Shao's defiance to Lü Bu's introduction and attempted purchase. Cao Cao's return on page 19 also pays off his page-13 introduction without stealing the focal action.

Pacing is controlled. Pages 11 and 16 use illustrated prose to establish scale and compress only intentional elapsed time; pages 12 and 17 provide spectacle pivots; the dramatic pages vary among encounter, rescue, insult, confrontation, political recognition, and seduction. No stretch reads as accidental summary or an undifferentiated wall of dramatic scenes. The consequential beats receive their needed aftermath, while page 20 ends on a deliberate unresolved choice that functions as a page-turn rather than an omission.

No material sequence, transition, continuity, focal, attribution, pacing, or page-turn failure justifies a complete-page redraw.
