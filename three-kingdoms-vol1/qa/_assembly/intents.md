# Page intents

Reader-facing goals, not image-generation specifications. Shown to both
builder and critic. Exact text and staging stay in the script and the prompt.

## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The book opens on the empire coming apart: a walled town burning on a hill under a smoke-brown sky, and down the road toward the reader a river of men in yellow headbands, singing, torches and hoes held high, filling the lower half of the page. The page's one dominant turn is the empire burning with the Yellow Turbans on the road. The title sits over the smoke and one caption tells the reader who these men are and why the land is starving. No one is close enough to have a face; the army is a force, not a set of people. The reader turns because somebody is going to have to stand in front of that road. The overturned farmer's cart at the roadside is a small grace note and must stay small; the march owns the page.

## PAGE 2

Illustrated prose. In a crowded, dusty market, Liu Bei stands behind his straw-sandal stall and does not sell. He is looking at a recruiting notice pasted on the wall beside him, and the prose tells the reader who he is: twenty-eight, quiet, long-armed, long-eared, a prince's descendant three hundred years down who now bows to every customer. The dominant turn is the sandal-seller with a prince's blood reading the notice eleven times. The large image is the man and the notice, the shoppers passing without a glance; the smaller image is his hands gone still on a half-woven sandal. What changes is that we see him wanting something badly and not moving. The reader turns to find out whether he will. The crowd and the stall goods stay subordinate; the notice is the one thing on the wall that matters.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. Liu Bei sighs at the notice and a stranger looming behind him laughs at it: Zhang Fei, a butcher built like a barrel, asks why a grown man sighs at a wall instead of doing something. The dominant turn is that challenge. Liu Bei, embarrassed, admits he wants to fight but has nothing to fight with except his blood and a stall of sandals. Zhang Fei announces himself, his shop, his farm and his money, throws an arm round him and hauls him off to drink, calling him Prince of Sandals; Liu Bei, quietly, gives his real name. The page belongs to Zhang Fei's energy colliding with Liu Bei's reserve. The reader turns because a stranger with money has just grabbed him and they are going to talk about war. The tavern sign and market crowd stay in the background.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. In a low lamplit tavern a giant ducks through the door and bangs the table, calling for wine because he means to enlist before the day ends. The dominant turn is Guan Yu: red-faced, beard to his belt, on the run five years, enlisting. Zhang Fei waves him over; Guan Yu gives his name and his reason: a rich man in his village beat the poor, so he killed him. Liu Bei asks the hard question, whether he will fight for the emperor whose soldiers are hunting him, and Guan Yu answers that he will fight anyone who burns villages like his. Guan Yu owns the page; Liu Bei's question is its edge. The reader turns because three men with nothing in common are at one table and none is leaving. The room and the other drinkers stay subordinate.

## PAGE 5

Dramatic. Night at Zhang Fei's farm, round a courtyard fire, after talking all night. Liu Bei says what he really fears: these two could join any army, so why would anyone follow a man who sells sandals? Guan Yu answers that he came for the man who sighed at the wall, not for the princes, and Zhang Fei crows that he counted the eleven readings. Then Zhang Fei stands and points into the dark at the blossoming trees over his wall: tomorrow, in the peach garden, they swear to be brothers. The dominant turn moves from the doubt to the answer, and lands on Zhang Fei pointing and Liu Bei's face lit and hungry as he accepts. The reader turns because tomorrow three strangers are going to swear. The fire and the wine jug stay subordinate.

## PAGE 6

Spectacle. Morning in the walled peach garden in full pink-and-white bloom, petals falling through slanting light: the only soft colour in the book. Under the largest tree, three cups of wine and smoking incense on a small table; the three men kneel side by side, cups raised, faces solemn, even Zhang Fei's. Liu Bei speaks the oath for all three: to stand together, protect the people, serve the empire, and, not born on the same day, to die on the same day. The dominant turn is the oath itself. The garden and the three kneeling men are one image; nothing else competes. The reader turns because they have just promised to die together and now must live up to it. The table, incense and cups are small and plain.

## PAGE 7

Dramatic. Under the blossom, straight after the oath, the three rise from their knees beside the empty cups, and Zhang Fei counts on his thick fingers who is oldest: Liu Bei is Big Brother, Red Face is Second, and he, the youngest, is Third, which sounds terrible. Then Liu Bei bows to them both, moved and hungry at once, and names what brothers need, weapons, soldiers, money, against his twenty pairs of sandals; Guan Yu, stroking his beard, offers a horse and a cart. The dominant turn is Liu Bei's bow and the plain admission of how little they have. Zhang Fei answers it by throwing his arms wide toward his farmhouse: tomorrow he sells everything but the house and this garden. The reader turns because Zhang Fei has just given away all he owns. The table and cups stay small.

## PAGE 8

Illustrated prose, two places. At a smith's forge in red heat and sparks, the new weapons lie ready: two matching swords in black scabbards, a spear as long as two men with a wavy snake blade, and a great curved blade on a long pole with a dragon at its base, which Guan Yu lifts in one hand while the smith gapes. Below, the peach garden, now in green leaf, is packed with farmers holding hoes and pitchforks, the three brothers before them. The prose tells how the money became a weapon for each brother, names the Green Dragon, and brings five hundred farmers who in a month have spears. The dominant turn is the brothers becoming an armed company: the forge owns the page, the gathering is its consequence. The reader turns because three brothers and five hundred farmers are going to war.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic. On a bare ridge, the three brothers on horseback, five hundred farmers with spears behind them; below, a dust-yellow plain full of yellow headbands to the horizon, and between ridge and plain a narrow valley between two scrubby hills. Zhang Fei jokes that it is only a hundred each; Guan Yu notes the farmers are shaking because they can count too. Then Liu Bei points at the two hills and makes the plan: his brothers hide behind them, he rides out with the farmers as bait. Zhang Fei protests that he is the worst fighter; Liu Bei says that is why they will chase him. The dominant turn is fifty thousand below and the plan making Liu Bei the bait. The reader turns on the plan. The individual farmers and rebels stay small.

## PAGE 10

Spectacle. The trap from the page before springs. Liu Bei, the bait, gallops down the valley toward us with the farmers running behind him and the whole yellow army pouring in after them; then, from the hill on the left, Guan Yu crashes down in green with his great crescent blade, and from the hill on the right Zhang Fei in black with his snake spear, men streaming behind each. The dominant turn: the ambush, two brothers coming down from two hills, and the yellow army breaking. Zhang Fei owns the one shout. A small closing image shows a rebel captain toppling backward out of frame as the spear drives past, his men already turning to run; the fall happens out of frame and nothing is shown of what follows. The reader turns because five hundred farmers just broke fifty thousand, and people will hear about it. The crowd stays a mass; no single rebel face matters.

## PAGE 11

Illustrated prose. Night, and a breath after the battle. The narration carries the brothers' growing name from town to town and brings them south to the biggest rebel army of all, camped in a plain of tall dry grass. The three brothers sit their horses on a low rise with their small army behind them, looking out over a sea of grass and the rebel camp's tents and watchfires. On the far side of that camp a thin line of orange has begun to glow. The dominant turn: the name spreads, the great camp waits in the grass, and the fire out there is someone else's. The brothers are small and still, their backs to us; the land and the glowing line carry the page. The reader turns because someone has set the grass on fire, and it isn't them. The prose sits in the night sky, never over the riders.

## PAGE 12

Spectacle. The whole plain is on fire: flames taller than men, black smoke, tents going up, rebels running out of the blaze toward us, and dark horsemen under black-and-indigo banners sweeping in from both sides to cut them off. In the foreground the three brothers rein in, black silhouettes against the orange, too late to do anything. The dominant turn: the plain burning, and Zhang Fei's outraged cry that somebody stole their battle. Zhang Fei owns the only line, in a small close panel of his orange-lit face, mouth wide; the outrage is funny and real. The reader turns wanting to know who got here first. The riders stay anonymous: no leader is singled out yet, and no one falls in view.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Grey dawn over black stubble. Cao Cao rides up under his black-and-indigo banners: short, quick, amused, the man who lit the fire. He names himself, then asks who pays this ragged army, and Liu Bei, bowing slightly, answers that nobody does; the rebels burned their villages, so they came. Cao Cao's answer is the dominant turn: they are the only men in the war who came because they wanted to, and he will remember Liu Bei. Then he is gone with a smile over his shoulder, and the brothers split on him, Zhang Fei delighted, Guan Yu wary. Cao Cao owns the page; Liu Bei is the man he is measuring. The reader turns because the cleverest man on the battlefield has noticed them, and the brothers cannot agree what that means. The farmers and riders stay background.

## PAGE 14

Dramatic. Weeks later, on the road north, the brothers look down from a rise on a hillside of trampled imperial banners, where a ring of yellow headbands is closing on a knot of horsemen around a huge fat general in gilded armor, helmet crooked, horse turning in panic. Liu Bei sees the emperor's banner; Zhang Fei asks whose general it is; Liu Bei answers that it doesn't matter, and orders the charge. The dominant turn: an imperial general surrounded, and the brothers cut him out. Guan Yu and Zhang Fei smash a road through the ring side by side while Liu Bei, swords still sheathed, waves the farmers on; the ring breaks and the fat general sags, gasping, his armor dented. Liu Bei owns the decision. The reader turns because they have saved someone important, and he is about to find out who saved him. No one falls in view.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. Before his tent, the fat general is helped down and named: Dong Zhuo. Eating a fig, he asks what office and rank his rescuers hold, and Liu Bei, bowing, says none; they are volunteers. The dominant turn is Dong Zhuo's broad gilded back: he walks into his tent eating, throwing over his shoulder an order to give the farmers with sticks a bag of rice. Zhang Fei lunges for the tent with his spear; Liu Bei's hand clamps on his wrist, his own face white and his jaw tight, saying that the man wears the emperor's armor and killing him makes them the rebels. Zhang Fei's last question, why Big Brother's hands are shaking, is the sting. Dong Zhuo owns the insult; Liu Bei owns the cost. The reader turns because Liu Bei swallowed it, and Zhang Fei saw what it cost him. Guards and servant stay background.

## PAGE 16

Illustrated prose, two places, because the page's turn is the passage of time. Above, three riders very small on a long empty road under a huge winter sky, going home with nothing. Below, far away and five years later, a great army marches on a walled city of grey roofs under purple banners, a fat general in gold at its head. The narration crosses between them: the rebellion broken by winter, titles and gold for the great men, nothing for three brothers and five hundred farmers; then the old emperor dead, murder in the palace, and a general from the west marching in. The dominant turn: nothing for heroes, five years, and the fat general marching on the capital. The empty road and the size of the army carry it; no face is close. The reader turns because the man who turned his back on them is marching into the capital.

## PAGE 17

Spectacle. One huge image: the capital seen from above, its avenues full of soldiers under purple banners, and at the centre the palace on its high platform with its long white stair. Up that stair, on horseback, rides Dong Zhuo, the fat general the brothers saved, in gold, a half-eaten drumstick in one fist, while courtiers in silk throw themselves out of his way. The dominant turn: Dong Zhuo rides his horse up the palace stair. The reader turns because a fat man has ridden into the emperor's palace and nobody stopped him. The one caption says it plainly. The city, the soldiers and the courtiers are scale and setting; the eye goes straight to the rider on the stair, and nothing else competes with him.

## PAGE 18

Dramatic. In the vast red-lacquer throne hall Dong Zhuo lifts a terrified nine-year-old boy onto the gold throne and points across the hall at the young emperor being marched out, and tells the court how it will be. Everyone stays on the floor except one man: Yuan Shao, tall and thin in his tall black hat, stands up, names himself and his family, and says Dong Zhuo has no right. Two hands go to two sword hilts, two short questions, and Yuan Shao walks out between the columns with his sword half drawn and nobody stops him. Dong Zhuo owns the opening image; Yuan Shao owns the turn. The dominant turn: the boy on the throne; Yuan Shao defies him and walks out. The reader turns because one lord defied Dong Zhuo and everyone else is still bowing. The kneeling courtiers stay subordinate.

## PAGE 19

Dramatic. At the night banquet an old lord in grey stands and says what Yuan Shao said, louder, and Dong Zhuo reaches for his sword. Then the page's real event: behind the old lord, standing perfectly still, is a giant with two long pheasant plumes and a halberd on his shoulder, Lü Bu, looking straight at Dong Zhuo, and Dong Zhuo's hand stops. Dong Zhuo laughs it off. Later he asks the young officer at his elbow, Cao Cao, who the giant was, and decides he wants him. Lü Bu's silent stare owns the page. The dominant turn: the giant behind the old lord; Cao Cao names him; "Then I want him." The reader turns because Dong Zhuo wants the one man who can stop him, and he always gets what he wants. The feast and the old lord stay subordinate.

## PAGE 20

Dramatic. In a stable at night, by one lantern, Lü Bu is polishing his halberd when Dong Zhuo leads in a huge blood-red horse, Red Hare, and offers it to him. Lü Bu's cold face changes when his hand touches the horse's neck: he wants it, and that wanting is the page's centre. Dong Zhuo, close and smiling, a fat hand on his shoulder, goes after the old man who raised him, and Lü Bu says he has a father; Dong Zhuo answers, "Do you?" and offers gold armor to match the horse. The dominant turn: Red Hare; "I have a father." "Do you?" The reader turns because Lü Bu has been offered a horse for his father and has not said no. The stable and the lantern stay subordinate.

## PAGE 21

Dramatic. Morning in the throne hall. Lü Bu strides up the black floor, kneels before Dong Zhuo, and lays down a small bronze tiger seal, the old man's seal, while Red Hare waits through the great doors behind him. He says the old man will never say no to anyone again, and he calls Dong Zhuo Father. Dong Zhuo, delighted, heaves himself up to raise him and calls for gold armor. Among the courtiers, Cao Cao, very still, says to nobody what has happened. Lü Bu kneeling over the seal owns the page. The dominant turn: the seal on the floor; "Father"; Cao Cao, "For a horse." The reader turns because the tyrant now owns the warrior nobody can beat, and Cao Cao is watching. The death is never shown; the seal says it. The courtiers stay subordinate.

## PAGE 22

Illustrated prose. A quiet page of aftermath across two places. Above, the emperor's bedchamber: Dong Zhuo asleep across the great imperial bed among silk curtains, a plate of half-eaten food on his belly, and Lü Bu standing guard in the doorway. Below, the throne hall at morning audience: rows of courtiers bowing, and in the front row, bowing lowest of all, Cao Cao, with the jeweled hilt of a dagger just showing at his sleeve. The prose tells what Dong Zhuo now has, how the city suffers, and that Cao Cao smiles at everything and carries a seven-jewelled dagger. The dominant turn: the tyrant in the emperor's bed; Cao Cao bows lowest, a dagger in his sleeve. The reader turns because Cao Cao has a dagger and is close enough to use it. The rows of courtiers stay subordinate; the dagger hilt is small but must be found.

## PAGE 23

Dramatic. Cao Cao comes late to Dong Zhuo's bedchamber, the imperial bedchamber the tyrant has taken, with an excuse about his slow old horse. Dong Zhuo, too fat to sit up for long, sends Lü Bu down to the stable to choose him a fast one, and Lü Bu goes. Then the tyrant rolls his great body over to face the wall and tells Cao Cao to wake him when the horse comes. The page's one dominant turn: Lü Bu sent away, the tyrant faces the wall, and Cao Cao's hand is on the jeweled hilt in his sleeve. The final image owns the page: the turned back, the man standing behind it, alone, and in the corner a round bronze mirror facing the couch that nobody is looking at yet. The excuse and the errand stay brisk and subordinate. The reader turns because the blade is one movement away.

## PAGE 24

Dramatic. The dagger comes out and rises over Dong Zhuo's back, and in the bronze mirror the reader sees what Cao Cao does not: the tyrant's one open eye, and the blade. Dong Zhuo rolls over roaring, Lü Bu's shadow already in the doorway. The page's one dominant turn is the bluff: Cao Cao is on both knees before anyone can move, the dagger lying flat across his two open palms like a present, smiling, offering it as a gift. Dong Zhuo, greedy, turns it in his fat fingers and admires the jewels, calling Lü Bu "my son," and Cao Cao, rising and bowing, asks to try the new horse once around the courtyard. The kneeling offer owns the page; the mirror and the roar are its fuse. The reader turns because Cao Cao has lied his way out and is about to be on a fast horse.

## PAGE 25

Illustrated prose. Cao Cao gallops out through the great city gate in a cloud of dust, crouched low over a fast horse, the guards turning too late; that escape is the dominant image and owns the page. Below it, smaller, Dong Zhuo sits up on his couch frowning at the jeweled dagger, Lü Bu beside him. The prose carries the turn: the ride once around the courtyard that never stopped, the slow question of why a man would kneel with the blade already out of its sheath, Lü Bu's flat answer, "he was going to kill you, Father," and by nightfall Cao Cao's face painted on every gate with a price on his head. The prose sits over sky and dust, never over the rider. The reader turns because the whole empire is now hunting Cao Cao.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. At dusk a dusty, hollow-eyed Cao Cao reaches a modest farm gate, and a thin little old man throws it open with his arms flung wide: his father's sworn brother from their young days, delighted to see "little Cao Cao." Cao Cao warns him there is a price on his head; the old man will not hear of danger in his house. The page's one dominant turn: the old man takes him in and rides off on a grey donkey with an empty wine jar to fetch the good wine from town, promising to be back by morning, telling him to rest while the family looks after him. The final image owns the page: the old man beaming as he goes, and Cao Cao alone in the courtyard watching him, one hand on his sword. The welcome is warm and talkative; the hand on the sword is the quiet weight. The reader turns because a kind man has left a hunted man alone in his house.

## PAGE 27

Dramatic. Night. Cao Cao lies awake on a mat; through the thin wall comes lamplight and the sound of a knife on a whetstone, and a whisper about tying the legs and one quick cut. He hears "tie," "cut," and "he," and does not wait. His drawn sword comes through a lamplit doorway toward an old woman and a young man still holding the knife he was sharpening, startled and alive, and the panel ends there; nothing of the killing is shown. The page's one dominant turn is the dawn: a small empty kitchen, a live pig tied by its legs on the table, a cleaver and the whetstone beside it, and Cao Cao in the doorway with his sword lowered, looking at it. That still image owns the page; the night is its approach. The reader turns because Cao Cao has killed a whole family for nothing, and the old man is still out buying wine.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. Morning on the road between willows. Cao Cao rides out fast and meets the old man coming home on his donkey with a full wine jar and a basket of fruit, waving, calling that he told them to kill a pig. Cao Cao reins in and says he is sorry; the old man asks, puzzled, sorry for what. The page's one dominant turn: Cao Cao above him, sword drawn, face calm, explaining that the old man would go home, see, and tell the world, and saying the line that defines him: he would rather betray the whole world than let the world betray him. The old man's open, puzzled face is under it. The blow is never shown; the last image is an empty road, the donkey wandering off, the wine jar rolling in the dust, and the old man never came home. The confrontation owns the page. The reader turns because only they saw it.

## PAGE 29

Illustrated prose. Cao Cao reaches home, raises an army, and writes to every lord in the empire calling on the loyal to march against Dong Zhuo. The dominant image owns the page: a camp under black-and-indigo banners, riders galloping out of it in every direction with letters in their coats, and Cao Cao on a rise watching them go. Below, smaller, the dusty market of Zhuo County, years later and unchanged, where Liu Bei still sits at his sandal stall under the same wall and takes a sealed letter, one of a bundle, from a dust-covered rider. The prose carries the turn and its irony: nobody there knows what Cao Cao did on the road. The prose sits over the sky above the camp. The reader turns because the letter has reached Liu Bei, and he has waited years for it.

## PAGE 30

Dramatic. In the peach garden, now in full green leaf, Liu Bei reads the letter aloud with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei leaning in on either side. Zhang Fei is outraged that Dong Zhuo is the fat man they saved; Guan Yu notes the letter is from the man who smiles too quickly. The page's one dominant turn belongs to Liu Bei: on his feet, the letter crushed in his hand, his face fierce and hungry, saying that nobody ever sent for them, Cao Cao sent for everybody, and he does not care who sent it: they go. That close, hungry face owns the page; it is the first time his want shows. Then Guan Yu rises and takes his Green Dragon blade from a peach tree, and Zhang Fei whoops and runs for his spear. The reader turns because the brothers are marching against the man they saved, at the call of a man they don't know.

## PAGE 31

Illustrated prose. The page's one turn: eighteen lords before Hulao Gate, and the brothers at the very edge of their camp. One great image owns the page: a mountain valley filled to its far end with tents, smoke and banners of many colours, a great gold banner over the largest tent, and at the head of the valley a grey fortress gate filling the pass under purple banners. The prose sits over the sky and tells the reader who came, where the gate stands, and who waits behind it. Small in the near corner, the three brothers ride in with their farmers and pitch one small tent. The scale is the story: the lords are a city, the brothers a speck. The reader turns because eighteen great lords are about to meet three nobodies.

## PAGE 32

Dramatic. The page's one turn: "I was told he sells sandals," and Liu Bei takes the very end of the bench. The opening image owns the page: the long table of the lords in the great tent, Yuan Shao in ivory under the gold banner at its head, Cao Cao halfway down, the three brothers standing by the door. Yuan Shao asks who they are; Cao Cao vouches for Liu Bei; Yuan Shao sneers at the sandals while the lords laugh behind their sleeves; Cao Cao asks for a seat for him. Liu Bei bows and sits on the last bench, Guan Yu like stone behind him, Zhang Fei bent to his ear offering to put the leader's hat in the soup, and Liu Bei refusing. The reader turns because Zhang Fei is about to burst.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic. The page's one turn: Dong Zhuo's champion kills the lords' best, and Guan Yu volunteers to bring back his helmet. A messenger throws himself down at the head of the table and Yuan Shao orders out their best; the messenger returns, kneeling, to a silent tent; Yuan Shao, pale, strikes the table and wishes for his own great generals. Those three beats are the build. The last image owns the page: from the back of the tent, Guan Yu steps forward, huge, stroking his beard, and every head at the table turns to him as he says he will bring the champion's helmet back to this table. The reader turns because a man with no rank has just volunteered in front of eighteen lords.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. The page's one turn: Guan Yu bets his life against a cup of wine. Yuan Shao demands the volunteer's rank and Liu Bei answers plainly that his brother has none; Yuan Shao, outraged, orders the common soldier thrown out; Cao Cao stands and talks the tent round. The last image owns the page: Cao Cao pours a steaming cup of wine and holds it out, and Guan Yu, already turning for the door with his great crescent blade, tells him to leave it on the table, because he will be back before it is cold. The reader turns because the wine is poured, Guan Yu is going, and there is no rank to save him if he fails.

## PAGE 35

Spectacle. The page's one turn: the war drums, the steaming cup, and the duel swallowed in dust. One image is the whole page, seen from the head of the table looking down the great tent: in the near foreground the cup of wine still steaming; behind it the lords frozen in their seats, listening; Liu Bei and Zhang Fei on their feet at the far end. Through the open flap, far away on the dusty plain under the grey wall of the gate, two tiny riders, one in green and one dark, meet at full gallop, and a wall of dust swallows them both. No result can be seen. The drums carry the sound across the top. The reader turns because somebody is going to come back through that flap, and the wine is still steaming.

## PAGE 36

Dramatic. The page's one turn: the helmet, the wine still warm, and the three nobodies thrown out anyway. The drums stop; Guan Yu ducks in through the flap and sets a dented plumed helmet on the table before Yuan Shao. The central image owns the page, close and quiet: Guan Yu lifting the cup, steam still rising, and Cao Cao beside him, delighted that it is still warm. Then the noise: Zhang Fei roars along the table that his brother killed the champion and they should take the whole gate; Yuan Shao, white with anger, orders the three nobodies thrown out; Cao Cao shrugs that a victory is a victory. The reader turns because Guan Yu won and they are being thrown out regardless.

## PAGE 37

Dramatic. The page's one turn: Cao Cao warns that Lü Bu comes tomorrow and asks for the brothers, and Liu Bei will not say yes. The opening image owns the page: the brothers' small tent at night, one lamp, Cao Cao crouched alone with the three of them over a wine jar and a basket of roast meat, his face serious for once, telling them who Lü Bu is and what he did for a red horse. He leans closer and asks Liu Bei to stand with him, not with Yuan Shao; Liu Bei answers that they will fight beside anyone who fights Dong Zhuo. Cao Cao gone, the flap still swinging, Zhang Fei punches his palm and Guan Yu judges the man who went on bowing. The reader turns because Lü Bu comes tomorrow and Liu Bei gave Cao Cao no answer.

## PAGE 38

Dramatic. The page's one turn: on the wall, Lü Bu asks Dong Zhuo for the gate. The opening image owns the page: the battlements of Hulao Gate at night under purple banners, Dong Zhuo eating on a heap of cushions, gleeful that a red-faced common soldier killed his champion and was then thrown out by the lords; Lü Bu beside him, halberd on his shoulder, looking down at thousands of campfires in the valley. Dong Zhuo stops laughing as he counts the armies against his one gate. Lü Bu turns from the parapet, plumes black against the moon, calls him Father, and promises all eighteen. The reader turns because tomorrow the gate opens, and Lü Bu comes out.

## PAGE 39

Spectacle. The great doors of Hulao Gate stand open and Lü Bu comes out of them at full gallop straight at the reader on Red Hare: the blood-red horse at full stretch, the gold armor and vermilion cloak, the two pheasant plumes streaming back, the halberd levelled. His army pours out behind him under purple banners, and the gate tower stands over everything. The one caption names him the best of men and his horse the best of horses. The dominant turn is the gate opening on Lü Bu, and the whole page belongs to him and the horse; the army and the fortress are only the size of the threat behind him. The reader turns because the man nobody can beat is riding out, and eighteen lords now have to face him.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic. The lords send their champions out one at a time, and none lasts: a spear snaps in two in one small panel, a rider is knocked out of his saddle and out of frame in the next, his horse running on alone. Then the large panel: the coalition's front line breaking, banners wavering, men turning to run, and Yuan Shao on his rise under the great gold banner asking whether eighteen armies have no one who can stop one man. Far off, Lü Bu turns Red Hare toward that banner. The three brothers sit their horses on the same rise near it. On a separate rise, plainly apart, Cao Cao holds his riders still under black-and-indigo banners and watches. The dominant turn is the line breaking while Cao Cao holds still on his own hill. The reader turns because Lü Bu has seen the leader's banner and is coming for it.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Lü Bu cuts through Yuan Shao's guards; Yuan Shao's horse stumbles and his tall black hat tumbles off. In the large panel Lü Bu stands over the fallen leader with the halberd raised high, and from the right, on a black horse, spear levelled and mouth wide open, Zhang Fei charges in roaring that he is the butcher come to carve the slave of three fathers. The dominant turn is that shout. Then the two face each other: Lü Bu cold, asking what he was called; Zhang Fei grinning, counting the three fathers out loud, the one who made him, the old man he killed, the fat pig who bought him with a horse, and daring him to fight. Yuan Shao scrambles away, subordinate. The reader turns because Zhang Fei has just insulted the greatest warrior alive to his face.

## PAGE 42

Spectacle. Zhang Fei and Lü Bu at the centre of the plain, the snake spear and the halberd locked, the black horse and the red horse rearing chest to chest, dust boiling up around them. Far off on both sides two whole armies watch without moving. One caption says they came together fifty times and neither gave a single step. The dominant turn is the deadlock itself: two warriors matched, and the whole page holds still around them. Nobody speaks. The armies are small and the dust is wide; the two riders own the page. The reader turns because fifty times is a long fight, and Zhang Fei is tiring.

## PAGE 43

Dramatic. Zhang Fei is spent, soaked in sweat, his horse staggering, and Lü Bu laughs and asks if that is all a butcher has. Guan Yu gallops in from the left with the Green Dragon blade swinging up and his beard flying, shouting to his Third Brother that he is here. The dominant turn is the large panel: two against one, blade and spear coming in together from either side, and Lü Bu catching both on the shaft of his halberd, still laughing, pleased that it is almost a fight now. A caption says thirty more times and still he did not give a step. Last, small and far off, Liu Bei sits his horse under the gold banner, gripping the reins, not moving, his swords still on his back. The reader turns because even two brothers cannot beat him, and Liu Bei is still on the hill.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. On the hill under the gold banner, Yuan Shao, hatless and muddy, blames the butcher and orders the sandal-seller to stay back under his banner. Liu Bei says nothing to him. The dominant turn is the large panel: Liu Bei drawing his two swords up out of the crossed scabbards on his back, one in each hand, his face fiercer than the reader has ever seen it, saying his brothers have fought eighty times while he sat here, that in a peach garden they swore to die on the same day, and that if today is that day he will not spend it standing on a hill. Then he is galloping down the slope, both swords out, white cloak flying, and Yuan Shao stares after him in silence. The reader turns because the worst fighter of the three is riding at Lü Bu.

## PAGE 45

Spectacle. Seen from above: the three brothers circle Lü Bu like the spokes of a turning lantern, the green blade, the snake spear, and the two swords coming at him from three sides at once. Red Hare rears at the centre and Lü Bu twists in the saddle, trying to face all three. A ring of dust surrounds them, and both armies watch from the edges of the page. One caption says that for the first time in his life Lü Bu could not see all of his enemies at once. The dominant turn is the ring closing: the oath fighting as one thing. No one speaks. The reader turns because Lü Bu is surrounded and something has to break.

## PAGE 46

Dramatic, two places: the plain, then the doors of Hulao Gate. Lü Bu feints a thrust straight at Liu Bei's face; Liu Bei jerks back, and a gap opens in the ring. Lü Bu wheels Red Hare out through it and gallops for the gate while Zhang Fei spurs after him, shouting that Lü Bu is running away from them. The dominant turn is the large final panel: the great doors slamming shut behind Red Hare's tail just as the brothers reach them, arrows coming down from the wall, Zhang Fei pounding the doors with the butt of his spear, Guan Yu pulling at his arm and telling him to get back, Liu Bei reined in, staring up at the wall. The feint, the flinch, and the slam are the turn. The reader turns because Lü Bu ran, and now he is behind the gate.

## PAGE 47

A dramatic page at the foot of Hulao Gate, late in the day. The gate has slammed and Lü Bu is safe behind it, but the page belongs to him looking down from the battlements, out of breath for the first time in the book, asking the three men below who they are. The dominant turn is the exchange itself: "Who are you?", Liu Bei answering with three names and the word "brothers," and Guan Yu's one-word refusal. Zhang Fei's shouted insult opens the page and stays a small beat; the brothers below are small against the grey wall. What changes is that the best warrior in the empire has had to learn their names. Guan Yu's "No." closes the page with the oath answering Lü Bu's bargain. The reader turns because Lü Bu knows who they are now and the light is going.

## PAGE 48

A dramatic page at the brothers' fire at the edge of the camp, at dusk. Cao Cao sits down beside Liu Bei with two cups of wine; Liu Bei will not take his and asks where Cao Cao was when Lü Bu came. Cao Cao answers without shame: on his hill, watching, testing them. He mocks Yuan Shao's claim to the victory, then leans close in the firelight and says the thing the page turns on: there are two heroes in this camp, Liu Bei and himself. That close, glittering face owns the page. Liu Bei, who refused the cup, ends the page holding it and looking into it, answering that he is only a man who sells sandals, while Cao Cao laughs and goes. Guan Yu and Zhang Fei stay subordinate, apart by the fire. The reader turns because Liu Bei has just been told what he most wants to hear by the man he trusts least.

## PAGE 49

An illustrated-prose page at the edge of the camp, at night. Liu Bei stands alone in moonlight with his back to us, looking at the dark mass of Hulao Gate filling the pass, while behind him his brothers sleep by the embers. The prose carries his sleepless thinking: who is behind the gate, who is in front of it, the moment he flinched and let Lü Bu through, the peach garden, and what Cao Cao said. The dominant turn is the last sentence: he is ashamed of how much he wants it to be true. The single image owns the page; the sleeping brothers stay small and quiet, a reminder of the oath behind him. The reader turns because Liu Bei wants the empire, and the gate is still shut.

## PAGE 50

A spectacle page before Hulao Gate at cold blue dawn, the last page of the volume. The three brothers sit their horses side by side before the shut gate, Liu Bei in the middle facing it, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei on either side. High on the battlements, small against the sky, Lü Bu's two pheasant plumes. Behind them, large and clear on a ridge in the near corner, Cao Cao on his horse under his black-and-indigo banners, watching. The dominant turn is that Liu Bei does not turn round: he has chosen his brothers over Cao Cao's praise, for today. One caption tells it, and a small title card ends the volume. Nothing else is said. The book ends with the gate still shut and the man on the ridge still watching.
