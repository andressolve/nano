## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The book opens on the empire coming apart: a walled town burning on a hill under a smoke-brown sky, and down the road toward the reader a river of men in yellow headbands, singing, torches and hoes held high, filling the lower half of the page. The page's one dominant turn is the empire burning with the Yellow Turbans on the road. The title sits over the smoke and one caption tells the reader who these men are and why the land is starving. No one is close enough to have a face; the army is a force, not a set of people. The reader turns because somebody is going to have to stand in front of that road. The overturned farmer's cart at the roadside is a small grace note and must stay small; the march owns the page.

## PAGE 2

Illustrated prose. In a crowded, dusty market, Liu Bei stands behind his straw-sandal stall and does not sell. He is looking at a recruiting notice pasted on the wall beside him, and the prose tells the reader who he is: twenty-eight, quiet, long-armed, long-eared, a prince's descendant three hundred years down who now bows to every customer. The dominant turn is the sandal-seller with a prince's blood reading the notice eleven times. The large image is the man and the notice, the shoppers passing without a glance; the smaller image is his hands gone still on a half-woven sandal. What changes is that we see him wanting something badly and not moving. The reader turns to find out whether he will. The crowd and the stall goods stay subordinate; the notice is the one thing on the wall that matters.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. Liu Bei sighs at the notice and a stranger looming behind him laughs at it: Zhang Fei, a butcher built like a barrel, asks why a grown man sighs at a wall instead of doing something. The dominant turn is that challenge. Liu Bei, embarrassed, admits he wants to fight but has nothing to fight with except his blood and a stall of sandals. Zhang Fei announces himself, his shop, his farm and his money, throws an arm round him and hauls him off to drink, calling him Prince of Sandals; Liu Bei, quietly, gives his real name. The page belongs to Zhang Fei's energy colliding with Liu Bei's reserve. The reader turns because a stranger with money has just grabbed him and they are going to talk about war. The tavern sign and market crowd stay in the background.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. In a low lamplit tavern a giant ducks through the door and bangs the table, calling for wine because he means to enlist before the day ends. The dominant turn is Guan Yu: red-faced, beard to his belt, on the run five years, enlisting. Zhang Fei waves him over; Guan Yu gives his name and his reason: a rich man in his village beat the poor, so he killed him. Liu Bei asks the hard question, whether he will fight for the emperor whose soldiers are hunting him, and Guan Yu answers that he will fight anyone who burns villages like his. Guan Yu owns the page; Liu Bei's question is its edge. The reader turns because three men with nothing in common are at one table and none is leaving. The room and the other drinkers stay subordinate.

## PAGE 5

Dramatic. Night at Zhang Fei's farm, round a courtyard fire, after talking all night. Liu Bei says what he really fears: these two could join any army, so why would anyone follow a man who sells sandals? Guan Yu answers that he came for the man who sighed at the wall, not for the princes, and Zhang Fei crows that he counted the eleven readings. Then Zhang Fei stands and points into the dark at the blossoming trees over his wall: tomorrow, in the peach garden, they swear to be brothers. The dominant turn moves from the doubt to the answer, and lands on Zhang Fei pointing and Liu Bei's face lit and hungry as he accepts. The reader turns because tomorrow three strangers are going to swear. The fire and the wine jug stay subordinate.

## PAGE 6

Spectacle. Morning in the walled peach garden in full pink-and-white bloom, petals falling through slanting light: the only soft colour in the book. Under the largest tree, three cups of wine and smoking incense on a small table; the three men kneel side by side, cups raised, faces solemn, even Zhang Fei's. Liu Bei speaks the oath for all three: to stand together, protect the people, serve the empire, and, not born on the same day, to die on the same day. The dominant turn is the oath itself. The garden and the three kneeling men are one image; nothing else competes. The reader turns because they have just promised to die together and now must live up to it. The table, incense and cups are small and plain.

## PAGE 7

Dramatic. Under the blossom, straight after the oath, the three rise from their knees beside the empty cups, and Zhang Fei counts on his thick fingers who is oldest: Liu Bei is Big Brother, Red Face is Second, and he, the youngest, is Third, which sounds terrible. Then Liu Bei bows to them both, moved and hungry at once, and names what brothers need, weapons, soldiers, money, against his twenty pairs of sandals; Guan Yu, stroking his beard, offers a horse and a cart. The dominant turn is Liu Bei's bow and the plain admission of how little they have. Zhang Fei answers it by throwing his arms wide toward his farmhouse: tomorrow he sells everything but the house and this garden. The reader turns because Zhang Fei has just given away all he owns. The table and cups stay small.

## PAGE 8

Illustrated prose, two places. At a smith's forge in red heat and sparks, the new weapons lie ready: two matching swords in black scabbards, a spear as long as two men with a wavy snake blade, and a great curved blade on a long pole with a dragon at its base, which Guan Yu lifts in one hand while the smith gapes. Below, the peach garden, now in green leaf, is packed with farmers holding hoes and pitchforks, the three brothers before them. The prose tells how the money became a weapon for each brother, names the Green Dragon, and brings five hundred farmers who in a month have spears. The dominant turn is the brothers becoming an armed company: the forge owns the page, the gathering is its consequence. The reader turns because three brothers and five hundred farmers are going to war.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic. On a bare ridge, the three brothers on horseback, five hundred farmers with spears behind them; below, a dust-yellow plain full of yellow headbands to the horizon, and between ridge and plain a narrow valley between two scrubby hills. Zhang Fei jokes that it is only a hundred each; Guan Yu notes the farmers are shaking because they can count too. Then Liu Bei points at the two hills and makes the plan: his brothers hide behind them, he rides out with the farmers as bait. Zhang Fei protests that he is the worst fighter; Liu Bei says that is why they will chase him. The dominant turn is fifty thousand below and the plan making Liu Bei the bait. The reader turns on the plan. The individual farmers and rebels stay small.
