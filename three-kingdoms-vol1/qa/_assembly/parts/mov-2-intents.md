## PAGE 10

Spectacle. The trap from the page before springs. Liu Bei, the bait, gallops down the valley toward us with the farmers running behind him and the whole yellow army pouring in after them; then, from the hill on the left, Guan Yu crashes down in green with his great crescent blade, and from the hill on the right Zhang Fei in black with his snake spear, men streaming behind each. The dominant turn: the ambush, two brothers coming down from two hills, and the yellow army breaking. Zhang Fei owns the one shout. A small closing image shows a rebel captain toppling backward out of frame as the spear drives past, his men already turning to run; the fall happens out of frame and nothing is shown of what follows. The reader turns because five hundred farmers just broke fifty thousand, and people will hear about it. The crowd stays a mass; no single rebel face matters.

## PAGE 11

Illustrated prose. Night, and a breath after the battle. The narration carries the brothers' growing name from town to town and brings them south to the biggest rebel army of all, camped in a plain of tall dry grass. The three brothers sit their horses on a low rise with their small army behind them, looking out over a sea of grass and the rebel camp's tents and watchfires. On the far side of that camp a thin line of orange has begun to glow. The dominant turn: the name spreads, the great camp waits in the grass, and the fire out there is someone else's. The brothers are small and still, their backs to us; the land and the glowing line carry the page. The reader turns because someone has set the grass on fire, and it isn't them. The prose sits in the night sky, never over the riders.

## PAGE 12

Spectacle. The whole plain is on fire: flames taller than men, black smoke, tents going up, rebels running out of the blaze toward us, and dark horsemen under black-and-indigo banners sweeping in from both sides to cut them off. In the foreground the three brothers rein in, black silhouettes against the orange, too late to do anything. The dominant turn: the plain burning, and Zhang Fei's outraged cry that somebody stole their battle. Zhang Fei owns the only line, in a small close panel of his orange-lit face, mouth wide; the outrage is funny and real. The reader turns wanting to know who got here first. The riders stay anonymous: no leader is singled out yet, and no one falls in view.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Grey dawn over black stubble. Cao Cao rides up under his black-and-indigo banners: short, quick, amused, the man who lit the fire. He names himself, then asks who pays this ragged army, and Liu Bei, bowing slightly, answers that nobody does; the rebels burned their villages, so they came. Cao Cao's answer is the dominant turn: they are the only men in the war who came because they wanted to, and he will remember Liu Bei. Then he is gone with a smile over his shoulder, and the brothers split on him, Zhang Fei delighted, Guan Yu wary. Cao Cao owns the page; Liu Bei is the man he is measuring. The reader turns because the cleverest man on the battlefield has noticed them, and the brothers cannot agree what that means. The farmers and riders stay background.

## PAGE 14

Dramatic. Weeks later, on the road north, the brothers look down from a rise on a hillside of trampled imperial banners, where a ring of yellow headbands is closing on a knot of horsemen around a huge fat general in gilded armor, helmet crooked, horse turning in panic. Liu Bei sees the emperor's banner; Zhang Fei asks whose general it is; Liu Bei answers that it doesn't matter, and orders the charge. The dominant turn: an imperial general surrounded, and the brothers cut him out. Guan Yu and Zhang Fei smash a road through the ring side by side while Liu Bei, swords still sheathed, waves the farmers on; the ring breaks and the fat general sags, gasping, his armor dented. Liu Bei owns the decision. The reader turns because they have saved someone important, and he is about to find out who saved him. No one falls in view.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. Before his tent, the fat general is helped down and named: Dong Zhuo. Eating a fig, he asks what office and rank his rescuers hold, and Liu Bei, bowing, says none; they are volunteers. The dominant turn is Dong Zhuo's broad gilded back: he walks into his tent eating, throwing over his shoulder an order to give the farmers with sticks a bag of rice. Zhang Fei lunges for the tent with his spear; Liu Bei's hand clamps on his wrist, his own face white and his jaw tight, saying that the man wears the emperor's armor and killing him makes them the rebels. Zhang Fei's last question, why Big Brother's hands are shaking, is the sting. Dong Zhuo owns the insult; Liu Bei owns the cost. The reader turns because Liu Bei swallowed it, and Zhang Fei saw what it cost him. Guards and servant stay background.

## PAGE 16

Illustrated prose, two places, because the page's turn is the passage of time. Above, three riders very small on a long empty road under a huge winter sky, going home with nothing. Below, far away and five years later, a great army marches on a walled city of grey roofs under purple banners, a fat general in gold at its head. The narration crosses between them: the rebellion broken by winter, titles and gold for the great men, nothing for three brothers and five hundred farmers; then the old emperor dead, murder in the palace, and a general from the west marching in. The dominant turn: nothing for heroes, five years, and the fat general marching on the capital. The empty road and the size of the army carry it; no face is close. The reader turns because the man who turned his back on them is marching into the capital.
