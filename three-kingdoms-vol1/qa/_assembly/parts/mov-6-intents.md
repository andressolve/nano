## PAGE 39

Spectacle. The great doors of Hulao Gate stand open and Lü Bu comes out of them at full gallop straight at the reader on Red Hare: the blood-red horse at full stretch, the gold armor and vermilion cloak, the two pheasant plumes streaming back, the halberd levelled. His army pours out behind him under purple banners, and the gate tower stands over everything. The one caption names him the best of men and his horse the best of horses. The dominant turn is the gate opening on Lü Bu, and the whole page belongs to him and the horse; the army and the fortress are only the size of the threat behind him. The reader turns because the man nobody can beat is riding out, and eighteen lords now have to face him.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic. The lords send their champions out one at a time, and none lasts: a spear snaps in two in one small panel, a rider is knocked out of his saddle and out of frame in the next, his horse running on alone. Then the large panel: the coalition's front line breaking, banners wavering, men turning to run, and Yuan Shao on his rise under the great gold banner asking whether eighteen armies have no one who can stop one man. Far off, Lü Bu turns Red Hare toward that banner. The three brothers sit their horses on the same rise near it. On a separate rise, plainly apart, Cao Cao holds his riders still under black-and-indigo banners and watches. The dominant turn is the line breaking while Cao Cao holds still on his own hill. The reader turns because Lü Bu has seen the leader's banner and is coming for it.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Lü Bu cuts through Yuan Shao's guards; Yuan Shao's horse stumbles and his tall black hat tumbles off. In the large panel Lü Bu stands over the fallen leader with the halberd raised high, and from the right, on a black horse, spear levelled and mouth wide open, Zhang Fei charges in roaring that he is the butcher come to carve the slave of three fathers. The dominant turn is that shout. Then the two face each other: Lü Bu cold, asking what he was called; Zhang Fei grinning, counting the three fathers out loud, the one who made him, the old man he killed, the fat pig who bought him with a horse, and daring him to fight. Yuan Shao scrambles away, subordinate. The reader turns because Zhang Fei has just insulted the greatest warrior alive to his face.

## PAGE 42

Spectacle. Zhang Fei and Lü Bu at the centre of the plain, the snake spear and the halberd locked, the black horse and the red horse rearing chest to chest, dust boiling up around them. Far off on both sides two whole armies watch without moving. One caption says they came together fifty times and neither gave a single step. The dominant turn is the deadlock itself: two warriors matched, and the whole page holds still around them. Nobody speaks. The armies are small and the dust is wide; the two riders own the page. The reader turns because fifty times is a long fight, and Zhang Fei is tiring.

## PAGE 43

Dramatic. Zhang Fei is spent, soaked in sweat, his horse staggering, and Lü Bu laughs and asks if that is all a butcher has. Guan Yu gallops in from the left with the Green Dragon blade swinging up and his beard flying, shouting to his Third Brother that he is here. The dominant turn is the large panel: two against one, blade and spear coming in together from either side, and Lü Bu catching both on the shaft of his halberd, still laughing, pleased that it is almost a fight now. A caption says thirty more times and still he did not give a step. Last, small and far off, Liu Bei sits his horse under the gold banner, gripping the reins, not moving, his swords still on his back. The reader turns because even two brothers cannot beat him, and Liu Bei is still on the hill.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. On the hill under the gold banner, Yuan Shao, hatless and muddy, blames the butcher and orders the sandal-seller to stay back under his banner. Liu Bei says nothing to him. The dominant turn is the large panel: Liu Bei drawing his two swords up out of the crossed scabbards on his back, one in each hand, his face fiercer than the reader has ever seen it, saying his brothers have fought eighty times while he sat here, that in a peach garden they swore to die on the same day, and that if today is that day he will not spend it standing on a hill. Then he is galloping down the slope, both swords out, white cloak flying, and Yuan Shao stares after him in silence. The reader turns because the worst fighter of the three is riding at Lü Bu.

## PAGE 45

Spectacle. Seen from above: the three brothers circle Lü Bu like the spokes of a turning lantern, the green blade, the snake spear, and the two swords coming at him from three sides at once. Red Hare rears at the centre and Lü Bu twists in the saddle, trying to face all three. A ring of dust surrounds them, and both armies watch from the edges of the page. One caption says that for the first time in his life Lü Bu could not see all of his enemies at once. The dominant turn is the ring closing: the oath fighting as one thing. No one speaks. The reader turns because Lü Bu is surrounded and something has to break.

## PAGE 46

Dramatic, two places: the plain, then the doors of Hulao Gate. Lü Bu feints a thrust straight at Liu Bei's face; Liu Bei jerks back, and a gap opens in the ring. Lü Bu wheels Red Hare out through it and gallops for the gate while Zhang Fei spurs after him, shouting that Lü Bu is running away from them. The dominant turn is the large final panel: the great doors slamming shut behind Red Hare's tail just as the brothers reach them, arrows coming down from the wall, Zhang Fei pounding the doors with the butt of his spear, Guan Yu pulling at his arm and telling him to get back, Liu Bei reined in, staring up at the wall. The feint, the flinch, and the slam are the turn. The reader turns because Lü Bu ran, and now he is behind the gate.
