## PAGE 23

Dramatic. Cao Cao comes late to Dong Zhuo's bedchamber, the imperial bedchamber the tyrant has taken, with an excuse about his slow old horse. Dong Zhuo, too fat to sit up for long, sends Lü Bu down to the stable to choose him a fast one, and Lü Bu goes. Then the tyrant rolls his great body over to face the wall and tells Cao Cao to wake him when the horse comes. The page's one dominant turn: Lü Bu sent away, the tyrant faces the wall, and Cao Cao's hand is on the jeweled hilt in his sleeve. The final image owns the page: the turned back, the man standing behind it, alone, and in the corner a round bronze mirror facing the couch that nobody is looking at yet. The excuse and the errand stay brisk and subordinate. The reader turns because the blade is one movement away.

## PAGE 24

Dramatic. The dagger comes out and rises over Dong Zhuo's back, and in the bronze mirror the reader sees what Cao Cao does not: the tyrant's one open eye, and the blade. Dong Zhuo rolls over roaring, Lü Bu's shadow already in the doorway. The page's one dominant turn is the bluff: Cao Cao is on both knees before anyone can move, the dagger lying flat across his two open palms like a present, smiling, offering it as a gift. Dong Zhuo, greedy, turns it in his fat fingers and admires the jewels, calling Lü Bu "my son," and Cao Cao, rising and bowing, asks to try the new horse once around the courtyard. The kneeling offer owns the page; the mirror and the roar are its fuse. The reader turns because Cao Cao has lied his way out and is about to be on a fast horse.

## PAGE 25

Illustrated prose. Cao Cao gallops out through the great city gate in a cloud of dust, crouched low over a fast horse, the guards turning too late; that escape is the dominant image and owns the page. Below it, smaller, Dong Zhuo sits up on his couch frowning at the jeweled dagger, Lü Bu beside him. The prose carries the turn: the ride once around the courtyard that never stopped, the slow question of why a man would kneel with the blade already out of its sheath, Lü Bu's flat answer, "he was going to kill you, Father," and by nightfall Cao Cao's face painted on every gate with a price on his head. The prose sits over sky and dust, never over the rider. The reader turns because the whole empire is now hunting Cao Cao.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. At dusk a dusty, hollow-eyed Cao Cao reaches a modest farm gate, and a thin little old man throws it open with his arms flung wide: his father's sworn brother from their young days, delighted to see "little Cao Cao." Cao Cao warns him there is a price on his head; the old man will not hear of danger in his house. The page's one dominant turn: the old man takes him in and rides off on a grey donkey with an empty wine jar to fetch the good wine from town, promising to be back by morning, telling him to rest while the family looks after him. The final image owns the page: the old man beaming as he goes, and Cao Cao alone in the courtyard watching him, one hand on his sword. The welcome is warm and talkative; the hand on the sword is the quiet weight. The reader turns because a kind man has left a hunted man alone in his house.

## PAGE 27

Dramatic. Night. Cao Cao lies awake on a mat; through the thin wall comes lamplight and the sound of a knife on a whetstone, and a whisper about tying the legs and one quick cut. He hears "tie," "cut," and "he," and does not wait. His drawn sword comes through a lamplit doorway toward an old woman and a young man still holding the knife he was sharpening, startled and alive, and the panel ends there; nothing of the killing is shown. The page's one dominant turn is the dawn: a small empty kitchen, a live pig tied by its legs on the table, a cleaver and the whetstone beside it, and Cao Cao in the doorway with his sword lowered, looking at it. That still image owns the page; the night is its approach. The reader turns because Cao Cao has killed a whole family for nothing, and the old man is still out buying wine.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. Morning on the road between willows. Cao Cao rides out fast and meets the old man coming home on his donkey with a full wine jar and a basket of fruit, waving, calling that he told them to kill a pig. Cao Cao reins in and says he is sorry; the old man asks, puzzled, sorry for what. The page's one dominant turn: Cao Cao above him, sword drawn, face calm, explaining that the old man would go home, see, and tell the world, and saying the line that defines him: he would rather betray the whole world than let the world betray him. The old man's open, puzzled face is under it. The blow is never shown; the last image is an empty road, the donkey wandering off, the wine jar rolling in the dust, and the old man never came home. The confrontation owns the page. The reader turns because only they saw it.

## PAGE 29

Illustrated prose. Cao Cao reaches home, raises an army, and writes to every lord in the empire calling on the loyal to march against Dong Zhuo. The dominant image owns the page: a camp under black-and-indigo banners, riders galloping out of it in every direction with letters in their coats, and Cao Cao on a rise watching them go. Below, smaller, the dusty market of Zhuo County, years later and unchanged, where Liu Bei still sits at his sandal stall under the same wall and takes a sealed letter, one of a bundle, from a dust-covered rider. The prose carries the turn and its irony: nobody there knows what Cao Cao did on the road. The prose sits over the sky above the camp. The reader turns because the letter has reached Liu Bei, and he has waited years for it.

## PAGE 30

Dramatic. In the peach garden, now in full green leaf, Liu Bei reads the letter aloud with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei leaning in on either side. Zhang Fei is outraged that Dong Zhuo is the fat man they saved; Guan Yu notes the letter is from the man who smiles too quickly. The page's one dominant turn belongs to Liu Bei: on his feet, the letter crushed in his hand, his face fierce and hungry, saying that nobody ever sent for them, Cao Cao sent for everybody, and he does not care who sent it: they go. That close, hungry face owns the page; it is the first time his want shows. Then Guan Yu rises and takes his Green Dragon blade from a peach tree, and Zhang Fei whoops and runs for his spear. The reader turns because the brothers are marching against the man they saved, at the call of a man they don't know.
