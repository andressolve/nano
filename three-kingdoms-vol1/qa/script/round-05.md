## 2. Cold read

China is starving and burning; rebels in yellow headbands march down the roads. In a market a sandal-seller, Liu Bei — descended from princes, owning nothing — reads a call for volunteers eleven times. A loud rich butcher, Zhang Fei, teases him for sighing at a wall and drags him off to drink; a huge red-faced man, Guan Yu, storms into the tavern — he killed a bully and has been running five years. The three talk all night, and the next morning in Zhang Fei's peach garden swear to be brothers and to die on the same day. Zhang Fei sells everything for weapons; five hundred farmers join. Liu Bei's first plan uses himself as bait, and the ambush breaks fifty thousand rebels. A clever, amused stranger, Cao Cao, wins their next battle before they arrive by setting the grass on fire, and notices them; Zhang Fei likes him, Guan Yu thinks he "smiles too quickly." The brothers save a fat general, Dong Zhuo, who learns they have no rank, sends them a bag of rice, and turns his back; Liu Bei stops Zhang Fei attacking him, hands shaking. They go home with nothing.

Five years later Dong Zhuo rides his horse up the palace stairs, swaps the emperor for the emperor's nine-year-old brother, and when one lord, Yuan Shao, defies him and walks out, buys the unbeatable warrior Lü Bu with a red horse called Red Hare; Lü Bu kills the old man who raised him and kneels to call Dong Zhuo "Father." Cao Cao tries to stab Dong Zhuo, is betrayed by a mirror, turns the dagger into a "gift," and gallops away. On the run he shelters with his father's sworn brother, hears a whisper through the wall about tying and cutting, kills the whole family — then finds the pig they were preparing for him. He meets the old man on the road and kills him too, because "I would rather betray the whole world than let the world betray me." Then he writes to every lord in the empire to rise. The letter reaches the sandal stall; Liu Bei doesn't care who sent it: "We go."

At Hulao Gate eighteen lords led by Yuan Shao sneer at the "sandal-seller." Guan Yu volunteers against Dong Zhuo's champion and returns with his helmet before Cao Cao's wine cools; the lords throw the brothers out anyway. That night Cao Cao asks them to fight with him, not Yuan Shao; Liu Bei dodges. Lü Bu rides out and beats every champion. Zhang Fei calls him "slave of three fathers" and fights him fifty times; Guan Yu joins for thirty more; Yuan Shao orders Liu Bei to stay back; Liu Bei rides in anyway. The three together surround Lü Bu; Liu Bei flinches, Lü Bu escapes through the gap and slams the gate. Afterwards Cao Cao reveals he watched from his own hill "to see what you would do," and tells Liu Bei there are only two heroes in the camp — the two of them. Liu Bei says he's only a man who sells sandals. That night he is ashamed of how much he wanted Cao Cao to be right. At dawn the brothers ride to the gate; Cao Cao watches from behind; Liu Bei doesn't turn around.

**Where I stopped:**
- p7 — prose says "three weapons," then lists four blades. I counted; so will a child.
- p26 — the young man in the lamplit room holds a whetstone; then the kitchen has a whetstone beside the pig. Which room was the sharpening in?
- p29 — "fix our mistake" — momentary "which mistake?"; resolved instantly from panel 1 (saving Dong Zhuo).
- p47 — panel 1, Liu Bei does not take his cup; panel 4, he's "looking down into his cup." Did he pick it up? The most important silent beat in the movement is left to the artist.

None of these stopped the story; all are nonblocking below.

## 3. Is it alive?

**Movement I (pp 1–8): Yes.** Pages 2–5 are a genuine meet-cute with real appetite under it. The eleven readings and Zhang Fei's "I counted!" mean Zhang Fei was watching him all morning before he walked over. Want-from works in every scene: Zhang Fei wants a companion worth drinking with, Liu Bei wants someone to say he is worth following, Guan Yu wants wine and then a cause. Liu Bei tests Guan Yu with "the emperor whose soldiers have been chasing you?" — the right question. "That's only a hundred each" is funny and only Zhang Fei's. I'd read it.

**Movement II (pp 9–15): Yes.** "Somebody stole our battle!" is a perfect entrance cue for Cao Cao, and "He smiles too quickly" plants something that pays for forty pages. Page 14 is the best page of the first half: the heat is explicit — pride versus becoming the rebel — and "Then why are your hands shaking, Big Brother?" is subtext delivered by the one character who never hides anything. The p15 prose earns its silence.

**Movement III (pp 16–21): Yes, and gripping without the protagonist.** The stable seduction (p19, "Do you? A real father would give his son everything") is temptation written as dialogue, not explained. Page 20 lets the seal tell us about the killing without showing it — exactly the owner's rule. Cao Cao's murmur ("For a horse.") gives the reader a witness to hold on to.

**Movement IV (pp 22–29): The strongest movement.** The dagger is a set piece of nerve; the farmhouse is the moral center of the book. The whisper is now genuinely ambiguous (pig or sleeping man), the family is seen alive at the doorway, and the turn to the empty kitchen is devastating. The uncle's sworn brotherhood with Cao Cao's father mirrors the brothers' oath without anyone pointing at it. "Uncle, I'm sorry." / "Sorry? Sorry for what?" is chilling. Then p29 turns the irony on our heroes: only the reader knows what the letter-writer did, and Liu Bei's hunger ("Nobody sent for us. Cao Cao sent for everybody.") makes him go anyway. That's dramatic irony with a real engine.

**Movement V (pp 30–37): Yes.** The book's humor and tactics shine: "I'll put his hat in the soup." / "Not a word, Third Brother. Not one." The wine-cup wager gives the duel a visible clock; p34's steam is the right object for it and doesn't depend on a tiny prop. Cao Cao's night visit (p36) has real heat — he wants to recruit them, Liu Bei dodges — and Guan Yu's "he went on bowing" is in voice. Dong Zhuo laughing, then not laughing (p37), is a nice small turn.

**Movement VI (pp 38–45): Yes.** The fight escalates by changing who is in it: one brother, two, three. The insult gives the fight a personal edge. Page 43 is the protagonist's decision — forbidden, he cites the oath, he rides down — and it's felt as chosen, not reported. The flinch (p45) is a quiet failure that gets its aftermath on p48.

**Movement VII (pp 46–49): Yes — the quietest movement, which is correct.** "Next time, come at me one at a time." / "No." is the best two-line laugh in the book. Page 47 is the volume's real duel: "Where were you?" / "On my hill, watching… Now I know," then flattery as a weapon, and "I am only a man who sells sandals." / "Of course you are." The final turn is interior, the prose page states the shame where a picture couldn't, and the last image — a man refusing to turn his head — lands.

**Overall:** the script is alive, funny, morally interesting, and paced so the next page is wanted. The protagonist has a clear appetite (to be seen, to be sent for, to be called a hero), and the book tests it against his oath in the climax and the final image.

**Presence tests, summary:** Want-from passes in every dramatic scene. Subtext passes (p14, p19, p23, p27, p29, p31, p36, p47). Attribution-strip passes — seven distinct voices (Zhang Fei loud and blunt, "fat pig"; Guan Yu formal and final, "I will…"; Liu Bei humble on top and hungry underneath; Cao Cao quick, amused, and cold; Dong Zhuo greedy and mocking; Yuan Shao pompous and ancestral; Lü Bu vain and terse). Logistics dialogue: none (the p22 horse errand is one line and is Cao Cao's ploy). Aphorism: none free-floating — "I would rather betray the whole world…" is Cao Cao's motive under pressure and only his; "A victory is a victory, Lord Yuan" and "Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu" are his wit; "Among men, Lü Bu…" is spectacle narration. Enacted-not-narrated: every major event is on-panel within the deaths-never-drawn rule. Page-turn: every page has a genuine reason.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

1. **p47 panel 4 — the cup.** Panel 1: "Liu Bei does not take his." Panel 4: "Liu Bei looking down into his cup." As written, the artist decides whether he picked it up — and that gesture is the visible form of the temptation p48 narrates. Taking it would enact the temptation; leaving it would enact refusal. Either works, but choose. Prescription: write the gesture in panel 4 — e.g. "Liu Bei, who would not take the cup, is holding it now, looking down into it." (or, if refusal is intended, "Liu Bei, the cup still untouched beside him, looking down at his hands").
2. **pp 36/40 — how Zhang Fei knows the "three fathers."** On p40 Zhang Fei knows Dong Zhuo became Lü Bu's father. The reader saw it (p20), but no one on-page told Zhang Fei; Cao Cao on p36 says only "kneel to Dong Zhuo." Nobody stumbles because the reader knows — but a character acts on information he never acquired. Prescription, p36 panel 1: "I watched him kneel to Dong Zhuo with his father's seal in his hand — and call that fat man Father."
3. **p26 — two whetstones.** Panel 3: the young man holds a whetstone; panel 4: another lies beside the pig in the kitchen. Prescription: have the young man in panel 3 hold the knife he was sharpening; keep the whetstone in the kitchen. (Or drop "a whetstone" from panel 4.)
4. **p7 — "three weapons."** The prose says the smith made three weapons, then lists two swords, a spear, and a glaive — four blades. Prescription: "the smith made weapons for the three brothers," or "a weapon for each brother."
5. **p29 — "Then we will go and fix our mistake."** The antecedent is in panel 1, two Liu Bei balloons back. Prescription: "Then we will go and fix the mistake we made when we saved Dong Zhuo."
6. **p36 text load.** ~104 words in six balloons — at the top of the dramatic budget (~105 only with large protected balloon lanes). Every line carries cause or character, so nothing should be cut; panel 1 is one speaker with ~41 words in two balloons, so the lanes exist. The prompt-writer for this page needs to reserve them first.
7. **p39 panel 3 — staging load.** The dominant panel carries five staging demands (line breaking, Yuan Shao, Lü Bu turning, brothers by the banner, Cao Cao apart on a separate rise). Cao Cao's absence is restated in dialogue on p47, so the plot doesn't depend on a small background figure, but the page prompt should give Cao Cao's separate hill a clear, readable placement.
8. **Spectacle text (p1 34 words, p6 31, p49 37).** All three are causal or essential (setting; the oath itself; the closing fork). Accept as written; do not trim.
9. **Protagonist absent pp 16–27.** Twelve pages offstage, earned — those pages give the reader what Liu Bei never learns and power pp 29–49. No change needed; recorded so a later gate doesn't mistake it for drift.
10. **Ledger vs. page on Liu Bei's want.** The ledger and the p48 page-turn note say Liu Bei secretly wants "the empire." The page has Cao Cao name him a *hero* and Liu Bei ashamed of wanting that to be true. What's on the page is enough and reads well; the metadata line shouldn't be treated as a deliverable.
