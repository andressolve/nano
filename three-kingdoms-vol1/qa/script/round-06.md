## 1. Cold read

A sandal-seller whose family were princes three hundred years ago reads a call for volunteers eleven times; a loud butcher teases him into a tavern; a red-faced giant on the run for killing a rich bully joins them; that night the butcher offers his peach garden, and in the morning the three swear to die on the same day. They arm five hundred farmers; Liu Bei — the worst fighter — uses himself as bait to beat fifty thousand rebels between two hills. A quick, amused cavalry commander, Cao Cao, beats them to the next battle by burning the grass; he likes them; Guan Yu doesn't trust his smile. They save a fat general, Dong Zhuo, who pays them in rice; Liu Bei stops Zhang Fei from killing him with shaking hands. Five years later the fat general rides his horse up the palace steps, swaps the emperor for a nine-year-old, and buys the unbeatable Lü Bu with a red horse; Lü Bu kills the old man who raised him and calls Dong Zhuo Father. Cao Cao tries to stab Dong Zhuo, is seen in a mirror, turns the dagger into a gift, and rides off on the fast horse he asked for; at his uncle's farmhouse he hears "tie… cut… he," kills the family, finds the pig, and kills the uncle on the road so nobody will tell. Only the reader knows. Cao Cao's letter reaches the sandal stall; Liu Bei decides they go. At Hulao Gate the snobbish Yuan Shao seats them at the end of the bench; Guan Yu kills the champion before the wine goes cold; they're thrown out anyway. Cao Cao tries to recruit them. Lü Bu rides out; Zhang Fei calls him a "slave of three fathers"; fifty clashes, thirty more with two brothers; Liu Bei defies Yuan Shao and rides in; three on one, Lü Bu flinches Liu Bei aside and escapes behind the gate. He asks their names; Guan Yu's answer to "one at a time" is "No." Cao Cao admits he only watched, and calls Liu Bei one of two heroes. Liu Bei is ashamed of how much he wants that to be true, and at dawn rides back to the gate without looking round at the man on the ridge.

**Where I paused** (none are stops requiring repair):
- p26 — reread the whispered caption; the double meaning is the point, and the page fixes it the same page.
- p28–29 — a rider hands Liu Bei a sealed letter, then he says "Nobody sent for us." Settled instantly as "a letter to everybody, not to me" — a man grabbing a general summons as permission. Character, not a gap.
- p39/p43 — briefly: why do the brothers stand on Yuan Shao's rise when he just threw them out? "You stand under my banner, sandal-seller, so you take my orders" settles it.

Nothing else. Paraphrase test (§8.10) passes for every movement.

## 2. Is it alive?

**Movement I (pp 1–8): Yes, and warm.** "He read it eleven times. I counted!" is the best introduction gag a sidekick could have — it shows Zhang Fei watched Liu Bei before approaching, and turns p2's prose into a friendship. Want-from: Zhang Fei wants a reason to fight and a big brother; Liu Bei wants to be told he is worth following (p5); Guan Yu wants a cause worth the man he killed. p8's battle plan is logistics turned to heat by "You're the worst fighter of the three of us!" / "That's why they'll chase me." I'd read it.

**Movement II (pp 9–15): Yes.** "Hey! Somebody stole our battle!" is the funniest line in the book and introduces Cao Cao by outrage before he speaks. "He smiles too quickly" is planted cleanly and pays on p29 and p36. p14's rice is a real sting, and "Then why are your hands shaking, Big Brother?" shows Liu Bei's appetite through the one person who saw it — the subtext test passed in a single line.

**Movement III (pp 16–21): Yes.** Clean villain story. Lü Bu's "He raised me. I have a father." / "Do you?" is the heat of the movement; the seal on the floor enacts the murder without drawing it (owner constraint honored); Cao Cao's murmured "He killed the man who raised him. For a horse." gives the book its later moral reaction on-page.

**Movement IV (pp 22–29): The best thing in the book.** "My horse is old and slow" is a lie that clears the room — the reader may only catch it on the next page, which is the pleasure. The farmhouse is genuinely frightening, and because the uncle is introduced as another sworn brother, the movement quietly tests the peach-garden oath against Cao Cao. "Uncle, I'm sorry." / "Sorry? Sorry for what?" is subtext at full strength. p29 restores the protagonist's hunger the moment he returns. This is also where the reader's dramatic irony is built: everything Cao Cao says afterward is heard by a reader who knows about the road.

**Movement V (pp 30–37): Yes.** The hot wine is a true set piece with suspense carried by steam and drums, not drawn combat. Yuan Shao's snobbery is specific and funny ("I was told he sells sandals"; "We are lords, not butchers!"). p36 is the second half's heat: Cao Cao wants the brothers; Liu Bei evades; Guan Yu's "he went on bowing" is a voice nobody else in the book could have.

**Movement VI (pp 38–45): Yes.** The escalation (50 → 30 → three) is countable and felt; Liu Bei's "My brothers have fought eighty times while I sat here" turns arithmetic into shame and decision. The flinch on p45 is small, enacted, and paid on p48.

**Movement VII (pp 46–49): Yes.** Lü Bu asking "Who are you?" grants the brothers recognition from their enemy that no lord gave them. "No." is earned. Cao Cao's "Of course you are" is the best subtext line in the script. The ending is a moral fork, not a battle result — right for a first volume, and p48 has made the stakes interior and specific.

**Presence tests across the book:** Attribution-strip passes on every dramatic page — seven distinct speaking voices (Zhang Fei loud and naming things, "fat pig"; Guan Yu formal and final; Liu Bei courteous, hunger leaking in questions; Cao Cao quick and cold within a sentence; Dong Zhuo mocking and greedy; Yuan Shao ancestral; Lü Bu cold, vain, brief). Aphorism: "I would rather betray the whole world than let the world betray me" (p27) is the only epigram-shaped line, and it fails the "any other mouth" test in the right direction — only Cao Cao could say it, and he says it to his victim as justification, not to the reader as theme. No logistics dialogue. Protagonist appetite never serene (p2, p5, p14, p29, p43, p47, p48). Every page has a real page-turn reason.

## 5. Architecture and balance

Each page declares exactly one mode, one turn, one dominant panel, and at most two locations; every two-location page (p7, 15, 21, 24, 28, 45) is a transition page or a single field of action. Mode share 30 dramatic / 9 prose / 10 spectacle = 61/18/20 — near target, the spectacle surplus earned by the battle set pieces. Dramatic word counts mostly within 45–80; p29 (~84), p31 (~84), p33 (~89), and p4 (~84) run slightly over. Illustrated prose all within 120 except p7 (~121, under the 140 ceiling). Spectacle text on p1, p6, p49 (31–37 words each) is acceptable for full-page panels (title card, oath, closing caption). No death is drawn (p9, 20, 26, 27, 32, 39 honor the owner constraint). Unnamed figures carry no balloons. No functional crowd — Yuan Shao is the only lord who speaks. Every consequential beat has aftermath, including Liu Bei's humiliation (p15), his flinch (p48), and the uncle's death (p28, p36, p48).

2. **p41's turn note** says "Zhang Fei is tiring," but the page shows deadlock ("neither gave a single step"); the tiring is shown on p42. Harmless — the reader's real turn reason is "who breaks first?"
3. **p28 panel 2:** consider having the rider hand Liu Bei a letter that is visibly one of a bundle (or the stall wall showing the same letter posted as a notice), removing the brief tension with "Nobody sent for us." Optional; the line already settles it.
4. **p26** has no dialogue (audit warning) — correctly a dramatic page carried by sound, captions, and discovery. No change needed.
5. **Protagonist absence (pp 16–28)** — 13 pages off-page. It earns its keep: the dramatic irony driving pp 36, 47, 48, 49 is built there, and "Nobody there knew what Cao Cao had done on the road" re-anchors it to him. Logged for the milestone critic, not a script defect.
6. **Ledger drift** (not script defects; tidy before packets): Guan Yu's arc still lists "he gives in secret…", Zhang Fei's "now there's a lord"; Cao Cao's arc lists "I count men who win" and his sample voice "Yuan Shao counts ancestors. I count men who win." (the script now has "Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu"); the uncle's arc ends "Why?" (script: "Sorry for what?"); Liu Bei's "page 46" reference is now p47.
7. **Reference planning:** the bed changes between p21 (Dong Zhuo asleep in the emperor's bed) and p22 (lying on a couch in "Dong Zhuo's bedchamber") — the packet should make them one room or two consistently. Liu Bei's white cloak first appears in text on p43; the costume lock already carries it.
