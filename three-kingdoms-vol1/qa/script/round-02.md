## 2. Cold read

A starving empire is overrun by the Yellow Turbans. In Zhuo County, Liu Bei — a sandal-seller whose family were princes three hundred years ago — reads a call for volunteers eleven times. A loud rich butcher, Zhang Fei, mocks and then adopts him; in the tavern they collect Guan Yu, a red-faced giant who killed a bully and has been running five years. After a night of talk they swear brotherhood in Zhang Fei's peach garden. Zhang Fei sells everything to arm them; five hundred farmers join. Liu Bei uses himself as bait and his brothers break fifty thousand rebels between two hills. Cao Cao steals their next battle with a grass fire, notices them, and promises to remember Liu Bei. They save a fat general, Dong Zhuo, who asks their rank and tosses them a bag of rice; Liu Bei holds Zhang Fei's wrist with shaking hands. Five years of nothing. Dong Zhuo rides up the palace stairs, swaps the emperor for a nine-year-old, faces down Yuan Shao (who walks out), and buys Lü Bu with a red horse; Lü Bu kills the old man who raised him. Cao Cao tries to stab Dong Zhuo, is caught in a mirror, turns the dagger into a gift, escapes — and at his uncle's farm mishears a pig being readied, kills the family, then kills the old man on the road home. Nobody but the reader knows. His letter reaches Liu Bei's stall; in the peach garden Liu Bei decides: "Nobody sent for us. Cao Cao sent for everybody." At Hulao Gate eighteen lords laugh at the sandal-seller; Guan Yu kills Dong Zhuo's champion before a cup of wine goes cold, and they're thrown out for it. Cao Cao recruits them; Liu Bei doesn't answer. Lü Bu rides out and beats every champion; Zhang Fei calls him a "slave of three fathers" and fights him fifty times; Guan Yu joins; Liu Bei finally comes down the hill; Lü Bu escapes through the gap Liu Bei's flinch leaves. From the wall Lü Bu asks who they are. Cao Cao tells Liu Bei they are the only two heroes in the camp. Liu Bei is ashamed of how much he wants that to be true. At dawn the three face the shut gate, and Liu Bei doesn't turn round to look at Cao Cao.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p17** — "This emperor is weak and foolish. His little brother is nine…" spoken while Dong Zhuo is lifting a boy onto the throne. I read the boy as "this emperor," then looked for a little brother. Had to reread the staging to find the young man being led off at the frame edge. (Finding 1.)
- **p34** — the open tent flap shows the result: "a helmet flying up into the air above another rider." I knew Guan Yu had won before the drums stopped — and then wondered whose helmet it was. (Finding 2.)
- **p36** — "A man who'd kill his own father for a horse? Tomorrow I'll tell him so, to his face," spoken while Zhang Fei is glaring at the flap Cao Cao just went through. For a beat I thought Zhang Fei was calling *Cao Cao* a father-killer. (Finding 3.)
- **p31** (small) — Cao Cao: "He came when you called, Lord Yuan" — but the reader knows Cao Cao sent the call. (Nonblocking.)
- **p42–43** (small) — Liu Bei sits still on the hill through eighty exchanges; I wanted the reason. (Nonblocking.)

## 3. Is it alive?

**Yes — this is the best draft of this book I could have hoped to read cold.** I would read it, and I wanted the next page through most of it. The three findings below are cheap fixes on a living script; none is deadness.

- **Movement I (pp 1–8): yes.** Zhang Fei carries it — "He read it eleven times. I counted!" is the line where I started to like them all. Want-from works on every page (Zhang Fei wants a big brother, Liu Bei wants to be told he is worth following, Guan Yu wants a cause). Attribution-strip passes: three distinct voices. p8's "You're the worst fighter of the three of us!" / "That's why they'll chase me" is strategy as character, not logistics.
- **Movement II (pp 9–15): yes.** p14 is the heart of the first half — the snub enacted, the wrist held, and Zhang Fei naming the subtext: "Then why are your hands shaking, Big Brother?" Heat in one sentence: whether Liu Bei will swallow a humiliation to stay loyal to the throne. p15's "It was the fat general they had saved" is a good closing turn.
- **Movement III (pp 16–21): yes, with one stumble.** The Red Hare seduction on p19 is properly tempting — "Do you? A real father would give his son everything" is Dong Zhuo's voice and no one else's. p20's seal on the floor is enacted, not reported, and Cao Cao's murmur sets up the irony that pays at Hulao. Stumble: p17's deixis (Finding 1).
- **Movement IV (pp 22–29): the best movement in the book.** The mirror, the gift-as-bluff, the farmhouse — dark, gripping, deaths never drawn. "Uncle, I'm sorry." / "Sorry? Sorry for what?" is the most frightening exchange in the volume. p29 returns Liu Bei with real appetite, and the reader now knows something about Cao Cao the brothers never will.
- **Movement V (pp 30–37): yes, with two fixable injuries.** The wine cup is a great clock; p33's "I will be back before it is cold" is a boast with Guan Yu's voice all over it. But p34 spends the suspense early (Finding 2) and p36's best irony page has an antecedent hole (Finding 3). p36's irony itself is superb: Cao Cao condemning Lü Bu for killing the man who raised him, eight pages after the farmhouse.
- **Movement VI (pp 38–45): yes.** Spectacle-heavy but earned; p40's "slave of three fathers" now runs on information Zhang Fei got on p36 — good causal work. p43 is Liu Bei's appetite finally boiling over: "I will not spend it standing on a hill."
- **Movement VII (pp 46–49): yes.** Guan Yu's "No." is the purest statement of the oath in the book. p47: "Of course you are." — subtext at its best. p48's "one who poured wine and might be anything" works because the reader knows exactly what he is. Quiet ending with a genuine moral fork.

**Presence-test results:** Logistics dialogue: none blocking (p22's "choose a fast horse for Cao Cao" is load-bearing setup with a motive). Aphorism dialogue: p27's "I would rather betray the whole world…" is earned in scene — Cao Cao's own self-justification, immediately after his motive is spelled out; p35's "A victory is a victory, Lord Yuan, whoever wins it" leans epigrammatic but is Cao Cao needling the leader. Neither blocks. Serene protagonist: no — Liu Bei is hungry on pp 2, 5, 14, 29, 43, 48.

## 4. Mandatory findings

**1. p17 — "This emperor" points at the wrong person.**
- **Failure:** Panel 1 has Dong Zhuo lifting the nine-year-old onto the throne while saying "This emperor is weak and foolish. His little brother is nine years old, and much cleverer." The actual emperor — "a young man in imperial robes" — is only "at the edge of the frame," being led away. p15 said the old emperor died but never said a young emperor succeeded him.
- **Reader harm:** "This" deictically lands on the boy in Dong Zhuo's hands, so the boy reads as the weak emperor, and then the reader searches for a "little brother" who isn't there. They must reverse-engineer from staging. Who is on the throne matters for the rest of the book ("the boy emperor," pp 21, 30, 48). Violates §7.4 (protect antecedents) and §8 test 1 (effortless first read).
- **Prescription:** Have Dong Zhuo point with his free hand at the young man being led out, and make that figure a clear second presence, not a frame-edge detail. Rewrite the lines along these lines: "The old emperor's son is weak and foolish. He is emperor no longer." / "This is his little brother. He is nine years old, and much cleverer. He will sit here now, and I will stand beside him."

**2. p34 — the spectacle shows the result and kills the wine-clock.**
- **Failure:** Through the tent flap the page shows Guan Yu's blade sweeping "and a helmet flying up into the air above another rider." p35 then opens "The drums stopped. Then the tent flap opened" as if the outcome were in doubt.
- **Reader harm:** The two-page set exists for one question — will he be back before the wine is cold? Showing the result on p34 spends the suspense a page early, and p35's helmet-on-the-table becomes a repeat instead of a reveal (fails §8b.7, page-turn). The flying helmet is also a tiny-prop problem at thumbnail scale (§8 test 4): a reader who can't tell whose helmet it is may think Guan Yu lost, and then p35 reads as a contradiction.
- **Prescription:** Restage the view through the flap: the two riders charging at each other on the far plain, swallowed in a wall of dust at the moment they meet — no result visible. Keep the steaming cup in the foreground and the frozen lords; keep the drums caption and BOOM. The helmet's first appearance is p35, on the table.

**3. p36 — Zhang Fei's "him" has two referents and the staging picks the wrong one.**
- **Failure:** Panel 3: "Zhang Fei, left, glaring at the flap" — Cao Cao has just exited through it — says: "A man who'd kill his own father for a horse? Tomorrow I'll tell him so, to his face."
- **Reader harm:** Two men are in play — Cao Cao (just gone through the flap Zhang Fei is glaring at) and Lü Bu (the subject). Read aloud, a listener asks "Tell *who*?" The glare aims the line at Cao Cao, which turns it into an accusation Zhang Fei has no grounds for, and Guan Yu's reply (genuinely about Cao Cao) deepens the confusion. This page carries the book's best irony and sets up p40, so it can't have an antecedent hole. Violates §7.4 and §7.8.
- **Prescription:** Name him, and restage the glare. Zhang Fei faces his brothers / slams a fist into his palm, not the flap. Suggested line: "Lü Bu killed his own father for a horse? Tomorrow I'll call him that to his face, in front of both armies." Keep Guan Yu's line, which now clearly turns the talk to Cao Cao. (The page is already ~100 words, within the ~105 exception — the prompt must protect the balloon lanes; do not cut causal words to make room.)

  - p17 "Do you think mine isn't?" — direct answer to the line just before. Pass.
  - p18 "Then I want him." — "him" follows Cao Cao naming Lü Bu; unique. Pass.
  - p22 "You're late, Cao Cao." — complete. Pass.
  - p27 "Uncle, I'm sorry." / "Sorry? Sorry for what?" — deliberate dread, made explicit next panel. Pass, strongly.
  - p32 "Send out our best!" — complete, object named. Pass.
  - p35 "His helmet. As I promised." — helmet is the visible object, promise from p32–33. Pass.
  - p40 "What did you call me?" — direct answer to the insult. Pass.
  - p42 "Third Brother! I am here!" — complete, arriving. Pass.
  - p46 "No." — direct refusal of "come at me one at a time" in the same panel; forceful. Pass.
  - p47 "Of course you are." — answers "I am only a man who sells sandals"; intentional subtext. Pass.
  - None fail.
- **p31:** "He came when you called, Lord Yuan" — the reader knows Cao Cao sent the call. Readable as Cao Cao flattering the leader, which fits his voice, but it causes a small stop. Consider "He came when we called, Lord Yuan."
- **p42–43:** Liu Bei watches eighty exchanges before he moves; p43's line acknowledges it but doesn't own it. Consider letting him say it ("I have stood here while my brothers fought eighty times…") or let p48's prose name the hill as well as the flinch.
- **p43:** Yuan Shao, just saved by Zhang Fei, still says "sandal-seller." The irony is there but could be missed; one clause would land it — e.g. "Your butcher had no business interfering. Stay back, sandal-seller…"
- **p14:** "So. Dong Zhuo owes his life to you." — third-person self-reference is a slightly visible naming device; acceptable as pomposity.
- **p36 / p48 word counts:** p36 ≈ 100 words (dramatic, ~105 with protected lanes); p48 ≈ 127 prose words (within the 140 exceptional ceiling). Both fine; prompts should reserve the space.
- **p1 (34 words) and p6 (31 words), spectacle:** p6's oath must be spoken; both acceptable as written. Place text over sky/smoke, never over faces.
- **p26, no dialogue:** the captions carry it well. The whispered line ("kill it… before he wakes") lets an alert reader guess the pig before Cao Cao does — dramatic irony, which counts in the scene's favour.
- **Protagonist absent pp 16–28:** 13 pages offstage. It works — the reader carries the farmhouse back to him and it powers pp 31–49 — but watch it at the whole-book gate.
- **Balance:** 61 / 18 / 20 — spectacle slightly above target, all earned. No functional crowd; every named character earns keep (the Uncle most of all, as the book's other sworn brother).
