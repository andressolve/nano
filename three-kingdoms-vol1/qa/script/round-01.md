## 2. Cold read

A starving China burns under a yellow-headbanded army. In a market a tall, quiet sandal-seller named Liu Bei reads a call for volunteers eleven times; his family were princes once. A loud rich butcher, Zhang Fei, hauls him off to drink; in the tavern a red-faced giant, Guan Yu — on the run five years for killing a cruel landlord — announces he's enlisting. They talk all night; Liu Bei asks why anyone would follow a sandal-seller; next morning in Zhang Fei's peach garden they swear to be brothers and to die on the same day. Zhang Fei sells everything for weapons; five hundred farmers join. Liu Bei's first plan uses himself as bait between two hills and breaks fifty thousand rebels. A sharp little lord, Cao Cao, burns a rebel camp before they can attack it, and remembers Liu Bei's name. They save a fat general, Dong Zhuo, who learns they have no rank and tosses them rice; Liu Bei stops Zhang Fei from killing him, with shaking hands. Five years later Dong Zhuo rides his horse up the palace stairs, swaps the emperor for a nine-year-old, and buys the unbeatable Lü Bu with a red horse; Lü Bu kills the man who raised him. Cao Cao tries to stab Dong Zhuo, is caught in a mirror, pretends the dagger was a gift, escapes — then, on the run, hears a knife sharpening in an old family friend's house, kills the household in the dark, finds they were only readying a pig, and kills the old man himself on the road: "I would rather betray the whole world than let the world betray me." He calls the lords against Dong Zhuo; the brothers answer. At Hulao Gate eighteen lords laugh at the sandal-seller; Guan Yu kills Dong Zhuo's champion before a cup of wine goes cold, and they're thrown out anyway. Lü Bu rides out and beats everyone; Zhang Fei calls him a "slave of three fathers" and fights him fifty times; Guan Yu joins; finally Liu Bei — the worst fighter — rides in, and the three together make Lü Bu run back behind the gate. From the wall Lü Bu asks who they are. Cao Cao tells Liu Bei there are only two heroes in the camp — the two of them. Liu Bei says he's only a sandal-seller, and that night is ashamed of how badly he wants it to be true. The gate stays shut.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed:**
- p5 "your great-grandfather" — p2 said three hundred years ago. Stopped.
- p12 "an hour ago" — p10 said midnight; this is dawn.
- p26 "Tie it up first. Then kill it." — why does Cao Cao panic at "it"? Reread. "When it was quiet" — had to infer he killed everyone in the house. Guessed correctly, but guessed.
- p27 — the old man rode out at dusk to "the village" and returns next morning?
- p28 — the brothers gather under the peach trees, but Zhang Fei sold his farm on p7. Whose garden is it?
- p28 — Liu Bei says nothing at the moment the brothers decide to go to war.
- p32 "archer on horseback" — he carries a giant blade. Stopped.
- p33 "BOOM… BOOM…" — what is booming? The page-turn line says "the drums have stopped," but no drums were ever named.
- p39 — how does Zhang Fei know Lü Bu killed the man who raised him, and about the horse? He was in Zhuo County. Flipped back looking for the moment the brothers learn it; it isn't there.
- p41 — how does Lü Bu know Zhang Fei is a butcher?
- p44 — Liu Bei flinches, Lü Bu escapes through the gap; nobody — Liu Bei included — ever registers it.
- p48 — the last line describes the picture.

## 3. Is it alive?

**Yes — this is a good script, and most of it is alive in the way the mandate asks.** The voices separate cleanly under attribution-strip: Zhang Fei is unmistakable ("I'll put his hat in the soup"), Guan Yu speaks in vows, Cao Cao flatters then goes cold, Dong Zhuo talks with his mouth full, Liu Bei asks hungry questions. I would read it. The findings below are holes in the floor, not a dead body.

**Movement I (pp 1–8) — alive.** "He read it eleven times" is the best line in the opening and Zhang Fei's "I counted!" pays it off with a laugh. Liu Bei's want is on the page immediately ("Why would anyone follow a man who sells sandals?"). Heat at the table on p4 (Liu Bei asks a wanted killer whether he'll fight for the emperor chasing him). The bait plan on p8 is exactly the tactical cleverness these readers love, and "a hundred each" is a perfect character entrance. One real flaw: Zhang Fei sells the farm whose garden the book keeps returning to.

**Movement II (pp 9–15) — alive, with the best scene of the first half.** "Somebody stole our battle!" / "You're late" — Cao Cao's entrance is superb. Page 14 is the heart: a bag of rice, a hand on a wrist, and "Then why are your hands shaking, Big Brother?" — subtext doing its job; Liu Bei says "kill him and we are the rebels" while his body says something else. Real heat.

**Movement III (pp 16–21) — alive, gripping, villain-driven.** Dong Zhuo riding up the stairs with a drumstick is a great image; Red Hare is temptation enacted ("What has the old man ever given you? A bed, and orders?"); the seal on the floor is murder told cleanly without a body. My one reservation: the protagonist is gone from p16 to p27 — a quarter of the book. The strand earns its place (see observations), but the brothers' story stops.

**Movement IV (pp 22–28) — the strongest movement in the book, and its central act is narrated, not enacted.** The mirror bluff is wonderful. The uncle is a quietly brilliant choice — the book's other sworn brother — and "I would rather betray the whole world" lands because it's earned, not aphoristic. But p26 hands the most morally serious thing in the volume to one narration line ("When it was quiet"), to a family the reader never sees, triggered by an overheard line whose grammar gives the misunderstanding away. Then p28 gives the movement's hinge to Guan Yu and Zhang Fei while Liu Bei stands there holding the letter, silent — after five years of nothing, the chance he's wanted since page 2 arrives, and he has no line.

**Movement V (pp 29–36) — alive at the peak, sags once.** The wine-still-warm is a perfect set piece; "Pour it and leave it on the table" is Guan Yu entire. Page 33 needs its sound defined. Page 35 is the first page that felt like I'd read it before: the same three-way reaction to Cao Cao as p12, and the same ancestors joke Cao Cao repeats on p46. It's the one place the script runs in place.

**Movement VI (pp 37–44) — alive, a real climax.** "Among men, Lü Bu. Among horses, Red Hare." The escalation — one brother, two, three — is the right shape, and Liu Bei's "I will not spend it standing on a hill" is earned by forty pages of swallowing. But the insult that ignites it (p39) runs on information the brothers were never shown acquiring, and a reader who notices stops at the most exciting moment in the book.

**Movement VII (pp 45–48) — alive until the last line.** Guan Yu's "No." is the funniest and most moving word in the volume. Page 46 is the best subtext in the book — Cao Cao names Liu Bei's secret want aloud, and "I am only a man who sells sandals" means *yes*. Page 47 is honest interior prose. Page 48 then narrates its own picture and makes no final turn; the book ends on a summary instead of a move.

## 4. Mandatory findings

### 1. Page 5 (vs page 2) — Liu Bei's ancestry contradicts itself
- **Failure:** p2 prose: "Three hundred years ago his family had been princes, the sons of emperors." p5 Guan Yu: "I did not come for your great-grandfather."
- **Reader harm:** a great-grandfather is not three hundred years back; an attentive ten-year-old stops on the very page that binds the brothers. (Also, Guan Yu was not present on p3 when Liu Bei mentioned his blood; the "talked all night" caption covers it, but the wrong number draws attention to it.)
- **Prescription:** change Guan Yu's line to "I did not come for your ancestors, the princes. I came for the man who sighed at that wall." Keep p2 as is.

### 2. Page 7 (vs pages 28, 47–48) — Zhang Fei sells the farm, but the peach garden remains the brothers' home
- **Failure:** p7: "that same week he sold his shop and his farm." The garden is "behind my house" (p5). On p28 the letter comes "to three brothers under the peach trees" in Zhuo County, five years later, and the panel stages them there.
- **Reader harm:** the reader must invent why the brothers still have the garden after selling the land it stands on — a continuity hole in the book's central symbol.
- **Prescription:** p7: "he sold his shop, his fields, and his cattle — everything but the house and the peach garden behind it." (This is also a character beat — Zhang Fei keeps the garden — so it's an upgrade, not just a fix.)

### 3. Page 12 (vs page 10) — fire timing
- **Failure:** p10: "At midnight the wind rose… the grass began to glow." p12, grey dawn, Cao Cao: "I set the grass on fire an hour ago."
- **Reader harm:** a time contradiction two pages apart, undercutting the cleverness of the best entrance in Movement II.
- **Prescription:** "You're late. I set the grass on fire at midnight, and the wind did the rest."

### 4. Pages 25–27 — the farmhouse killings are narrated, the trigger line gives the misunderstanding away, and the victims have no faces (BLOCKER: enacted-not-narrated / antecedent / heat)
- **Failure 1 (p26):** "someone whispering: 'Tie it up first. Then kill it.'" The pronoun "it" makes English resolve the misunderstanding before Cao Cao acts — the reader is left asking *why does he think "it" is him?* The deadly mistake needs a line that genuinely could be about a person.
- **Failure 2 (p26):** the killing of the family is a single narration line — "Cao Cao went through the house in the dark with his sword. When it was quiet, he found the kitchen." (The audit flags p26 as a dramatic page with no dialogue.) The most morally serious act in the volume is summarized by the narrator, and the reader must infer that people died. The owner's rule is *deaths are real but never drawn*; here the death is not real either, because nothing states it happened.
- **Failure 3:** the family exists only as "My family will look after you." The reader never sees one of them, so the horror is abstract.
- **Failure 4 (p25 → p27):** the old man rides to "the village" at dusk and returns "Morning," saying "we'll eat in an hour" — an unexplained overnight errand.
- **Reader harm:** the reader must solve the misunderstanding, infer the deaths, and accept an impossible errand — in the movement that establishes the book's central moral ambiguity (Cao Cao).
- **Prescription:**
  (a) Overheard line: "Is he asleep? Good. Tie it up tight first, so it can't struggle — then kill it quickly, before he wakes." ("he" aims the danger at Cao Cao; the reader, who sees the pig on the next panel, then *understands* the mistake rather than puzzling at it.)
  (b) Give the family faces without drawing a death: before the kitchen, a panel where Cao Cao's sword comes through a lamplit doorway and two or three household members — an old woman, a son with the whetstone in his hand — turn toward him, startled and alive. Cut away there.
  (c) State the fact in words on the dawn kitchen panel: "He killed every person in that house. Then he found the kitchen." followed by the existing "They had been getting a pig ready. For him."
  (d) p25: "I'll ride to the town for the good wine — I'll be back by morning!"; p27 keeps "we'll eat in an hour."
  (e) p27 panel 4: keep the empty road but add one caption so the old man's death is real-but-undrawn, e.g. "The old man never came home."
  Split p26 into two pages if the business needs the room — length is not a reason to compress.

### 5. Page 28 — the protagonist is silent at his own decision (BLOCKER: appetite / want-from)
- **Failure:** the letter that ends five years of nothing reaches Liu Bei, and the only lines are Zhang Fei's ("That's the fat pig we saved!") and Guan Yu's ("Then we will go and fix our mistake."). Liu Bei holds the letter. The page-turn line on p28 says "the brothers are going to war" — but Big Brother never decides it.
- **Reader harm:** the movement's hinge belongs to the wrong people; the hunger that should drive the book (p2's eleven readings, p14's shaking hands) doesn't surface at the moment its object finally arrives. And there's no word of what five years of being ignored did to him.
- **Prescription:** give Liu Bei the decision, with the hunger showing — e.g. "Five years I've sold sandals and waited for someone to send for us. Nobody sent for us — Cao Cao sent for everybody. I don't care. We go." Keep Guan Yu's line after it as the vow. (Matches the ledger's arc beat — reads the letter aloud.) If the illustrated-prose page can't carry three balloons cleanly, make p28's letter scene a dramatic page and keep the camp/riders as a prose page before it — add a page rather than cut.

### 6. Page 32 — "He is an archer on horseback, in my company."
- **Failure:** Guan Yu is introduced to the lords as an archer while carrying the Green Dragon blade the book spent pages making famous.
- **Reader harm:** the reader stops to reconcile "archer" with the giant curved blade; the point (he has no rank) gets lost in the confusion.
- **Prescription:** "My brother, Guan Yu. He rides in my company. He has no rank at all." — clearer, and hotter: Liu Bei is choosing to say the humiliating thing aloud.

### 7. Page 33 — sound with no source; false page-turn line
- **Failure:** `SOUND: "BOOM… BOOM… BOOM…"` with no caption or balloon identifying it; the page-turn line ("the drums have stopped") describes something not on the page, and the method requires that line be true of what's on the page.
- **Reader harm:** a first-time reader can't tell war drums from cannon, gate, hooves; the page's real clock (the steam on the cup) competes with a mystery noise.
- **Prescription:** add a small caption: "Outside, the war drums began to beat." On p34, add the beat that the drums have stopped (caption, or fold it into Zhang Fei's entrance: "The drums stopped — and here he is!"). Or rewrite the page-turn line to match what's on the page.

### 8. Page 35 (with pages 12 and 46) — a scene played twice (BLOCKER: repeated point / no new want)
- **Failure:** p35 replays p12's three-way reaction beat for beat — Zhang Fei "Now there's a real lord! I'd follow that man anywhere" (p12: "I like him!"), Guan Yu "I don't trust a man like that" (p12: "He smiles too quickly"), Liu Bei undecided. Cao Cao's "Yuan Shao counts ancestors. I count men who win" is then repeated on p46 ("Yuan Shao has ancestors. Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu"). Cao Cao's want-from on p35 is only to feed them; nothing is asked, nothing at stake.
- **Reader harm:** the one page in the book that runs in place, and it wastes the page that should be handing the brothers the information Finding 9 needs.
- **Prescription:** give Cao Cao a real ask and the brothers new information. He comes with meat and tells them what's coming tomorrow — he was in the throne hall on p20 and saw it: "Tomorrow they'll send out Lü Bu. I watched him kneel to Dong Zhuo with his own father's seal in his hand — he killed the man who raised him, for a red horse. Nobody in this camp will last three blows against him." Then the ask: "When he comes, I want you three with me, not with Yuan Shao." Now Liu Bei must choose, and his non-answer is subtext. Cut the ancestors joke from p35 (it lands better on p46) and Zhang Fei's "real lord" restatement; Guan Yu can carry distrust in a new form ("He watched a son kill his father, and he went on bowing.").

### 9. Page 39 (with pages 41 and 47) — the climactic insult runs on information the reader never saw the brothers acquire (BLOCKER: gap)
- **Failure:** Zhang Fei: "One old man raised you, and you killed him. And a fat pig bought you with a horse." Liu Bei on p47 thinks of "the warrior who had sold his father for a horse." The brothers were in Zhuo County for all of pp 16–27; no page gives them this. Similarly p41, Lü Bu: "Is that all a butcher has?" — knowledge Lü Bu was never given (Zhang Fei has worn black armor since p7).
- **Reader harm:** at the most exciting moment in the book, a careful reader stops to ask *how does he know?* — exactly "recoverable after looking back," a revision flag.
- **Prescription:** Finding 8's p35 rewrite supplies the knowledge. For Lü Bu's line, have Zhang Fei announce himself as he charges on p39: "STOP, you slave of three fathers! Zhang Fei of Zhuo, butcher, is here to carve you!" — then "Is that all a butcher has?" is Lü Bu throwing Zhang Fei's own words back, which is better.

### 10. Pages 44 / 47 — Liu Bei's flinch lets Lü Bu escape, and nobody registers it (aftermath)
- **Failure:** p44 panel 1: "Lü Bu feinting a thrust straight at Liu Bei's face; Liu Bei jerking back; a gap opening in the ring." The protagonist's weakness is the direct cause of the escape, and it gets no reaction — not on p44, p45, or in p47's interior prose.
- **Reader harm:** a protagonist beat without consequence. Either the reader wonders "was it Liu Bei's fault?" and the book never answers, or the beat reads as staging accident.
- **Prescription:** decide it was, and pay it off in one sentence of p47's prose — "And he thought about the moment the halberd came at his face, and how he had flinched, and how Lü Bu had gone through the gap he left." — which sits next to his shame about wanting Cao Cao's praise and deepens it. (Or give Zhang Fei a line on p45 that forgives it before Liu Bei can apologize.)

### 11. Page 48 — the last caption narrates the picture; the book's final page has no turn
- **Failure:** "Hulao Gate was still shut. But the three brothers were in front of it, together." The mandate: narration may not "repeat what picture and dialogue already make clear." The image *is* a shut gate and three brothers side by side. The volume's real fork — Liu Bei tempted by Cao Cao (who sits on the ridge behind him) versus the oath beside him — is carried only by the page-turn line, a note the critic never sees on the rendered page.
- **Reader harm:** the book's last beat is a summary; the fork the whole second half built (pp 46–47) is never decided or stated.
- **Prescription:** make the final caption a choice, not a description — e.g. "Behind them on the ridge, Cao Cao was watching. Liu Bei knew it. He did not turn around." Liu Bei choosing not to look back at the man who flattered him enacts the oath winning tonight while leaving tomorrow open. Ensure Cao Cao in the near corner is legible so the hook doesn't depend on a tiny figure.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

1. **Protagonist absent pp 16–27 (12 pages, a quarter of the book).** The strand earns its place — it's what loads p35, p46 and p47 with dramatic irony ("you know what he did on the road") and gives the volume its villain and its anti-oath (Lü Bu). I'm not prescribing a cut. But if Finding 5 doesn't fully reconnect the reader to Liu Bei at p28, consider one brief Zhuo County beat inside the five-year gap.
2. **Seed Liu Bei holding back before p42.** The p41 page-turn line says "Liu Bei is still on the hill," but no panel on p39–41 shows him. One small reaction panel on p39 or p41 — Liu Bei gripping the reins, not moving, while his brothers fight — would make his p42 charge an overcome fear rather than a late arrival.
3. **The oath (p6) is captioned, not spoken.** The book's central line is set as a caption in "we" while three men kneel with raised cups. A shared spoken balloon, or Liu Bei speaking it for all three, would enact it. Consider also adding what they swear *to do* (protect the people, etc.), so the vow has a purpose as well as an ending.
4. **p1→p2 naming:** p1 says "an army in yellow headbands"; p2's notice says "the Yellow Turbans." The art makes the link, but naming them once in p1's caption ("…the Yellow Turbans, an army in yellow headbands") makes it effortless. (p1's caption is 31 words — flagged by the audit for a spectacle page; it does real orienting work, so trim only if the title card crowds it.)
5. **p10: "marched south to where the biggest rebel army in the north was camped"** — the compass reads oddly. Cut "in the north."
6. **p20: Cao Cao's "For a horse."** He wasn't at the stable; gossip or Lü Bu arriving on Red Hare explains it. A detail in p20 panel 1 (Red Hare visible at the hall doors) would close it for free.
7. **Location tags:** p21 (bedchamber + throne hall) and p44 are effectively two-location pages without the `two —` prefix. Format only.
8. **Mode balance:** 29 / 9 / 10 = 60 / 19 / 21. Spectacle runs over the ~13% target; every spectacle page carries a turn, so no cut prescribed.
9. **Five-word audit — all 17 balloons:**
   - p3 "My name is Liu Bei." — Pass; a pointed correction of "Prince of Sandals," real subtext.
   - p11 "Hey! Somebody stole our battle!" — Pass; complete, funny.
   - p12 "I like him!" — Pass; Cao Cao, leaving, is the only referent.
   - p12 "He smiles too quickly." — Pass; character note in Guan Yu's voice, not an aphorism.
   - p13 "Whose general is he?" — Pass; visible in the circle.
   - p13 "Does it matter whose? Ride!" — Pass; direct answer.
   - p17 "Do you think mine isn't?" — Pass; mirrors Dong Zhuo's complete question.
   - p18 "Then I want him." — Pass; "him" = Lü Bu, named in the preceding line.
   - p22 "You're late, Cao Cao." — Pass.
   - p27 "Uncle, I'm sorry." — Pass; deliberately incomplete, the next balloon asks "for what?"
   - p27 "Sorry? Sorry for what?" — Pass.
   - p31 "Send out our best!" — Pass after the caption.
   - p34 "His helmet. As I promised." — Pass; object on the table, promise made on p31.
   - p39 "What did you call me?" — Pass.
   - p41 "Third Brother! I am here!" — Pass.
   - p45 "No." — Pass, and the best word in the book; answers a complete request in the same panel.
   - p46 "Of course you are." — Pass; intentional subtext, answers a complete line.
10. **Functional crowds / introductions:** none found. The eighteen lords, the messenger, the smith, the old lord, the champion stay unnamed — correct. Each named character is named and given a want before the plot depends on them; Cao Cao (p18) and Yuan Shao (p30) are re-identified well.
