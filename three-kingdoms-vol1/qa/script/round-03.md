## 1. Cold read

Read p1→p49 before opening the ledger or audit.

**The story, as a first-time reader would tell it.** The Han empire is burning; the Yellow Turbans are marching. Liu Bei, a sandal-seller descended from princes, reads the volunteer notice eleven times. Zhang Fei, a loud rich butcher, catches him sighing and drags him to drink; Guan Yu, a red-faced giant who killed a cruel landlord and has run five years, joins them. They swear brotherhood in Zhang Fei's peach garden; Zhang Fei sells everything to arm them; five hundred farmers follow. Liu Bei uses himself as bait between two hills and the farmers beat fifty thousand. Cao Cao beats them to the next battle by burning the grass, and notices them. They rescue a fat general, Dong Zhuo, who asks their rank, sneers "farmers with sticks," and hands them rice; Liu Bei grips Zhang Fei's wrist to stop him, and his own hands shake. Five years later Dong Zhuo rides into the capital, swaps the young emperor for a nine-year-old, faces down Yuan Shao (who walks out), and buys Lü Bu — the warrior nobody can beat — with Red Hare; Lü Bu kills the man who raised him to pay for it. Cao Cao fails to stab Dong Zhuo, talks his way out with the "gift" of the dagger, flees — and at his sworn uncle's farmhouse hears a knife sharpening, kills the family, finds they were tying a pig for him, then kills the old man on the road: "I would rather betray the whole world…" His call to arms reaches the sandal stall; the brothers join eighteen lords at Hulao Gate, are mocked; Guan Yu brings back the enemy champion's helmet before the wine is cold, and the three are thrown out anyway. Cao Cao asks them to stand with him, not Yuan Shao; Liu Bei gives no answer. Lü Bu rides out; Zhang Fei saves Yuan Shao and calls Lü Bu a "slave of three fathers"; Guan Yu joins after fifty bouts; Liu Bei finally rides down from the hill; the three drive Lü Bu back through the gate. Lü Bu asks their names; Guan Yu answers "No." Cao Cao tells Liu Bei they are the only two heroes in the camp; Liu Bei is ashamed of how much he wants that to be true. At dawn the three face the gate, and Liu Bei does not turn to look at Cao Cao.

**Where I stopped or reread:**
- **p42 panel 4 / p43** — Liu Bei is "on the hill under the gold banner," beside Yuan Shao. I flipped back to p36: Cao Cao said "I want you three with me. Not with Yuan Shao." Did Liu Bei just answer Cao Cao by standing with Yuan Shao? And where is Cao Cao? He appears nowhere from p38 to p46. When he says on p47 that "Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu," I asked: did Cao Cao fight Lü Bu? This is the one real stop in the book (Finding 1).
- **p49** — brief "what are they doing at the gate at dawn?" I inferred they mean to call Lü Bu out again from p46's "We're not finished!" (nonblocking).
- **p29** — "I don't care who sent it" made me pause a beat; it reads as Liu Bei arguing down his own hurt pride, which works (nonblocking note on heat below).

Nowhere else did I have to reconstruct anything — including p17's two emperors, p26's "he"/"it" whisper (a clue the reader can catch before Cao Cao does), and p45's flinch paid off on p48.

## 2. Is it alive?

**Yes, throughout.** This is a book I would turn the pages of. Movement by movement:

**I — The Peach Garden (pp 1–8).** Warm, funny, and the appetite is on the surface. The eleven readings / "I counted!" is a great small invention — narration fact turned into a character's observation. Voices separate instantly: Zhang Fei is loud and joking ("That's only a hundred each"), Guan Yu formal and final, Liu Bei humble with a visible hunger. Want-from: Zhang Fei wants a drinking companion and a cause; Guan Yu wants a man worth following; Liu Bei wants someone to tell him he's worth following. Page-turns are strong on every page; the p8 plan has real heat ("You? You're the worst fighter of the three of us!" / "That's why they'll chase me.").

**II — Five Hundred (pp 9–15).** Moves well. Cao Cao's p12 arrival is a rival with charm, and "He smiles too quickly" plants the doubt in the right mouth. p14 is the best dramatic page of the first half: the insult is enacted (his back, the fig, the rice), Liu Bei's restraint is a choice with a cost, and "Then why are your hands shaking, Big Brother?" is subtext delivered by the character least likely to notice it — who notices it. p15 earns its prose: the brothers come home with nothing, and the turn ("It was the fat general they had saved") is a proper hook.

**III — The Red Horse (pp 16–21).** Chilling, and the oath theme works without anyone stating the theme: Lü Bu is the anti-oath — a father traded for a horse. Dong Zhuo's "A real father would give his son everything" is greedy and specific to him. The seal on the floor (p20) lets the reader infer the murder without seeing it; the volume constraint is honoured cleanly. Cao Cao's murmured line closes it in his cold register. The protagonist is absent here; Dong Zhuo and Cao Cao carry it, and it holds (see observations).

**IV — The Dagger (pp 22–29).** The best movement in the book, and among the best of its kind I have read in this pipeline. The mirror is planted and paid inside one page turn. "My horse is old and slow" is Cao Cao manipulating Dong Zhuo into sending Lü Bu away — felt, not told. The farmhouse sequence mirrors the brothers' oath ("Your father and I swore to be brothers") and lets the reader catch the pig before Cao Cao does, so the horror is dramatic irony rather than twist. "Uncle, I'm sorry." / "Sorry? Sorry for what?" is the best subtext in the volume. p28's "Nobody there knew what Cao Cao had done on the road" converts everything Cao Cao does from here on into suspense.

**V — Eighteen Lords (pp 30–37).** Fun, high status-heat. Yuan Shao's pompous voice ("I was told he sells sandals") passes attribution-strip easily. "I'll put his hat in the soup" / "Not a word, Third Brother. Not one." is character, humour, and restraint in two lines. The warm-wine set piece is staged so the reader waits inside the tent with the lords — exactly right. p36: Cao Cao's recruiting pitch has a real want-from, Liu Bei's careful non-answer is subtext, and Guan Yu's "he went on bowing" lands with double weight because the reader knows about the farmhouse.

**VI — Lü Bu (pp 38–45).** Big, legible spectacle with dialogue that carries heat. "Slave of three fathers" is an insult a reader can unpack in two lines; Lü Bu's "Now it's almost a fight" is vain and specifically his. Liu Bei's p43 turn is earned ("My brothers have fought eighty times while I sat here") — the climax of his appetite. **The thread the movement drops:** the Cao Cao question p36 explicitly hooked. See Finding 1.

**VII — The Gate (pp 46–49).** Lands. Guan Yu's "No." is the line of the book — the oath answered in one word to the man who has no oath. p47's "Of course you are" is Cao Cao in three words. p48's prose names Liu Bei's flinch and his shame — a protagonist with appetite and conscience, nowhere near serene. p49 is a quiet, earned ending — and Finding 1 would make it stronger, because the reader would have *watched Cao Cao not ride*.

**Presence-test summary.** Logistics dialogue: none that blocks (the p8 battle plan has opposition and humour; p22's horse errand is a manipulation, not an arrangement). Aphorism: none that blocks — "I would rather betray the whole world…" is Cao Cao's creed spoken at the moment he acts on it; "A victory is a victory…" is pragmatic and his alone; p38's proverb is a caption, not a mouth. Attribution-strip: passes; seven distinct speaking voices. Serene protagonist: no — Liu Bei's restraint is chosen and the script pays it off three times (p14, p43, p48).

## 3. Mandatory findings

### Finding 1 — pp 36 → 39–43 → 47: Cao Cao vanishes from the climax, and the hook p36 sets is dropped

**The failure.** On p36 Cao Cao says, "When he comes, I want you three with me. Not with Yuan Shao." Liu Bei evades; the page-turn names this as the hook ("Cao Cao asked for an answer Liu Bei didn't give"). Then when Lü Bu comes, Cao Cao appears nowhere on pp 38–46. On p42 panel 4 Liu Bei is placed "on the hill under the gold banner" — Yuan Shao's banner — and on p43 he sits beside Yuan Shao, exactly where Cao Cao asked him not to be, with nothing on the page saying whether that was a choice, an accident, or an answer. On p47 Cao Cao reappears, warm and flattering, and says "Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu" — and the reader has no idea whether Cao Cao fought Lü Bu, held back, or watched.

**What the reader must repair.** They must guess where the second pole of the book's moral fork was during the battle that decides it; whether Liu Bei standing under Yuan Shao's banner means anything; and whether Cao Cao's p47 sneer is earned or hypocritical. The ending on p49 — Liu Bei not turning to look at Cao Cao — depends on the reader's picture of what Cao Cao is, and the climax is the best evidence the book could give and gives none. This is "eventually understandable" (the reader can decide it doesn't matter), which is a revision flag, not a pass. It is also a missed piece of heat: the richest irony available — the man who asked for the brothers at his side kept his own cavalry still and watched — is left off the page.

**Prescription** (one panel added, two panels restaged, one exchange added; no page split needed):
1. **p39, panel 3** — add, on a separate rise in the distance, Cao Cao on his horse under black-and-indigo banners, his riders held in check behind him, watching. Not riding. Plainly a second figure apart from Yuan Shao's gold banner. No balloon.
2. **p42 panel 4** — make Liu Bei's position meaningful instead of incidental. Either put the brothers' small company on its own patch of slope near but not under the gold banner (so p43's Yuan Shao has ridden over to him), or keep it under the gold banner and have Yuan Shao's p43 line claim him: "You stand under my banner, sandal-seller, so you take my orders. Stay back, or you will die with them." Either way the reader knows why Liu Bei is where he is.
3. **p47** — before or in place of the "how many heroes" opener, let Liu Bei — who now has the right — ask the question p36 left open. E.g.:
   **LIU BEI:** "You asked us to stand with you when Lü Bu came, Lord Cao. Where were you?"
   **CAO CAO:** "On my hill, watching. I wanted to see what the three of you would do. Now I know."
   Then Cao Cao's "Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu" carries a sting the reader can feel — neither did Cao Cao — and "two heroes" becomes a recruitment, not just flattery. Keep p47 within the dramatic budget; if it no longer fits comfortably, give the exchange its own panel rather than trimming it.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **p29, heat.** The decision has no opposition. Guan Yu's distrust from p12 ("He smiles too quickly") is the natural objection to answering *Cao Cao's* letter, and voicing it would give Liu Bei's "I don't care who sent it" something to push against. Suggested: **GUAN YU:** "Cao Cao's letter. The man who smiles too quickly." → **LIU BEI:** "I don't care who sent it. We go." Guan Yu's "fix our mistake" then reads as him agreeing anyway.
2. **p49** — the caption tells us about Cao Cao but not why the brothers are at the gate. One clause would make the final image self-explaining, e.g. "At dawn the three brothers rode to the gate to call Lü Bu out again. Behind them on the ridge…" (still within a spectacle page's anchor-line allowance).
3. **p26** — the whisper is the trigger of the book's darkest beat and is delivered as a narration caption. Rendering it as a small whisper balloon whose tail comes through the wall from off-panel would enact rather than report it. Keep "SHHK… SHHK…" and the two lower captions as they are. (This would also clear the audit's "dramatic page with no dialogue" warning naturally.)
4. **p4 density** — six balloons, ~96 words, four balloons stacked in panel 3. Within the ~105 ceiling, but panel 3 is the crowded one. Consider moving Guan Yu's introduction ("My name is Guan Yu… I've been running for five years.") into panel 2 alongside Zhang Fei's wave.
5. **p43 panel note** — "his face fierce for the first time in the book" contradicts p29 ("his face fierce and hungry"). Suggest "fiercer than we have ever seen him," so the builder isn't given a false instruction.
6. **p30 troops** — "their five hundred": p10 says more men joined after the first victory, and p15 sends everyone home. Readers will accept that the brothers raised five hundred again; if wanted, p28 or p30 could say "the old five hundred came back."
7. **Protagonist absence pp 16–28** — twelve pages without Liu Bei. It works (Movement IV is the strongest material and p28 returns to the sandal stall with a hook), and is worth noting only so no later revision lengthens it further.
8. **Rescue aftermath** — Yuan Shao's ingrate line on p43 covers his reaction to being saved. If Finding 1's p47 exchange is added, a single Cao Cao clause ("Yuan Shao is telling the camp his guards drove Lü Bu off") would also show the coalition denying the brothers credit once more — the book's engine. Optional.
9. **Spectacle text (audit warnings p1, p6)** — both justified: p1's caption carries the opening orientation; p6's text is the oath itself. No action needed.
