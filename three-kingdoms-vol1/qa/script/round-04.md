## 2. Cold read

An empire is burning; an army in yellow headbands is marching. In a market, Liu Bei — a sandal-seller whose family were princes three hundred years ago — reads a call for volunteers eleven times. A loud rich butcher, Zhang Fei, teases him into a tavern; a red-faced giant on the run, Guan Yu, joins them. That night at Zhang Fei's farm Liu Bei asks why anyone would follow a sandal-seller; Guan Yu says he came for the man who sighed at the wall. Next morning in the peach garden the three swear to die on the same day. Zhang Fei sells everything for weapons; five hundred farmers join. Liu Bei — worst fighter of the three — offers himself as bait and draws fifty thousand rebels into an ambush between two hills. At the next battle someone has already set the grass on fire: Cao Cao, quick and amused, who says he'll remember Liu Bei. The brothers rescue a fat general, Dong Zhuo, who tosses them a bag of rice and turns his back; Liu Bei stops Zhang Fei from attacking him with shaking hands. They go home with nothing.

Five years later Dong Zhuo rides his horse up the palace stairs, replaces the emperor with the boy's little brother, watches Yuan Shao walk out on him, and buys the unbeatable Lü Bu with a red horse; Lü Bu kills the man who raised him. Cao Cao, bowing lowest at court with a jewelled dagger, is caught through a mirror, turns the dagger into a "gift," and escapes. At an old family friend's farm he hears a whetstone and a whisper, kills the household, and finds the pig they were readying for him; on the road he kills the old man too — "I would rather betray the whole world than let the world betray me." Cao Cao's letter reaches the sandal stall; Liu Bei decides they go, whoever sent it.

At Hulao Gate, Yuan Shao leads eighteen lords and sneers at the sandal-seller. Guan Yu brings back the enemy champion's helmet while Cao Cao's wine is still warm; the lords throw the brothers out anyway. Cao Cao comes to their tent and asks them to stand with him when Lü Bu comes. Lü Bu rides out and breaks every champion; Cao Cao holds his riders still on a separate hill; Zhang Fei fights Lü Bu, Guan Yu joins, and Liu Bei sits on Yuan Shao's hill — then rides in. Three against one, Lü Bu runs for the gate through the gap Liu Bei's flinch leaves. Afterwards Cao Cao admits he was "watching," and says there are two heroes in this camp: Liu Bei and himself. Liu Bei can't sleep, ashamed of how much he wants that to be true. At dawn the three ride to the gate, and Liu Bei does not turn around to look at Cao Cao.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p26, the whisper.** Stopped and reread. The whisper says tie up "it," kill "it," "before **he** wakes" — which separates the sleeper from the thing being killed. My first reaction was not dread or "they mean him"; it was "so why does Cao Cao think it's him?" (Finding 1.)
- **p42 panel 4** — brief stop: why are the brothers under Yuan Shao's banner when Cao Cao asked them to stand with him (p36)? p43 answers ("You stand under my banner") and p49 resolves it; recovered within the movement. (Nonblocking obs. 1.)
- **p12→p13** — a small hitch on when the broken-banner hillside happens (not a real stop).

## 3. Is it alive?

**Yes — this is the liveliest script I could ask for at this gate.** Heat on nearly every page; attribution-strip passes easily. Zhang Fei jokes and threatens in one breath ("That's only a hundred each"); Guan Yu is short and final ("No."); Cao Cao flatters and goes cold in a sentence; Dong Zhuo asks questions he already knows the answers to; Yuan Shao talks *about* the brothers rather than to them; Liu Bei is courteous with hunger leaking through.

- **Movement I (pp 1–8): I would read it.** Liu Bei's appetite is in the first image of him (eleven readings), and the brothers come together through comedy and need, not exposition. Guan Yu's "I came for the man who sighed at that wall" earns the oath. Want-from carries pp 3–5: what Liu Bei wants from these two, and whether they want him. p8 ends on a real hook — the worst fighter as bait.
- **Movement II (pp 9–15): I would read it.** "Somebody stole our battle!" is a delight; Cao Cao's entrance is economical and charged; Guan Yu's "He smiles too quickly" is planted perfectly. p14 is the emotional peak: "Then why are your hands shaking, Big Brother?" is real subtext — Zhang Fei says what Liu Bei can't, and the protagonist's hurt is dramatized without being named.
- **Movement III (pp 16–21): I would read it.** "Be my son" vs. "I have a father" is a scene with an engine — both want something from each other right now. The seal on the floor and "He will never say no to anyone again" is chilling and bodiless. Cao Cao's murmur on p20 turns him from a court officer into a man with a motive.
- **Movement IV (pp 22–29): the best of the book.** "My horse is old and slow" is a lie that sets up the rescue; the mirror, the kneeling gift, and the hour-late realization are tactical intelligence the boys will love. The Uncle sequence is the book's dark heart — the other sworn brother, and what Cao Cao does to him is what an oath is worth to Cao Cao. Weakest link in the strongest movement: the whisper (Finding 1), which is why it's a blocker — so much rests on it. p29 is good: Liu Bei "fierce and hungry," and "I don't care who sent it. We go." is the protagonist choosing.
- **Movement V (pp 30–37): I would read it.** The wine steaming through the tent flap is the best spectacle page in the book. Yuan Shao's "Throw these three nobodies out!" immediately after the victory is proper heat. p36's want-from is clear: Cao Cao wants the brothers' swords for himself; Liu Bei deflects ("We will fight beside anyone who does") — good subtext.
- **Movement VI (pp 38–45): I would read it.** The escalation works — fifty bouts, thirty more, then the third brother. p43 is the protagonist's turn, with a fresh insult to push against ("sandal-seller, so you take my orders"); Liu Bei's line pays off the oath and the "worst fighter" setup at once. His flinch on p45 is a fine, unshowy detail.
- **Movement VII (pp 46–49): I would read it.** "Who are you?" / "We are brothers." / "Next time, one at a time." / "No." — a wonderful little scene. p47 is the best dialogue in the book: "On my hill, watching… Now I know" is a confession dressed as a compliment, and "Of course you are" is Cao Cao seeing straight through Liu Bei's modesty. p48's shame is the right ending emotion. The volume ends with Hulao Gate still shut — honest and earned; the fork is the brothers vs. Cao Cao's praise.

**Appetite:** Liu Bei is off-page pp 16–28; it works because Cao Cao's appetite drives the book there and p28 returns the reader to him immediately. Through pp 31–42 he is restrained but never serene — we see him swallowing insults, gripping reins, waiting — and that strain is what makes p43 pay.

## 4. Mandatory findings

**1. p26 — the whisper doesn't support Cao Cao's misreading, so the book's central moral turn doesn't land.**

- **Failure:** The caption reads: *"Is he asleep? Good. Tie it up tight first, so it can't struggle. Then kill it quickly, before he wakes."* The line itself separates "he" (the sleeping guest — Cao Cao) from "it" (the thing being killed). People don't call a person "it." The whisper tells both Cao Cao and the reader that the target is not the sleeper.
- **What a first-time reader must repair:** Two readings, both failures:
  - The reader catches "it" — then the pig reveal ("They had been getting a pig ready. For him.") is spoiled a panel early, and worse, Cao Cao's killings become hard to understand: why would he cut down a family who just said they were waiting for him to stay asleep?
  - The reader skims past "it" — then they understand only after the pig reveal, by going back to reconstruct that Cao Cao must have heard "kill" and nothing else. That is "understandable after looking back" — a revision flag by definition.
- **Why it blocks:** This page establishes Cao Cao's distinct moral responsibility, which the mandate protects (§9): a terrified man's fatal mishearing, made monstrous by what he does next on p27 — or something murkier. The p26→p27 sequence only works if the reader first feels the whisper as a threat, then gets the reveal, then sees Cao Cao choose murder on the road with nothing misheard. Right now the first step doesn't work, and the reader must solve the adaptation at the volume's darkest moment.
- **Prescription:** Rewrite the whisper so the same words could mean a pig or a sleeping man — no "he"/"it" split. For example:
  *"Is he asleep? Good. Tie the legs tight first, so there's no struggling. Then one quick cut, so he never hears a thing."*
  (Truth: they tie the pig and kill it fast so the squealing won't wake the guest. What Cao Cao hears: they'll tie his legs and cut him in his sleep. Every word stays honest about the pig, so the reveal stays fair.)
  Optionally add a short narration line in panel 2 that names his choice without explaining it: *"Cao Cao heard 'tie,' and 'cut,' and 'he.' He did not wait to hear more."*
  Keep the whisper as a caption (the family are scenery, no balloons) and keep the deaths undrawn exactly as staged.

No other mandatory findings.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

1. **pp 36–42 — Liu Bei's choice of Yuan Shao's line over Cao Cao's happens off-panel.** Cao Cao asks for "you three with me. Not with Yuan Shao"; Liu Bei deflects; the brothers first appear on the battlefield at p40/p42 panel 4, already under the gold banner. p43 and p49 make the choice legible, but it would land harder if visible earlier — e.g., on p39 panel 3, put the three brothers on Yuan Shao's rise near the gold banner while Cao Cao is visibly apart. Then p47's "Where were you?" lands from a position the reader has seen.
2. **p12→p13 time marker.** A three-word caption on p13 ("Later that spring," or similar) would prevent any reader from thinking the hillside rescue happens on the same dawn as Cao Cao's fire.
3. **Spectacle text (p1 34 words, p6 31, p49 37).** All three justified: title/orientation, the oath, and the closing fork. p49's caption is the heaviest; "Liu Bei knew it" is interior and earns its place. If anything is trimmed, trim the first sentence (the art already shows "At dawn the three brothers rode to the gate…") rather than the rest. Do not shrink type.
4. **p48 ~125 prose words** — within the 140 exceptional ceiling, justified by its job; no action. Compose the prose field first.
5. **p36 ~97 dialogue words** — within the ~105 protected-lane ceiling; the packet should reserve lanes.
6. **p47 "Ancestors don't fight Lü Bu"** — at the edge of epigram, but it passes attribution-strip: Cao Cao's own mocking register, tied to Yuan Shao's "four generations." Keep.
