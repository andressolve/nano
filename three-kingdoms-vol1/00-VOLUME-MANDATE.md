## The pitch the readers voted for (2026-09-27, `menu/VOTES.md`)

The boys chose this book over nine others. The book must deliver what they
were promised: the falling Han, the Yellow Turbans, a boy emperor; the oath in
a peach garden; Liu Bei the sandal-seller of imperial blood, Guan Yu the
red-faced giant with the crescent blade, Zhang Fei the butcher with the voice;
a few hundred volunteers and no money against Dong Zhuo, who seized the
capital, and Lü Bu on the blood-red horse whom nobody has beaten; Cao Cao,
sometimes with them and sometimes not, hero or villain left open; ambushes,
bluffs, betrayals, fire; the question of who deserves to rule when the old
world breaks; **and an ending at Hulao Gate with the three brothers facing Lü
Bu together.** Everything else is story material.

## Volume constraints (accepted by the owner at greenlight ①, 2026-10-04)

- **Deaths are real and never drawn.** This is a war, and men die in it. No
  blood, no wound, no body, no severed anything is ever drawn. A blow lands in
  the gutter between panels; a rider falls out of frame; a killing happens
  behind a door. Guan Yu brings back the champion's helmet, never his head. A
  page that draws blood or a corpse fails the page gate whatever else it does.
- **The politics are a fat man on a throne that is not his.** No eunuchs, no
  regents, no court offices, no map of commanderies. The reader learns three
  things by watching: the empire is falling apart, the man who saved himself
  by grabbing the capital now holds a nine-year-old emperor like a puppet,
  and every lord who rises against him cares first about his own rank.
- **Names cost.** Eight people have names in this book, plus one horse. Every
  champion, rebel captain, messenger, and lord outside the eight is unnamed.

## The specific failure this volume must avoid

**The chronicle — and the saint.**

The source is a chronicle. It names a general before every duel, reports
battles in a sentence each, and lists lords by the dozen. Adapted faithfully
it becomes a scroll of names and outcomes: "Pan Feng rode out; in three
bouts he was slain." The countermeasure is structural. Eight names. No battle
is reported that is not enacted, and no battle is enacted that does not turn
the three brothers' story. Eighteen lords are one man, Yuan Shao, and a field
of banners.

The second failure is Liu Bei. In the source he is patient, humble, and
virtuous, and a protagonist like that reads as calm and above it all, which
is the engine written out. In this book Liu Bei is **hungry**: twenty-eight,
royal blood, selling sandals, and ashamed of it. Every insult he swallows on
the page costs him something visible, Zhang Fei says aloud what Liu Bei is
thinking, and the climax is the most patient man in the book riding at the
most dangerous one because he swore to. A page where Liu Bei is serene is a
script-gate blocker.
