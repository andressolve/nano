China at the end of the Han, over about five years. Three places matter:
Zhuo County, where the peach garden is; the capital, where the throne is;
and Hulao Gate, the fortress in the mountain pass between them. One time
jump, after Movement II, marked in narration.

## I — THE PEACH GARDEN · pp 1–8

**Emotional entry:** hunger. **Exit:** three men and five hundred farmers on
a hill above fifty thousand yellow headbands, and a plan that makes Liu Bei
the bait.

The empire is burning: the emperor is weak and far away, the land is
starving, and out of the hunger rises an army with yellow cloths around
their heads, chanting that the blue sky is dead. In the market of Zhuo
County a man of twenty-eight with the blood of emperors and the hands of a
sandal-maker reads a notice calling for volunteers, and sighs. A voice like a
thunderclap behind him asks why a grown man sighs at a wall instead of doing
something. The voice is Zhang Fei, a butcher with money and no patience. In
the tavern a red-faced giant bangs the table for wine because he is off to
enlist before the day is out; he is Guan Yu, who killed a bully and has been
running for five years. They talk all night. Liu Bei says what he is ashamed
of: a prince's great-great-grandson who sells sandals, whom nobody would
follow. Guan Yu says he did not come for the grandfather. Zhang Fei says
there is a peach garden behind his house in full bloom. In the morning they
kneel there and swear. Zhang Fei sells everything he owns for three weapons
and five hundred men, and the five hundred march to a hill above fifty
thousand. Liu Bei, the weakest fighter of the three, says he will ride out
with the farmers and run, and his brothers will come down from the hills.

**The turn:** three strangers become brothers who have sworn to die on the
same day.
**Page-turn out:** fifty thousand, and the plan is that Liu Bei runs.

## II — FIVE HUNDRED · pp 9–15

**Emotional entry:** nerve. **Exit:** the fat general they saved marching
on the capital.

Liu Bei rides out and runs and the yellow army chases him between two
hills, and Guan Yu and Zhang Fei come down on it from both sides, and the
rebel captain goes down under Zhang Fei's spear, and five hundred beat fifty
thousand. Their name goes ahead of them. At the great rebel camp in the tall
dry grass somebody has thought of fire before them: the grass goes up in the
night and the rebels run into horsemen under black banners. Their commander
is a small sharp man named Cao Cao, who lit it an hour ago and asks who pays
these farmers, and when Liu Bei says nobody, says that makes them the only
men in this war who came because they wanted to. Riding on, the brothers
find an imperial army broken and a general in gold armor surrounded; they
cut him out. He is Dong Zhuo, and he climbs off his horse panting and asks
what office they hold. None, they are volunteers. He turns his back and
walks into his tent without a word of thanks. Zhang Fei draws to go in after
him, and Liu Bei holds his wrist: the man wears the emperor's armor; kill
him and they are the rebels. The rebellion breaks. The great men take the
rewards. The brothers go home with nothing. Five years pass, the palace
falls into chaos, and the general they saved marches on the capital.

**The turn:** the brothers save the life of the man who will take the
empire, and he despises them.
**Page-turn out:** the man they saved is at the capital's gates.

## III — THE RED HORSE · pp 16–21

**Emotional entry:** dread. **Exit:** an officer bowing lowest of all, with
a dagger in his sleeve.

Dong Zhuo's army pours into the capital and he rides his horse up the
palace steps. In the throne hall he lifts the emperor's nine-year-old
brother onto the throne and has the emperor led away, and one lord stands:
Yuan Shao, whose family has served the throne for four generations. Dong
Zhuo asks whether he thinks his sword is not sharp; Yuan Shao asks whether
Dong Zhuo thinks his is not, and walks out of the capital with his hand on
the hilt. At the banquet an old lord rises to object, and Dong Zhuo reaches
for his own sword and sees, behind the old man, a giant with two long
pheasant plumes and a halberd, looking at him. Dong Zhuo laughs and sits
down. Afterward he asks the young officer beside him who that was, and the
officer, Cao Cao, now at court, says: Lü Bu, the old man's adopted son;
nobody alive can beat him. Dong Zhuo says then he wants him. That night he
walks into Lü Bu's stable leading a blood-red horse. Lü Bu says he has a
father. Dong Zhuo asks: do you? In the morning Lü Bu lays the old man's seal
of command on Dong Zhuo's floor and kneels and calls him father. Now the
tyrant has the capital, the emperor, and the one man nobody can beat. He
sleeps in the emperor's bed. Nobody dares. One officer bows lowest of all
every morning and keeps a jeweled dagger in his sleeve.

**The turn:** the tyrant buys the greatest warrior alive with a horse, for
the price of Lü Bu's own father.
**Page-turn out:** Cao Cao has a dagger.

## IV — THE DAGGER · pp 22–28

**Emotional entry:** suspense. **Exit:** a letter read aloud under the
peach trees, and three men taking down their weapons.

Cao Cao comes to Dong Zhuo's chamber late, says his horse is slow, and Dong
Zhuo sends Lü Bu to choose him a better one from the stable and rolls his
great body over to face the wall. Cao Cao draws the dagger. Dong Zhuo sees
the blade in the bronze mirror and turns, and Lü Bu's step is in the
doorway, and Cao Cao is already on his knees holding the dagger out flat: a
gift for my lord, the finest blade in the city. Dong Zhuo is delighted. May
he try the horse? He rides out of the gate and does not stop, and it is an
hour before anyone wonders why a man kneels with a drawn blade. His face is
on every gate by nightfall. At a farmhouse an old man, his father's sworn
brother, takes him in and rides to the village for good wine. In the night
Cao Cao hears a whetstone on a blade and a voice say "tie it up first, then
kill it," and goes through the dark house with his sword. At dawn there is a
pig tied on the kitchen table, ready to be killed for him. On the road he
meets the old man coming home with the wine, and the old man asks why he is
leaving. Cao Cao says that when the old man gets home he will find what was
done, and tell the world, and that he would rather betray the whole world
than let the world betray him. Then he raises an army and sends a letter to
every lord in the empire: Dong Zhuo holds the emperor; rise. In Zhuo County
Liu Bei reads it aloud under the peach trees, and Zhang Fei says it is the
fat pig they saved.

**The turn:** the man who will call the empire to war is a man who murdered
his father's sworn brother on a road, and only the reader knows.
**Page-turn out:** the brothers are going to war against the man they
saved.

## V — EIGHTEEN LORDS · pp 29–36

**Emotional entry:** pride. **Exit:** Lü Bu on the wall of Hulao Gate,
asking his father for the gate.

Eighteen lords camp before Hulao Gate, the fortress in the pass on the road
to the capital, and behind the road is the boy emperor. The three brothers
arrive with their five hundred and stand at the back of the great tent.
Yuan Shao is chosen leader. He asks who those three are, and Cao Cao says
Liu Bei, of the imperial family, who fought the Yellow Turbans, and Yuan
Shao asks since when the imperial family sells sandals, and the tent laughs.
Cao Cao says he came, give him a seat; Yuan Shao says the end of the bench,
then. Dong Zhuo's champion rides out of the gate and kills the lords'
champions one after another. Yuan Shao says if his own great generals were
here they would not fear one man, and from the back of the tent Guan Yu says
he will bring the champion's helmet to this table. Yuan Shao asks his rank:
a mounted archer. A common soldier, to shame eighteen lords? Throw him out.
Cao Cao says the champion does not know he is a common soldier, and pours a
cup of hot wine, and Guan Yu says pour it and leave it, he will be back
before it is cold. Drums. The tent holds its breath; through the flap, far
off, a green blade turns in the sun. Guan Yu comes back and sets a helmet on
the table and drinks the wine, still warm. Zhang Fei wants the whole gate
now. Yuan Shao has the three nobodies thrown out. That night Cao Cao comes
to their tent with meat and wine, says Yuan Shao counts ancestors and he
counts men who win, and goes; Zhang Fei would follow him anywhere, Guan Yu
does not trust a man who gives in secret what he will not give in the open,
and Liu Bei says he is the only one who stood up for them. The reader knows
what Cao Cao did on the road. On the wall of Hulao Gate, Dong Zhuo hears
that a red-faced common soldier killed his champion, and Lü Bu says: give
me the gate, father, and I will give you all eighteen.

**The turn:** Guan Yu proves the brothers in front of the lords, and the
lords throw them out anyway.
**Page-turn out:** Lü Bu is coming out of the gate.

## VI — LÜ BU · pp 37–44

**Emotional entry:** awe. **Exit:** the gate slamming shut on Lü Bu, and
Zhang Fei hammering on it with his spear.

The gate opens and Lü Bu rides out on Red Hare: among men, Lü Bu; among
horses, Red Hare. The lords' champions go out one at a time and come back
without spears, without horses, or not at all, and the line of eighteen
armies breaks. Lü Bu cuts straight for the leader's banner, and Yuan Shao's
horse stumbles, and the halberd goes up, and a roar stops it: Zhang Fei,
calling Lü Bu a slave of three fathers, the one who made him, the old man who
raised him, and the fat pig who bought him with a horse. Fifty times spear
and halberd come together and neither gives a step. Zhang Fei is tiring and
Lü Bu is laughing, and Guan Yu rides in. Two against one, thirty more times,
and Lü Bu is still laughing. On the hill by the banner Liu Bei draws his two
swords, and Yuan Shao, whom his brother just saved, tells the sandal-seller
to stay back or die. Liu Bei says he and his brothers swore to die on the
same day, and if this is the day he will not spend it on a hill. Three
against one, circling like a turning lantern, and for the first time in his
life Lü Bu cannot see all his enemies at once. He feints at Liu Bei's face,
breaks out, and spurs the red horse for the gate, and the three ride after
him, and the gate shuts in their faces.

**The turn:** the warrior nobody has beaten runs from three men who swore
an oath.
**Page-turn out:** Lü Bu is behind the gate, and he is still alive.

## VII — THE GATE · pp 45–48

**Emotional entry:** breath. **Exit:** three brothers on horseback before a
shut gate at dawn, Lü Bu's plumes on the wall, and Cao Cao on the ridge
behind them.

Zhang Fei hammers the gate and shouts for the three-father slave to come
back out, and Lü Bu appears on the wall above, breathing hard, and asks who
they are. Liu Bei says their three names and the word brothers. Lü Bu says
next time come one at a time. Guan Yu says no. At dusk, by the fire, Cao Cao
rides up alone and pours Liu Bei a cup and asks how many heroes there are in
eighteen armies. Liu Bei says Yuan Shao has the most soldiers. Cao Cao says
Yuan Shao has ancestors; there are two heroes in this camp, Liu Bei: you,
and me. Liu Bei says he is only a sandal-seller, and Cao Cao laughs and rides
away. That night Liu Bei walks out past his sleeping brothers and looks at
the gate in the moonlight: behind it the tyrant and the boy, in front of it
eighteen lords who would not fight, a man who pours wine and might be
anything, and two brothers who rode in after him. In the morning the three
are in front of the gate again.

**The turn:** Cao Cao names Liu Bei his equal, and his rival.
**Page-turn out:** (none: the book ends.) The promise is that the gate is
still shut, and the three of them are in front of it together.

Spectacle runs above the ~13% target on purpose: this is the war epic the
boys voted for, and every spectacle page is an oath, an ambush, a fire, a
capital falling, a gate opening, or a duel the whole book has been building
toward. It relaxes no rule; the target is a balance, not a ceiling. The
script gate should still ask of each spectacle page whether a dramatic page
would do more.

**No movement runs more than five dramatic pages without a prose or spectacle
page.** Longest runs: 17–20 (four); 3–5, 12–14, 25–27, 30–32, 34–36, 44–46
(three). The prose and spectacle pages are what buy the dramatic pages their
room; they cannot be cut for budget.

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
The oath (p. 6) is its own page after the night that decides it (p. 5).
Dong Zhuo's snub (p. 14) is not the rescue (p. 13). Lü Bu's offer (p. 19) is
not his answer (p. 20). The bluff (p. 23) gets its consequence (p. 24) before
the farmhouse. The farmhouse is three pages: the welcome (p. 25), the
mistake (p. 26), and the choice (p. 27), and the mistake and the choice never
share a page. Guan Yu's wager (p. 32), the waiting (p. 33), and the warm cup
(p. 34) are three pages. Each brother entering the fight against Lü Bu is
its own page (pp. 39, 41, 42), and the three together (p. 43) is a fourth.
The gate shutting (p. 44) is not the names on the wall (p. 45), and Cao
Cao's two heroes (p. 46) get their night (p. 47) before the last image.

**One dominant turn per page.** Every row above names the one thing that
changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence comes back here,
not to the prompt.
