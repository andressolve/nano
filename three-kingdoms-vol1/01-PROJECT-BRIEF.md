# Three Kingdoms, Volume I: The Peach Garden Oath — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, 49 pages.
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* (三國演義), attributed to Luo
Guanzhong, 14th century, chapters 1–5 (the Yellow Turbans through the battle
at Hulao Gate), with one line borrowed from chapter 21 and one incident from
chapter 4 reordered. Consulted in the Moss Roberts and C. H. Brewitt-Taylor
translations as evidence of incident, never for wording.

---

## The engine

**Three nobodies who met yesterday swear to die on the same day, and every
great man in a falling empire shuts the door on them, until the door is a
gate held by the greatest warrior alive and the three ride at him together.**

People have told this story for six hundred years because of the oath. A
sandal-seller, a fugitive, and a butcher swear in a peach garden that they
were not born on the same day but will die on the same day, and then they
mean it. Around them the great men of the empire, who were born to rank,
lie, bargain, buy each other with horses, and murder their fathers. The
battles are the noise. The question underneath is the one the boys were
promised: when the old world breaks, who deserves the new one? Both are
protected.

**If the protagonist reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a
stretch, the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.
Liu Bei is never calm. He is twenty-eight, descended from emperors, selling
straw sandals in a market, and every closed door in this book is aimed at
the thing he wants most, which is to be what his blood says he is.

## The spine

The empire is burning, and a sandal-seller sighs at a recruiting notice on a
market wall. A butcher with a voice like thunder asks him why he sighs
instead of doing something, and in a tavern a red-faced giant on the run
bangs the table for wine because he is going to enlist. By morning the three
have sworn brotherhood in a peach garden. They raise five hundred farmers
and beat fifty thousand rebels with an ambush; they meet a sharp little
officer named Cao Cao at a burning field; they save the life of a fat
imperial general, Dong Zhuo, who asks their rank, hears "none," and turns
his back. Then that general marches on the capital, throws the emperor off
his throne, sets a nine-year-old in his place, and buys the greatest warrior
in China, Lü Bu, with a blood-red horse, for the price of Lü Bu's own
adoptive father. Cao Cao tries to kill the tyrant with a jeweled dagger,
bluffs his way out, flees, and on the road commits a crime the brothers
never learn about. Then he calls every lord in the empire to war. The three
go, and stand at the back of the tent. The lords call Liu Bei a
sandal-seller and Guan Yu a common soldier, and Guan Yu goes out and kills
the champion at the gate before Cao Cao's cup of wine has gone cold. Then
the gate opens and Lü Bu rides out on the red horse, and nobody can stand
against him, and the lords run, and Zhang Fei stands in his way, then Guan
Yu, and then Liu Bei, the worst fighter of the three, draws his two swords
and rides in, because he swore to. The three drive Lü Bu back through Hulao
Gate. The gate shuts. The last pages land on a fork the reader can feel: the
three have just done what eighteen lords could not, and the tyrant is still
behind the gate with the boy emperor, and Cao Cao, who poured the wine and
killed the old man on the road, rides up to Liu Bei and says there are only
two heroes in this whole camp: Liu Bei, and himself.

## What this volume is

- A complete story: the oath, the first war, the tyrant, the call, the lords,
  the gate. Lü Bu is not killed and the tyrant is not fallen, but the
  brothers' question, can three nobodies stand against the greatest warrior
  alive, is answered on the page.
- A brotherhood book with a weapon in every movement. The three are the
  spectacle, and the climax is the oath paying off in combat: each brother
  rides in because the others are there.
- A strategy book. Liu Bei's ambush, Cao Cao's fire, Cao Cao's bluff with the
  dagger, Dong Zhuo's purchase of Lü Bu, Guan Yu's wager on a cup of wine.
  Every set piece is somebody being cleverer, braver, or worse than expected.
- A book with two villains of different kinds and one man in between. Dong
  Zhuo wants everything for himself; Lü Bu wants only to be the best and be
  paid for it; Cao Cao wants to save the empire and will do anything,
  anything, to get there. The reader is never told which he is.
- Funny. Zhang Fei is a running thunderstorm. Guan Yu is a wall of dignity
  that a cup of wine can crack. Dong Zhuo eats in every scene.

## What this volume is not

- Not the chronicle. No battle reported in a sentence, no lord list, no
  champion with a name who dies a panel later.
- Not a court drama. No eunuchs, no regent, no palace coup explained. The
  capital falls in one spectacle page and the reader sees a fat man lift a
  boy onto a throne.
- Not a history lesson. No dates, no dynasties beyond "the Han emperors have
  ruled for four hundred years," no map.
- Not Diaochan's book, not Zhao Yun's, not Zhuge Liang's. None appears or is
  foreshadowed. The book is three brothers, their enemies, and Cao Cao.
- Not a gore book. See `00-VOLUME-MANDATE.md`: deaths are real and never
  drawn.

**Collapses.** Yuan Shu's sneers at the coalition (the "mere archer," the
"throw out these nobodies") **merge into Yuan Shao**, so the lords have one
face and the reader resents one man. Gongsun Zan's place in the climax
(the lord Lü Bu runs down, whom Zhang Fei saves) **merges into Yuan Shao**
as well: the man who called them nobodies is the man they save. Chen Gong's
whole function (the witness to the farmhouse) **is cut**: the reader is the
only witness, and Cao Cao says his famous line to the old man himself.
Huangfu Song's fire attack **becomes Cao Cao's**. Li Su's errand with the
red horse **becomes Dong Zhuo's own**: the tyrant walks into the stable
himself. The Sishui Pass and Hulao Gate **become one gate**, so the champion
and Lü Bu defend the same wall.

**Scenery, never named on the page:** the boy emperor (a small figure on a
great throne, no balloon), the deposed emperor, Ding Yuan (the old lord
behind whom Lü Bu stands, called only "the old man who raised him"), the
champion at the gate (Hua Xiong in the source; here "Dong Zhuo's champion"),
the sorcerer who leads the Yellow Turbans, the rebel captains, the smith,
the lords of the coalition other than Yuan Shao, every messenger.

**Source names in chapters 1–5:** well over a hundred. **Locked here: eight.**

## The audience promise

**What these readers already enjoy.** Underdogs who are better than the
people above them. Three heroes who are each the best at something. A
monster of a warrior on a monster of a horse. A tyrant who eats. A clever
man who might be the villain. Ambushes, fire, a dagger in a sleeve, a cup of
wine as a dare. Being told someone cannot be beaten and watching three
people try.

**The repeatable pleasures.** Zhang Fei's voice stopping things. Guan Yu
stroking his beard before he says something nobody will forget. The great
men saying "who are you?" and the brothers answering with what they do.
Cao Cao turning up where nobody expects him, always one step ahead. Dong
Zhuo with food in his hand. The oath's words coming back. Liu Bei's straw
sandals thrown in his face as an insult, and Liu Bei never once denying
them.

**The emotional promise.** The reader knows what it is to be told you are
too small, too poor, too young to sit at the table. The brothers never win
the argument; they win the fight, and the oath is the reason each of them
rides in. And the reader gets a man they cannot make up their mind about:
the only lord who treats the brothers as equals is also the only one the
reader has watched kill an innocent old man.

**The central dramatic question.** *Can three nobodies who swore to die on
the same day stand against the warrior nobody has beaten?* The book's answer
is yes, together, and only together. Underneath, unanswered: *who deserves
the empire, the man with the blood, the man with the rank, or the man who
will do anything?*

**Complexity budget.** Eight names, one horse (Red Hare), one weapon name
(the Green Dragon blade). Three places that matter: Zhuo County (the peach
garden), the capital, Hulao Gate. Two sides in the first half (the empire
and the Yellow Turbans) and two in the second (the tyrant and the lords).
One time jump, marked in narration. Zero assumed knowledge: nobody needs to
know what the Han was, what a prime minister is, or where Luoyang lies.

**What this project refuses to become.** A chronicle. A roll call of
generals. A book where the three brothers sound alike. A book in which Liu
Bei is a saint. A book that tells the reader what to think of Cao Cao.

## Length

Working count **49** (48 at greenlight; the script gate added Liu Bei's decision in the peach garden, p 29), reached by cutting: five source chapters and over a
hundred names reduced to seven movements and eight names; the eunuchs, He
Jin, the inspector, Gongsun Zan, Zhao Yun, Chen Gong, Lu Zhi, and every named
champion cut to nobody or to scenery. The boys were promised about 48. The
count follows the script and will not shrink by compression. If the script
gate grows it past the low fifties, the split point is after Movement IV
(the call to arms), and the owner decides.

## The reader

A first-time reader who will never read the source. They must never have to
solve the adaptation, supply a missing causal step, or recognize a character
the book has not introduced. "Recoverable," "eventually clear," and
"understandable after looking back" are revision flags, not passing grades.
