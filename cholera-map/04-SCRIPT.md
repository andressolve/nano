# 04 — SCRIPT

All text below is VERBATIM render text unless marked (note). Every page lists: layout,
refs to pass via `imagePaths`, per-panel beats, and the full LETTERING block. Clue boxes
are cream cards with a gold `CLUE:` tab. No quotation marks inside speech bubbles.
Density notes assume the narrative-mode generous-text rule.

Recurring glosses budget (first use): miasma (P4), Vestry / Board of Guardians (P12),
cesspool (P16), epidemiology (P19, glossed as "the study of how disease spreads through
a population").

Verbatim historical quotes are marked **(VERBATIM, see RESEARCH.md)**. All other dialogue
is reasonable dramatization in period voice, consistent with shelf practice on Vol 1.

## P1 — THE FEAR

Layout: THREE panels — two equal top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: none (no locked cast yet).
Beats: (1) An ordinary Soho evening, late August 1854: crowded, noisy streets, laundries
and shopfronts, families going about their evening. (2) Close on an unremarkable public
water pump at a street corner — a woman filling a jug, children nearby, nothing
sinister about it. (3) WIDE: the same streets at dusk, gas lamps not yet lit, a
gathering fog — mood shifting from lively to uneasy without anything visibly wrong yet.

LETTERING:
- Caption, top left (p1): London, late August 1854. Soho is loud, crowded, and full of
  life — laundries, tailors' shops, pubs, families packed six to a room.
- Caption (p1, lower): Everyone here knows cholera. It has come before, in 1832 and
  1849, and killed by the thousands. But no one — not even England's finest doctors —
  agrees on how it actually spreads.
- Caption (p2): At the corner of Broad Street stands an ordinary public pump. Hundreds
  fill their jugs here every day. Some say it tastes better than any water in Soho.
- Case card, bottom right, gold-edged: This is a true detective story. The victim: an
  entire neighbourhood. The weapon: something no one can see, hear, or smell coming.

Density: T4. Tier note: caption-led hook page, dread without spectacle.

## P2 — THE FIRST NIGHT

Layout: FOUR panels — two top, two bottom. Refs: none.
Beats: (1) Night, a single lit window, a family's shape moving inside — worry, not
illness shown. (2) A neighbour knocking urgently on a door across the street. (3) A
harried local doctor hurrying down the street with his bag, dawn light. (4) A parish
clerk's hand, writing names into a register by candlelight, the list already long.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): On the night of the 31st of August, 1854, the first deaths came —
  suddenly, and close together.
- Caption (p2): By morning there were more. Then more again. Neighbours who had shared
  supper the night before did not live to see the next one.
- Caption (p3): Local doctors were overwhelmed within a day. Nothing in their training
  had prepared them for a disease this fast.
- Caption (p4): A parish clerk kept the register of the dead. By the second day, his
  hand ached from writing names.

Density: T4.

## P3 — TEN DAYS

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: none.
Beats: (1) A family loading a handcart with belongings, fleeing. (2) A row of shuttered
shopfronts, a single black ribbon tied to one door-knocker — restrained, no bodies. (3)
WIDE: a near-empty street at dusk, fog rolling in, the pump still standing at the
corner, ordinary and unquestioned.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Word spread faster than any doctor could explain the disease. Families
  who could afford to leave, left.
- Caption (p2): In ten days, five hundred people died within a few streets of Broad
  Street — one of the fastest-moving outbreaks Britain had ever recorded.
- Case card, bottom right, gold-edged: Five hundred deaths. No agreed cause. And the one
  object every victim's household shared in common was standing in plain sight the
  whole time — nobody had thought to suspect it.

Density: T4.

## P4 — THE ESTABLISHED THEORY

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: none (unnamed period figures).
Beats: (1) A lecture hall or learned society meeting, respectable gentlemen nodding at a
speaker gesturing toward a chart of foul air rising from slums and sewers. (2) Close on
the chart itself: arrows showing "bad air" drifting from low, damp ground upward into
healthy neighbourhoods. (3) WIDE: London's skyline at dusk, chimney smoke and river fog
mingling into one grey haze over the whole city — visually inseparable, which is exactly
the point.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): For as long as anyone could remember, the answer to "how does disease
  spread" had been the same: bad air. Doctors called it miasma — poisonous vapours
  rising from filth, sewage, and rot.
- Caption (p2): It was not a foolish idea. Slums WERE filthy, and slums WERE where
  cholera struck hardest. Miasma theory fit the facts everyone could already see.
- Caption (p3, wide): And in a city like London — coal smoke, river stench, and open
  sewers blurring into one grey fog — how could anyone tell bad air from clean?
- Case card, bottom right, gold-edged: A detective's hardest rival is not a liar. It is
  a serious, respected theory that happens to be wrong.

Density: T4.

## P5 — THE RIVAL DETECTIVE

Layout: FOUR panels — two top, two bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_farr.
Beats: (1) FARR at his government office desk, tables of mortality figures stacked
around him, quill in hand, methodical. (2) Close on one of his tables: parish names on
one column, deaths in another, elevation-above-the-Thames in a third — the pattern
clearly showing more deaths at lower elevation. (3) Farr addressing colleagues, pointing
to the table, confident. (4) A small inset: an earlier report of Farr's, open to a
paragraph, one line underlined.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): William Farr was no fool either. A statistician at the government's
  General Register Office, he tracked every death in England by the numbers.
- Caption (p2): Farr found something real: cholera deaths were consistently worse in
  low-lying areas near the Thames. He called it his "elevation law" — and took it as
  proof that bad air pooled, like water, in the lowest ground.
- Bubble (Farr, panel 3): The numbers are plain. The lower the ground, the higher the
  deaths. What more evidence does a theory need?
- CLUE box (panel 4): CLUE: Years earlier, reviewing a rival doctor's pamphlet on
  cholera, Farr himself had written that it was **(VERBATIM)** "in many respects the
  most important theory that has been propounded" — and still rejected it.
- Caption, small, under clue: Even Farr admitted the water theory was serious. He just
  didn't believe it yet.

Density: T5.

## P6 — ENTER THE DOCTOR

Layout: FOUR panels — two top, two bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_snow.
Beats: (1) SNOW in his study, a worn 1849 pamphlet on the desk beside him, largely
ignored by the look of its undisturbed dust. (2) Close: Snow's steady hands administering
chloroform at a birth attended by finely dressed attendants — establishing his real-world
credibility without naming the patient in-panel. (3) Snow at his own table, drinking from
a plain glass of water he clearly trusts, contrasted with the crowded city outside his
window. (4) Snow standing, pulling on his coat, notebook in hand, resolve on his face.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Dr. John Snow had already published his answer — five years before Broad
  Street. In 1849 he argued that cholera was not carried by air at all, but swallowed,
  in water or food fouled by the waste of the sick. Almost no one believed him.
- Caption (p2): Snow was already one of England's most trusted physicians — trusted
  enough to be called to administer chloroform at the births of the Queen's own
  children.
- Caption (p3): He was famously careful about what he ate and drank, and about what he
  claimed as fact. He did not chase theories. He chased evidence.
- Bubble (Snow, panel 4): If I am right, the proof is still walking those streets. I
  only have to go and ask it questions.
- Caption (p4): He lived in Soho himself, a short walk from Broad Street. When the
  outbreak began, he did not wait for an invitation.

Density: T5.

## P7 — THE ONE QUESTION

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_snow.
Beats: (1) Snow at a doorway, notebook out, speaking with a grieving household member —
respectful distance, no bodies shown. (2) Close on his notebook page: a scrawled list of
addresses, each with one word circled — "PUMP?" (3) WIDE: Snow walking the length of
Broad Street at dusk, the pump visible ahead of him, houses on both sides, his path
tracing the same streets house by house.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Snow obtained the Registrar-General's list of the dead and went, in
  person, to as many of their households as he could — asking one question above every
  other.
- Bubble (Snow, panel 1): Forgive the intrusion. I only need to know — where did your
  household draw its drinking water?
- Caption (p2): Not what they had eaten. Not how clean their rooms were. Only the water.
- CLUE box (panel 3): CLUE: Almost every answer was the same word: the pump at Broad
  Street.
- Caption, bottom, small: One question, asked at door after door, was about to become a
  map.

Density: T5.

## P8 — THE MAP TAKES SHAPE

Layout: CASE-FILE SPLASH (not a panel grid) — the exhibit fills most of the page, Snow
small at one corner, pen in hand, adding another bar. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_snow,
plate_map_full.
Scene: THE MAP pinned to a board, streets inked in, small black bars accumulating house
by house, three pump symbols marked. The cluster around the Broad Street pump is already
visibly the tallest, though the page shows it still being built — a bar being added in
the foreground as we watch.

LETTERING:
- Caption, top: Every death, a small black bar on the street where it happened. Every
  public pump, marked with its own symbol.
- Caption, lower left: At first it looked like chaos — dots and bars scattered across
  a dozen streets.
- CLUE box, bottom right: CLUE: But bar by bar, night after night, one shape kept
  repeating. The bars were not spreading evenly. They were piling up — and they were
  piling up around ONE pump.
- Caption, small, bottom: Snow was not guessing anymore. He was counting.

Density: T5. Case-file splash — evidence page, no panel borders needed.

## P9 — THE FIRST TEST

Layout: FOUR panels — two top, two bottom. Refs: none (incidental, unnamed workers).
Beats: (1) The brewery yard: workers filling tankards from their own well, sacks and
barrels around them, unbothered. (2) Close: a brewery ledger or foreman's face, calm,
untouched by the surrounding panic. (3) The workhouse courtyard: inmates at a separate
well, orderly, distant. (4) Snow, notebook in hand, standing at the brewery gate,
making a note, a small satisfied nod.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): If water was the cause, Snow's theory made a prediction: anyone who
  didn't drink from the Broad Street pump should be safe — even living right in the
  middle of the outbreak.
- Caption (p2): The workers at the Broad Street brewery had their own well, and were
  given a daily allowance of beer instead of water. Almost none of them fell ill.
- Caption (p3): The inmates of the nearby Poland Street workhouse drew water from their
  own separate well too. They were spared as well.
- CLUE box (panel 4): CLUE: Two groups, living inside the worst-hit streets in London —
  both nearly untouched. Not because of cleaner air. Because of a different well.
- Bubble (Snow, panel 4): If it were the air, these men would be dying too. It is not
  the air.

Density: T5.

## P10 — THE SECOND TEST

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: none (incidental elderly figure).
Beats: (1) A modest cottage doorway in Hampstead, miles from Soho — greener, quieter,
brighter than the fog-bound city streets. (2) A servant loading a corked stone bottle
onto a small handcart, a Soho street sign visible in the background of the journey. (3)
WIDE: the same cottage, now quiet, curtains drawn — handled with restraint, no illness
shown, only stillness.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Snow's theory made a second prediction, in the opposite direction: if
  someone far from Soho drank the SAME water, they should fall ill too — even with
  clean air all around them.
- Caption (p2): A widow named Susannah Eley had moved away to Hampstead, miles from
  Soho — but she loved the taste of the Broad Street well water so much that she had a
  servant fetch her a bottle of it, by cart, every single day.
- CLUE box (panel 3): CLUE: Her last bottle of Broad Street water was fetched on the 31st
  of August — the very first day of the outbreak. She died of cholera on the 2nd of
  September. Her niece, who shared the water, fell ill too.
- Caption, bottom, small: No shared air with Soho. No shared street. Only one bottle of
  water, carried three miles by cart.

Density: T5.

## P12 — THE CONFRONTATION

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_snow,
plate_map_full.
Beats: (1) A formal Vestry board room: half a dozen gentlemen in dark coats seated at a
long table, tall windows behind. (2) Snow standing at one end, the map propped on an
easel beside him, mid-argument. (3) WIDE: the Guardians' faces — skeptical, weighing it,
one leaning to whisper to another.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): On the evening of the 7th of September, 1854, Snow brought his map
  before the St James's Parish Vestry — the Board of Guardians responsible for the
  parish's health.
- Bubble (Snow, panel 2): Every case I have traced leads back to this one pump. I ask
  you to disable it — today, if you can.
- Caption (panel 3): He was not a Guardian. He held no office over them. He had only a
  map, a notebook full of doorstep answers, and his own reputation.
- Caption, bottom, small: It should not have been enough. It was.

Density: T4.

## P13 — THE PUMP HANDLE

Layout: THREE panels — one WIDE top, two bottom. Refs: none (the pump itself, unnamed
workman).
Beats: (1) WIDE: morning, the 8th of September — a workman with a wrench removing the
iron handle from the Broad Street pump, a small watching crowd. (2) Close: the bare
pump spout, handle gone, no water to be drawn. (3) The same street, days later, quieter
— not empty of people now, but no longer panicked.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): The next morning, the 8th of September, 1854, the handle was removed
  from the Broad Street pump. No one could draw water from it again.
- Caption (p2): It became the single image everyone remembered — the moment the case
  seemed closed.
- Case card, bottom right, gold-edged: A famous gesture. But a good detective does not
  let a famous gesture stand in for proof — and neither did Snow.

Density: T3.

## P14 — THE HONEST DOUBT

Layout: TWO panels — split evenly left/right. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_snow.
Beats: (1) Snow alone at his desk that same week, reviewing his own numbers by lamplight,
expression thoughtful rather than triumphant. (2) A simple chart of daily death counts,
already falling in the days BEFORE the handle came off, then falling further after.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Snow himself did not claim the pump handle had ended the outbreak alone.
  He wrote it down plainly, in his own report.
- CLUE box (panel 2): CLUE **(VERBATIM)**: "The number of attacks of cholera had been
  diminished before this measure was adopted, but whether they had diminished in a
  greater proportion than might be accounted for by the flight of the great bulk of the
  population I am unable to say."
- Caption, bottom: Most residents had already fled. The outbreak was already dying down
  on its own. A famous gesture is not the same thing as a finished case.
- Caption, small: The pump handle was the headline. It was not yet the proof.

Density: T4. Tier note: the shelf's honesty rule applied to its own hero moment.

## P15 — THE SECOND DETECTIVE

Layout: FOUR panels — two top, two bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_whitehead.
Beats: (1) WHITEHEAD in his own modest curate's room, Snow's published theory (a
pamphlet or newspaper account) open before him, doubtful expression. (2) Whitehead
walking the same Soho streets, welcomed at a doorway where Snow, a stranger to most of
these families, had not been. (3) Whitehead making his own careful notes, door to door.
(4) Close on his notebook — his OWN tally, independent of Snow's.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Not everyone was convinced — including a young curate of the parish
  named Reverend Henry Whitehead, who knew these streets and these families far better
  than any visiting physician could.
- Bubble (Whitehead, panel 1, thinking): If this theory is wrong, the people here
  deserve to know it is wrong.
- Caption (p2): Whitehead set out to check Snow's claim himself — going door to door
  through the same streets, meaning to find the holes in the doctor's map.
- Caption (p3): He was welcomed everywhere. His parishioners trusted him instantly, in a
  way they had not trusted a stranger with a notebook.
- CLUE box (panel 4): CLUE: Whitehead's own count, gathered independently, matched
  Snow's pattern — and then went further, toward a question Snow's map could not answer:
  not just WHERE the water was fouled, but HOW.

Density: T5.

## P16 — THE MECHANISM

Layout: CASE-FILE SPLASH with one figure. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_whitehead,
plate_cesspool_diagram.
Scene: Whitehead crouched at the front area of a Broad Street house, examining old
brickwork beside the well shaft. A cream cross-section diagram is pinned into the panel
alongside him, showing the well and, only three feet away, an old brick cesspool with a
crack in its wall. A closed door and a single black ribbon on its knocker sit quietly in
the background — the only reference to the house's grief, never shown directly.

LETTERING:
- Caption, top: At **40 Broad Street** — directly across from the pump — Whitehead found
  what Snow's map alone could never show him.
- CLUE box: CLUE: The well had been dug only three feet from an old brick cesspool — a
  covered pit for household waste. Its brickwork had decayed. It was leaking.
- Caption, lower: In that house, weeks earlier, an infant girl had fallen gravely ill.
  Her mother, washing the soiled linens, had emptied the rinse water into that same
  failing cesspool — a few feet from the well that hundreds of households drank from
  every day.
- Caption, small, bottom: Snow had proven WHERE the poison entered the water. Whitehead
  had just found HOW it got there in the first place.

Density: T5. Dignified-death discipline: no infant shown, no illness depicted — the
black ribbon and the closed door carry the weight instead.

## P17 — THE NUMBER-PROOF

Layout: CASE-FILE SPLASH. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_snow, plate_grand_experiment_table.
Scene: Snow at a broad table piled with parish ledgers, assistants in the background
cross-checking addresses against a large map of south London. Pinned beside him, a
simple cream exhibit table with two columns.

LETTERING:
- Caption, top: While Broad Street was still fresh, Snow had already been building a
  second, larger case — across the whole south of the city.
- Caption: Two water companies supplied the same streets, sometimes the very same
  block — whichever company's old pipe happened to run underneath, decided by contracts,
  not by anything about the household itself. A city had accidentally run its own
  experiment.
- CLUE box (the exhibit table itself): CLUE — deaths per 10,000 houses: Southwark &
  Vauxhall Company (drew water straight from the sewage-fouled Thames) — 315. Lambeth
  Company (had moved its intake upstream, above the sewage) — 37.
- Caption, bottom: Same streets. Same people. Only the water source differed — and the
  death rate differed nearly eightfold. Anyone could check this arithmetic themselves.

Density: T5. Tier note: "the reader can check the math" cross-check page, per shelf
recipe.

## P18 — OFFICIALDOM SAYS NO

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_farr.
Beats: (1) A formal government chamber, the General Board of Health's Committee for
Scientific Inquiries in session, ledgers and charts on miasma still dominating the
table. (2) Close on an official printed report, its conclusion favouring bad air. (3)
WIDE, quiet: Snow, alone, older now, at his study window — years passing implied by
lighting alone, not by depicted illness.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): In 1855 the government's own Committee for Scientific Inquiries reviewed
  the year's evidence — with William Farr himself among its members.
- Caption (p2): Its official report still favoured bad air as the true cause of
  cholera. Snow's water theory was set aside.
- Caption (p3, wide): John Snow died on the 16th of June, 1858 — a highly respected
  physician to the end, but without ever seeing his water theory officially accepted.
- Case card, bottom right, gold-edged: The map was correct. The city said no anyway.
  Being right is not always the same as being believed.

Density: T4.

## P19 — THE WORLD CHANGES

Layout: FOUR-ZONE MONTAGE (closing-as-invention page, per shelf recipe). Refs:
ref_cast_plate, ref_farr, plate_map_solved.
Zones: (1) top-left — London's Thames embankment under construction, Bazalgette's new
sewer tunnels being laid, 1858–1865. (2) top-right — Farr, older now, at his own desk,
tracing a fresh outbreak on a map back to a water company reservoir, 1866 — the former
rival now using Snow's own method. (3) bottom-left — a small sunlit inset: a distant
figure at a brass microscope in Florence, 1854, utterly unconnected to London, unaware
that an ocean away a doctor was reaching the same truth by a different road. (4)
bottom-right — the present day: Broadwick Street, a red kerbstone and a small memorial
pump on a modern corner, a period-signed pub in the background. Small figures of Snow,
Whitehead, and Farr appear faintly, translucently, at the center of the composition,
watching all four zones — the volume's closing device.

LETTERING:
- Caption, top-left: London finally rebuilt its sewers, separating waste from drinking
  water for good. Cholera never again struck the city as it had in 1854.
- Caption, top-right: In 1866, a new outbreak struck East London. This time it was
  William Farr — the very statistician who had championed bad air — who traced it,
  using Snow's own methods, straight to a fouled reservoir.
- Caption, bottom-left: Unknown to anyone in London, an Italian scientist named Filippo
  Pacini had, in that same year of 1854, looked at cholera under a microscope in
  Florence and actually seen the living cause — and been ignored for thirty years, just
  as Snow was. In 1965, the organism was formally named in his honour: Vibrio cholerae
  Pacini 1854.
- Caption, bottom-right: The pump is gone, but its corner is still marked. The habit of
  mapping disease, house by house, death by death, until the pattern speaks for itself —
  that began here, in a very human study of one bad map.
- Case card, center, gold-edged, largest text on the page: Snow drew the map. Whitehead
  found the leak. Farr, once the sharpest rival, carried the method forward. None of
  them alone solved it. Together, they had already changed how the whole world would
  ever answer the question: what is making people sick, and how do we find out?

Density: T6 (explicitly over the normal T5 ceiling — the shelf's proven closing-montage
exception, same allowance Rosetta's finale and Honda's P24 used). Prototype this page
early; split into two pages if it fights the model.
