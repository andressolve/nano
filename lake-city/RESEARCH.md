# RESEARCH — Tenochtitlán & Tlaxcala, 1519

Ground truth for this volume. **Every date, name, protocol, quote and number in `04-SCRIPT.md` is checked against this file.** If the script and this file disagree, this file wins. If something isn't in here, don't assert it.

Source tiers: **P** = primary (Cortés's *Second Letter*, MacNutt trans. 1908; Bernal Díaz, Maudslay trans., vols 1–2). **S** = scholarly. **W** = Wikipedia floor.

---

## ⚠️ THE THREE THINGS THAT CHANGED

1. **POV CONFIRMED.** Tlaxcalans *were* inside Tenochtitlán. Bernal Díaz: the Tlaxcalan lords offered **ten thousand** men; Cortés took **one thousand**, explicitly "to carry the tepusques and the baggage, and to clear some of the roads." They were quartered with the Spanish, and Bernal Díaz records **two Tlaxcalans warning the interpreter Aguilar, from inside the city, that the Mexica seemed badly disposed.** Our protagonist's job description and his function as a listener are both in the primary source. (P, vol 2 ch. LXXXV)
2. **THE TLAXCALAN DEBATE IS A FATHER–SON SPLIT, not what I assumed.** **Maxixcatzin AND Xicotencatl the Elder both argued FOR** the alliance. **Xicotencatl the Younger alone** refused — massed 20,000 men for a night attack in defiance of his own elders, who sent four sets of messengers ordering him to stand down and told the other captains to disobey him. He said he "was not for peace, for he had already killed many of the Teules and a mare." (P, vol 1 chs. LXVII–LXX)
3. **THE EMOTIONAL ENGINE HAD TO BE RE-FOOTED.** See §2 — the "captive farm" story is one hostile source; the **salt-and-cotton blockade is primary-source solid and comes out of a Tlaxcalan lord's own mouth.**

## 1. Tlaxcala, autumn 1519

- Four altepetl: **Tepeticpac, Ocotelolco, Tizatlan, Quiahuiztlan.** Ocotelolco held the main market; Tizatlan held military command.
- **Maxixcatzin** — lord of Ocotelolco ("Mase Escasi" in Bernal Díaz). **FOR the alliance.**
- **Xicotencatl the Elder** — lord of Tizatlan, blind with age. **FOR the alliance.**
- **Xicotencatl the Younger (Axayacatzin)** — his son, captain-general of the Tlaxcalan armies. **AGAINST, alone, and loudly.**
- Sequence: skirmish 31 Aug → battles 2 and 5 Sept → the Younger comes in person to sue for peace → **Spanish enter Tlaxcala city 23 Sept 1519.** Alliance sealed by the lords giving their daughters to Cortés's captains.
- **Usable nuance:** the same two elders who wanted the alliance **opposed marching on Mexico** and specifically warned against Cholula. Pro-alliance ≠ pro-adventure.

## 2. The blockade vs. the "captive farm"

**Two separate claims. Do not merge them.**

- ❌ **"Tlaxcala was deliberately left unconquered as a supply of sacrificial captives" — DISPUTED, single hostile source.** It rests on **Andrés de Tapia** alone, reporting Moctezuma saying they could conquer Tlaxcala easily "but then there would be nowhere for the young men to exercise themselves… and besides we always like to have people to sacrifice to our Gods." The Tlaxcalan historian **Muñoz Camargo denies it. Hicks (1979)** accepts Camargo. **Isaac (1983)** shows 1503–1517 was genuinely bloody with high casualties, incompatible with a ritual arrangement, and concludes the Triple Alliance *wanted* to conquer Tlaxcala and couldn't. **Hassig (1988)** treats flower wars as the opening phase of a real war of conquest and calls the mutual-arrangement reading dubious. Consensus direction: **Mexica face-saving propaganda.**
  → **Script rule: this line may appear ONLY in a Mexica mouth, as a boast.** Never as narration, never as fact.
- ✅ **The economic encirclement — WELL ATTESTED, and from a Tlaxcalan.** Cortés puts it in **Xicotencatl's own mouth**: they were "surrounded on all sides, so that no one could go out of the country… **they ate no salt**, since there was none in their country, nor were they allowed to go to buy it anywhere else, **nor did they wear any cotton clothing**… They had endured it, and held it as better thus to be free." Bernal Díaz corroborates: Cortés suggests peace so they could obtain "**salt and cotton**"; Xicotencatl answers that peace is useless, "**enmity was deeply rooted in their hearts**." Hassig's term: **encirclement.** Bernal Díaz also has the Tlaxcalans date the wars at "**more than a hundred years**."
  → **This is the hatred. Salt, cotton, a hundred years of annual war.**

**Numbers trap.** Cortés's "~100,000 Tlaxcalans, of whom 5,000–6,000 remained" is about **CHOLULA, not Tenochtitlán.** For the march to the city use Bernal Díaz: **10,000 offered, 1,000 accepted.**

## 4. Tlaxcalans inside the city — CONFIRMED

- **1,000 Tlaxcalans entered**, framed as **porters (tamemes), cannon-haulers and road-clearers** — not a war party. (P)
- The **Totonacs/Cempoalans turned back at Cholula.** The Tlaxcalan thousand did not.
- Spanish quartered in the **palace of Axayacatl** (Moctezuma's father) — great halls and chambers, matting beds with canopies, containing Axayacatl's sealed treasure, adjacent to Moctezuma's own residence, entered through a great court.
- Bernal Díaz doesn't itemise where the Tlaxcalans slept in Tenochtitlán, but records the Cholula arrangement — lodged "in some large rooms where we were all together with our friends from Cempoala and **the Tlaxcalans who carried the baggage**" — and, decisively, **inside Tenochtitlán: "two of our Allies the Tlaxcalan Indians said secretly to Jerónimo de Aguilar, our interpreter, that the Mexicans had not appeared to be well disposed towards us during the last two days."**
- **Safe version to write:** ~1,000 Tlaxcalans quartered with the Spanish in the Axayacatl palace complex, in the courts and outer rooms. Don't specify exact rooms.
- 🔑 **Documented irony to use:** at **Cholula** the Tlaxcalans were **refused entry** — the Cholulan priests protested that armed enemies should not enter their city, and Cortés made them camp "in the fields," excepting only the men hauling the cannon. Excluded at Cholula, admitted at Tenochtitlán.

## 5. The causeway meeting, 8 November 1519

**Both eyewitnesses agree on:**
- Moctezuma came in a **litter under a canopy of green feathers** with gold, silver, pearls, chalchihuites; he dismounted from it; he was **supported under the arms** by great lords.
- **He alone was shod.** All other lords barefoot (Cortés). Bernal Díaz: sandals *(cotaras)* with **soles of gold**, uppers set with stones.
- **Cortés dismounted and was physically prevented from embracing him** — the attending lords "held back Cortés by the arm… for they considered it an indignity" (Bernal Díaz); "the two lords in attendance prevented me, with their hands, that I might not touch him" (Cortés).
- **Cortés's gift: a glass-bead necklace** — "made of glass stones, which are called *Margaritas*… strung on a cord of gold and with musk so that it should have a sweet scent."
- **The reciprocal gift: shell-and-gold** — Cortés: "two collars, wrapped in a cloth, which were made of **coloured shells**… and from each of the collars hung **eight golden shrimps** executed with great perfection and a span long." Bernal Díaz calls them **golden crabs**. (Same object; translators differ. **Script uses "shrimp."**)
- **Ground protocol:** lords went before him "**sweeping the ground where he would tread and spreading cloths on it, so that he should not tread on the earth**," and "**not one of these chieftains dared even to think of looking him in the face**, but kept their eyes lowered."
- **Removable bridges:** "a wooden bridge, ten paces broad… **they remove and replace the long broad wooden beams** whenever they wish."

**Where they differ — flagged, write around:**
- **Who flanked him.** Cortés: two — Cacamatzin of Texcoco, and Moctezuma's brother the lord of Iztapalapa (Cuitláhuac). Bernal Díaz: four nephews supported his arms and names Texcoco, Iztapalapa, Tacuba, Coyoacán — and treats "lord of Iztapalapa" and "Lord of Cuitlahuac" as **two different men** (Cuitláhuac is both a person and a lake town). **Safe staging: Cacamatzin of Texcoco on one side, Cuitláhuac lord of Iztapalapa on the other, two more lords bearing the canopy.** Satisfies both without asserting the contradiction.
- **The handshake** (Cortés offering his right hand, Moctezuma declining to take it but giving his own) — Bernal Díaz only.
- **When the shell necklace was given** — Cortés says on the street; Bernal Díaz says at the lodging. **Pick one. Script uses the street.**
- Moctezuma's parting line at the palace: "**Malinche, you and your brethren are in your own house, rest awhile.**" (Bernal Díaz)

## 6. The city — scene-building (Cortés's Second Letter unless marked)

**Tlatelolco market.** A square "**twice as large as that of Salamanca**," arcaded, "**more than sixty thousand souls**" daily. **Each commodity in its own street**, strictly enforced. Sold: gold, silver, lead, brass, copper, zinc, stone, bones, shells, feathers; hewn and unhewn stone, adobe, timber; a **game street** — "chickens, partridges, quails, wild ducks, turtle-doves, pigeons, parrots, owls, eaglets, falcons, sparrow-hawks and kestrels" — and **the skins of birds of prey with feathers, heads, beaks and claws**; rabbits, hares, **small edible dogs**; a **herb street** and "houses like apothecary shops"; **barbers**; cookshops; **porters for hire**; charcoal, braziers, sleeping mats; onions, leeks, garlic, cresses, thistles; **maguey honey, sugar and pulque**; skeins of dyed cotton "**quite like one of the silk markets of Granada**"; painters' pigments; tanned deerskins; glazed painted pottery; maize and maize bread; fish fresh, salted, cooked, raw; egg cakes. **Everything sold by measure, nothing by weight.** A **market court with ten or twelve sitting judges** plus inspectors who have been seen to **break false measures**. Bernal Díaz adds **slaves brought in tied to long poles with collars around their necks.**

**Sacred precinct.** Walled; "a village of five hundred houses could easily be built" inside; ~**40 towers**, the largest with 50 steps and "**higher than the tower of the chief church in Seville**"; inner chapels lightless and priest-only, "**full of blood from the sacrifices**." Priests in **black, hair never cut or combed**, matted and "clotted with blood"; noble sons entered from age 7–8. **Tzompantli** (Bernal Díaz): "**full of skulls and large bones arranged in perfect order, which one could look at but could not count** — the skulls were by themselves and the bones in separate piles." **Twin shrines to Huitzilopochtli (south) and Tláloc (north)** — standard archaeology (S/W), not explicit in these two texts.

**Aqueduct.** "**Two conduits of masonry each two paces broad and five feet deep**," one carrying fresh water "the bulk of a man's body" from Chapultepec **while the other lies empty so the first can be cleaned.** Pipes carry water over the causeway breaks. **Canoes peddle water through the streets.**

**Moctezuma's bird and animal houses.** **Ten pools** — salt water for sea-birds, fresh for lake birds, renewed by pipes; fish-eating birds fed **250 lb of fish daily**; **300 men employed on the birds alone**, plus bird-doctors; galleries above the pools for Moctezuma to watch. A court paved like a chessboard with **individual latticed cages for birds of prey, "from the kestrel to the eagle,"** each with a night perch and a sun perch. Heavy-timbered **cages of jaguars, foxes and every kind of cat** — **another 300 men** for these. Also apartments of albinos and of dwarfs. (INAH 2026 zooarchaeology supports the menagerie as real.)

**Court etiquette.** "**At dawn every day, six hundred lords and men of rank came to his palace**," waiting in halls and corridors without entering his room, retainers filling two or three great courts and the street, remaining till night. Larder and cellar open to all.

**City form.** Four causeways "two cavalry lances in width"; streets half land, half water; bridges "over which ten horsemen can ride abreast." Cortés: the city is "**as large as Seville or Cordoba**." **Chinampas** ~6–10 m wide × 100–200 m long (S/W).

**Population — RANGE ONLY.** Modern estimates **200,000–300,000** (Sanders/Parsons/Santley; Calnek ≈ 200,000). Contemporary Europe: Paris ~150–185,000, Venice ~130,000, Constantinople ~200,000. **Safe sentence: "very probably larger than any city in western Europe." Never a precise figure.**

**Sequencing:** Bernal Díaz has Cortés first go out to see the city, the Tlatelolco market and the great temple **on the fourth day** after arrival.

## 7. The seizure of Moctezuma — ⚠️ HEAVILY DISPUTED

- **Pretext (Cortés):** **Quauhpopoca**, lord of Nauhtla ("Almería"), lured four Spaniards from the Vera Cruz garrison and killed two.
- **Method (Cortés):** guards stationed in the cross streets; he visited casually "as I had at other times gone to see him," talked "lightly on pleasant subjects," then demanded Moctezuma remain in the Spanish quarters. Quauhpopoca, his son and 15 others were **burned publicly in a square**; as they burned they said Moctezuma had ordered it; Cortés **had him put in chains, then removed the irons the same day.**
- **Status afterwards (Cortés's version):** kept his household and service, continued to govern and receive nobles.
- **⚠️ Modern scholars doubt any real captivity at this date.** MacNutt's own 1908 note: "Cortés's action is indefensible." **Restall** (*When Montezuma Met Cortés*): in **235 days no Spaniard wrote to the king claiming control of the city**, and indigenous accounts show Moctezuma still receiving ambassadors and tribute and speaking publicly. See also the HAHR article "**The Construction of an Arrest**."
- 🔑 **SAFE VERSION — and it's better drama anyway:** stage it as the Spanish **asserting** control from inside the palace while the city's routines visibly continue around them, and **let the boy be unable to tell whether the tlatoani is a prisoner.** That uncertainty is the historically honest reading. Put it in his mouth as "I don't know."

## 8. Cholula, 18 October 1519

- **Pretext:** **Malintzin**, reportedly warned by the wife of a Cholulan lord, told Cortés the Cholulans meant to kill the Spanish; Cortés claimed 20,000 Mexica troops were near. Bernal Díaz adds barricaded streets, stones piled on rooftops, families evacuated, and a warning carried in by **eight Tlaxcalans** from the camp outside.
- **Tlaxcalan role — documented and ugly.** Excluded from the city, camped in the fields. Cortés's captains agreed "we should at once warn the Tlaxcalans so that they might join in it." Bernal Díaz: they "**had fought very fiercely in the streets**… went about the city, **plundering and making prisoners and we could not stop them**," and next day "**more companies from the Tlaxcalan towns arrived, and did great damage.**" Cortés then ordered them back to the fields and **made them give up their captives** — "giving up the prisoners went against the grain with them."
- **Casualties — NO RELIABLE FIGURE.** Cortés: 3,000 in three hours. Gómara: 6,000. Vázquez de Tapia: up to 30,000. **Write "thousands." Nothing more.**
- **Instigation — disputed.** Spanish sources claim a pre-emptive strike; Sahagún's informants say unarmed civilians were killed with no military resistance. The Tlaxcalans had their own motive (Cholula had defected to the Mexica; their ambassador **Patlahuatzin** was said to have been tortured). **Restall** goes furthest: substantially a **Tlaxcalan initiative**, and even the route via Cholula was a Tlaxcalan choice. **McCafferty (2000)** documents a dozen contradictory first-hand accounts.
- 🔑 **Safe frame: the Tlaxcalans wanted Cholula punished; the Spanish supplied the occasion, the timing and the volley.** Do not assign single-author blame. **Depict from outside the wall only** (see the moderation rule in `01-STYLE-GUIDE.md`).

## 9. Material culture — the basis of every character lock

**Sumptuary law** (Anawalt, *Costume and Control*, 1980; Codex Mendoza; laws of Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina c. 1440–60). Sixteen laws governing fabric, ornament, garment length, accessories:
- **Only nobles may wear cotton.** Commoners and slaves wear coarse **maguey / ixtle fibre** (*ayate*, *ichtli*).
- **A commoner's cloak may not fall below the knee.** Noble mantles reach the ankle — embroidered, feathered, jewelled.
- **Violation punishable by death or enslavement.**
- Commoners wore **sandals only when travelling on roads — barefoot in the city.** Cortés independently confirms the top of the hierarchy: on the causeway **only Moctezuma was shod.**
- Garments: **maxtlatl** (loincloth), **tilmatli** (mantle knotted at the shoulder), **ichcahuipilli** (quilted cotton armour).

**Warrior rank = captives taken** (Codex Mendoza). Rank governed the **tlahuiztli** war suit and the right to sandals, jewellery, warpaint, hairstyle. Roughly: 1 captive → basic advance; **2 → white tlahuiztli + *pamitl* back-banner**; **3 → *papalotl* butterfly device**; **4 → jaguar (*ocelotl*) suit.** Above: eagle and jaguar knights, **Otomitl**, **Cuachicqueh** (Shorn Ones). **Back-devices were wooden frames roped to the back**, not carried.

**Hair — 🔑 the single best character detail available.** Boys entered the *telpochcalli* around 6–7. At about **10** the head was cut leaving a **lock at the nape — the *piochtli* — which he could not cut until he had taken his first captive.** A proven warrior (*tequihua*) wore the **temillotl**, the bound "pillar of stone" topknot. (Sahagún has Moctezuma cut his hair like a seasoned warrior on his fourth captive; Codex Mendoza shows the pillar after one — **inconsistent, don't assert a number.**) Priests: hair never cut or combed, matted, blood-clotted, black robes.

**Tlaxcalan visual identity.** Best-attested and eyewitness: **red-and-white livery.** Bernal Díaz saw Xicotencatl the Younger arrive with fifty chieftains "**all clothed in white and red cloaks, half of the cloak was white and the other half red, for this was the device and livery of Xicotenga.**" The **Lienzo de Tlaxcala** shows a **twisted red-and-white headband** as an ethnic marker and the **white heron (*aztatl*) back-standard for Tizatlan** — but the Lienzo is a mid-16th-c. pro-Tlaxcalan document made to advertise loyal service, so treat its iconography as partly retrospective self-branding. **Design choice: red-and-white twisted headband on the boy; half-red/half-white mantles on the lords; heron standard only for Tizatlan.**

## 10. Moctezuma II

Bernal Díaz, who saw him (ch. XCI): "**about forty years old**, of good height and well proportioned, slender, and muscular, not very attractive… He did not wear his hair long, but so as just to cover his ears, his **scanty black beard was well shaped and thin**. His face was somewhat long, but cheerful, and he had good eyes and showed in his appearance and manner both tenderness and, when necessary, gravity." **Bathed daily** towards evening; **never wore the same clothes again until four days later.** Rendered as **merry and ironic**: "Montezuma said, laughing, for he was very merry in his princely way of speaking."

**Age uncertain** — birth not securely recorded; c. 1466 commonly given (→ ~53). **Write "in his forties or early fifties," or just follow the eyewitness impression. Do not state a birth year.**

## 11. Nahuatl naming

(Kalyuta via Mexicolore; Wired Humanities *Nahuatl Dictionary* and *Visual Lexicon of Aztec Hieroglyphs*.)

- **Day-sign name at birth** from the 260-day *tonalpohualli*: **number 1–13 + day sign** (e.g. Nahui Olin, "Four Movement"). Named on the **seventh day**.
- A **temple name** could follow within three months, often referencing the calpulli deity.
- **Nobles:** multi-element names plus titles (*tecuhtli*). **Commoners:** usually a **single** name — often a **bare day-sign without the numerical coefficient** — or a small animal, bird or plant.
- **-tzin / -tzintli** is a diminutive functioning as a **reverential**. An **honorific of address**, not part of a commoner's own name. Our boy says **Maxixcatzin**; nobody says anything-*tzin* to our boy. 🔑 **If a lord ever addressed him with -tzin it would read as unusual warmth — reserved as a deliberate beat.**

**Protagonist: OLIN — confirmed usable.** 17th day sign, "movement / earthquake." Scholarly orthography is **Olin** (single *l*); "Ollin" is looser. Attested as a personal name element (*Nahui Olintzin* on a jaguar throne in the **Codex Quetzalecatzin**). Commoners often bore bare day-signs without the coefficient, so a commoner boy simply called **Olin** is defensible. Thematic bonus: "movement" for a runner, and the resonance with *Nahui Ollin*, the Fifth Sun that ends in earthquake.

**Other commoner names available** (attested stems, 1540/1544 Morelos & Cuernavaca censuses): **Tototl** "bird", **Elotl** "green corn cob", **Mimich** "little fish", **Telpoch** "young man", **Coyotl**, **Zolin** "quail". (*Yaotl*, "enemy/war," is accurate but heavy-handed — avoided.)

**Mexica noble boy names, all checked against no known historical figure:** **Xiuhtototzin** "turquoise bird" (an elite feather bird), **Quiyauhtzin** "rain", **Tepiltzin** "the noble child", **Chimaltzin** "shield".

**NAMES TO AVOID — real people:** Cuauhtemoc, Cacamatzin, Cuitláhuac, Axayacatl, Itzcoatl, Ahuitzotl, Tizoc, Tlacaelel, Chimalpopoca, Nezahualpilli, Ixtlilxochitl, Xicotencatl, Maxixcatzin, Tlacotzin, Ecatzin, Tzilacatzin, Itzquauhtzin, Cuauhtlatoatzin.

## 12. Quotes — what may and may not be used

### ❌ NEVER present as real
- **Moctezuma's welcome/submission speech** ("you are in your rightful place… all we possess is at your disposal"). Both the Cortés and Florentine Codex versions are **constructions** — Cortés had a direct legal interest in staging a voluntary submission to Charles V; the Sahagún version was compiled decades later in a colonial setting. Restall: no Spaniard in the city ever wrote to the king claiming the submission happened.
- **The Cortés-as-Quetzalcoatl / returning-god frame.** **Townsend, "Burying the White Gods"** (*AHR* 2003): no evidence of a pre-conquest story of Quetzalcoatl returning from the east, and little evidence anyone seriously took the Spanish for gods. A **post-conquest Franciscan construction.** This myth does not appear in this book in any form.
- **The "we like to have people to sacrifice" line.** Andrés de Tapia only. Usable **only** as a boast a character reports.

### ✅ Safe to dramatise (eyewitness reconstruction — attributed in the reader's endnote, never presented as transcript)
- **"Malinche, you and your brethren are in your own house, rest awhile."** — Bernal Díaz, at the palace door.
- 🔑 **"Look at me, and see that I am flesh and bones, the same as you, and everybody, and that I am mortal"** — Cortés's Second Letter. Moctezuma lifting his robes to disprove the gold-and-godhood rumours. **The best line in the episode for this book.**
- 🔑 **"I know very well that these people of Tlaxcala with whom you are such good friends have told you that I am a sort of God or Teul, and that everything in my houses is made of gold and silver"** — Bernal Díaz, who adds that he said it **laughing.** Doubly useful: it puts our protagonist's own people in the room as the source of the rumour.
- **Xicotencatl on the blockade:** "they ate no salt… nor did they wear any cotton clothing" (Cortés); "enmity was deeply rooted in their hearts" (Bernal Díaz).
- **Bernal Díaz's own wonder** (his, therefore safe): "we did not know what to say, or whether what appeared before us was real… and we, we did not even number four hundred soldiers!"

**Standing rule:** real figures get **paraphrased action and our own plain words**; verbatim quotation is reserved for lines attributable in the back-matter note. **Invent freely only for Olin.**
