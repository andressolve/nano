Only after recording the cold-read problems did I consult `00-PROJECT-BRIEF.md`, `RESEARCH.md`, and `02-CHARACTERS.md` to test whether the proposed repairs preserved the book’s structure and historical constraints.

The benchmark here is effortless first-pass comprehension: the clarity, pacing, and visual-verbal division of labor expected from a top-tier middle-grade/YA myth-history comic. Historical accuracy and legible lettering do not by themselves earn a pass.

## Cold-reader findings

The book’s large-scale arc is unusually clear. Olin’s deprivation, appetite for war, astonishment at the city, friendship with Xiuhtototzin, and final silence form a strong causal line. Most pages have one dominant dramatic job and most dialogue sounds spoken rather than explanatory.

Five continuity/readability problems interrupt an otherwise confident first read:

1. **“The Speaker” appears before the title is explained.** Page 10 expects the reader to infer that this is the city’s ruler. The project’s own editorial rule requires a first-use helper.
2. **Page 12 begins “He came” before the page has named the person arriving.** The art may rescue it, but the caption does not orient before elaborating.
3. **Page 18 contains both an ambiguous “he laughed at us” and a direct continuity contradiction.** Olin calls the ruler the first Mexica he has stood near, after spending four pages face-to-face with Xiuhtototzin.
4. **Page 19 suddenly makes Olin understand and speak Spanish.** Nothing in the preceding story establishes this; it conflicts with the story engine in which Malintzin mediates the strangers’ language. Calling Olin “the only person” able to speak to both sides also contradicts Malintzin’s presence in the same panel.
5. **Page 20 panel 2 reads like compressed research notes.** Bathing and wardrobe facts do not help the reader answer the scene’s urgent question—whether the ruler is a prisoner—and the panel is assigned several competing jobs.

## Required revisions

### 1. Page 10, panel 1 — explain the title on first use

**Why a cold reader stumbles:** “the Speaker’s own brother” assumes a title the book has not introduced. The reader should not have to infer whether “Speaker” means ruler, priest, diplomat, or literal public speaker.

**Replace the P1 caption with:**

> Before the city there was a town on the shore called Iztapalapa. It was ruled by the brother of the tlatoani — the Speaker, their king.

This follows the project brief’s exact first-use model and makes later uses of “the Speaker” effortless.

**Panel-plan repair:** add Malintzin to P3’s visual description because she owns the line in that panel:

> Mid-ground: the Spanish captain walking the paved edge of the pool with measured, deliberate paces, looking down at his own feet. The young woman in the white cotton tunic stands nearby, watching him and the boy.

No lettering change is needed in P3.

### 2. Page 12, panel 1 — replace the unsupported pronoun

**Why a cold reader stumbles:** “Then the city came out to meet us. He came…” asks the reader to invent the male antecedent before the caption names the office or the image establishes identity.

**Replace the second P1 caption with:**

> The Speaker came in a litter under a canopy of green feathers. Lords walked ahead of him sweeping the road and laying cloths down so that his feet would not touch the earth.

The sentence now orients before describing protocol.

### 3. Page 18, panels 1 and 4 — repair pronouns and continuity

**Why a cold reader stumbles:** “he laughed at us” can mean the ruler laughed at Olin, the Tlaxcalans, the strangers, or everyone present. Later, “the first Mexica I ever stood close enough to” contradicts Olin’s intimate friendship with a Mexica boy on pages 14–17.

**Replace the P1 lower-left caption with:**

> Which is how I came to be standing in the room when the Speaker laughed at what my people had told the strangers.

**Replace the P4 lower-right caption with:**

> He was the man my people had called a god. I stood close enough to hear him breathing.

The second replacement preserves the intended demystification while respecting Xiuhtototzin’s existence.

### 4. Page 19 — remove the unearned Spanish fluency and create a causal bridge into page 20

**Why a cold reader stumbles:** Olin has been shown sharing Nahuatl with the Mexica and relying on Malintzin for the strangers’ words. The script suddenly says he understood “both languages” and was the only person who could speak to both sides, although Malintzin is standing in the foreground. Neither assertion has been earned, and the second is plainly self-contradictory.

The cleanest repair is to let the reader understand the Spanish exchange while Olin reads Malintzin’s reaction and only confirms its meaning when the ruler moves into the strangers’ rooms.

**Replace the P1 lower-left caption with:**

> I was in the doorway with an empty jar while they spoke about what to do.

**Keep all three P2 speech bubbles unchanged.** They are for the reader; Olin need not understand them word for word.

**Replace both P3 captions with:**

> I did not know the strangers’ language. I knew her face. She looked at me exactly once and said nothing.

> When the Speaker crossed into their rooms, I understood what I had witnessed. My legs were the only thing I had ever been proud of.

This preserves Olin as “a boy made of ears” without turning him into a fluent interpreter, preserves Malintzin’s unique narrative function, and creates a direct causal transition into page 20.

### 5. Page 20, panel 2 — give the panel one job

**Why a cold reader stumbles:** the current caption asks one panel to explain relocation, household movement, continuing government, daily court attendance, bathing, and costume rotation. The bathing and cloak facts are vivid research but irrelevant to the immediate dramatic question. They delay rather than deepen the ambiguity.

**Replace the P2 caption with:**

> Inside the strangers’ quarters, he still heard petitions and received lords at dawn. His household came with him. Nothing about the room told me whether he could leave.

This keeps every historically safe element needed by the scene and returns the final sentence to Olin’s immediate uncertainty.
