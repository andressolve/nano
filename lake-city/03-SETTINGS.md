# 03 — SETTINGS

Every detail below is from `RESEARCH.md`. **The visual argument of the volume is Tlaxcala vs. the lake city: crooked, dry, ochre, hand-made against ruled, wet, white, engineered.** Never let the two worlds share a palette.

---

## 1. TLAXCALA — hill country, September 1519

Dry upland, crooked streets on hillsides, low adobe houses with flat roofs, terraces of **maguey** (thick grey-green spiked agave) stepping down the slopes. Dust, ochre, grey-green, sun-bleached brown. **No cotton anywhere on a commoner's back; no salt in the food.** Lean stray dogs, turkeys, cooking smoke, women grinding maize. Woven maguey cloth drying on lines. Light: hard, high, dry, short shadows.

**The lords' council house:** a low rectangular room, whitewashed walls with a painted red band at floor level, reed mats, low reed-backed seats for the lords, braziers, a doorway of hanging cloth. Everyone else stands or crouches at the edges. Dim, warm, close.

**Contrast note:** Tlaxcala is *hand-made*. Nothing here is straight. Save every straight line for the city.

## 2. THE CHOLULA ROAD AND CHOLULA — October 1519

Flat maize country between volcanoes. **Cholula:** a large old holy city of many small temples and one **colossal ancient stepped pyramid, so overgrown it reads as a green hill with a temple on its summit.** Whitewashed courtyards, high enclosure walls, narrow streets.

**The Tlaxcalan camp is OUTSIDE, in the open fields** — thousands of men squatting around small fires beyond the city wall, looking at the rooftops they are not allowed to enter. Cold night, dew, flat dark plain, the city glowing behind its wall.

**For the massacre pages: we never go inside.** The camera stays with Olin outside the enclosure wall — smoke rising over whitewashed parapets, the sound described in captions, men coming back out through a gate carrying bundles and roped captives. No violence depicted. See the moderation rule in `01-STYLE-GUIDE.md`.

## 3. THE PASS BETWEEN THE VOLCANOES — c. 1 November 1519

High pine forest giving way to bare rock and snow. **Popocatépetl**, a near-perfect cone trailing a straight plume of smoke, and **Iztaccíhuatl**, longer and flatter, both snow-capped. Thin cold air, breath visible, frost in the shadows, black volcanic gravel. The column strung out along a narrow track. Light: sharp, blue-shadowed, high-altitude.

## 4. THE FIRST SIGHT OF THE LAKE — the hero page

From the height, the whole basin at once: a vast shallow lake system filling the valley floor, mountains all round, and the white city sitting out in the water with **four dead-straight causeways** reaching to the shore. Towns ringing the lakeshore. Canoes as countless small dark marks. Morning mist lying flat on the water; the city lime-white; the water **turquoise** — the volume's accent at its absolute maximum here. This page is the promise the whole book is written to keep.

## 5. IZTAPALAPA — 7 November 1519

A large lakeshore town of twelve to fifteen thousand households, ruled by the Speaker's brother. Well-built stone-and-plaster houses, and **gardens**: flowers in beds, fruit trees, cut walkways, canes and reeds. Its centrepiece is a **masonry-lined pool four hundred paces square**, its edges paved and finished, **full of fish and waterfowl**, with a walkway around it. Wet stone, reflections, birds. Light: late afternoon, low gold.

## 6. THE IZTAPALAPA CAUSEWAY — 8 November 1519

**The single most important location in the book.** A raised stone road running two leagues dead straight across open water toward the city — **"two lances broad," wide enough for eight horsemen abreast.** Open lake on both sides, so crowded with canoes that the water is barely visible; more canoes come in to stare. Towns built out on the water along the way. Where the road crosses a channel there is a **wooden bridge about ten paces wide, made of long heavy beams that can be lifted out and put back whenever the city wishes** — ⭐ *plant this deliberately; it is the hinge of Books Two and Three.* Where two causeways join there is a **fort with two towers** and a wall with two gates.

Light: bright mid-morning, glare off water, the city growing ahead.

## 7. TENOCHTITLÁN — streets, canals, chinampas

Half the streets are packed earth, half are water. **Whitewashed lime-plaster buildings, flat roofs, low parapets, painted bands of cochineal red and ochre** at the base of walls. Everything is **rectilinear**: a grid of straight canals and straight streets. Canoes everywhere, including **canoes selling drinking water door to door**. **Chinampas** at the edges — long narrow garden plots built up out of the shallow lake, six to ten metres wide and up to two hundred long, edged with willows, growing maize, beans, flowers. Clean swept streets. Crowds. The city is **pale, wet, and geometric**, and it should feel *larger than the panel*.

**The aqueduct:** **two masonry channels side by side, each about two paces wide and five feet deep**, running from Chapultepec — **one carrying a column of fresh water as thick as a man's body while the other lies dry, so that the first can be cleaned.** Where the causeway is cut by channels, pipes carry the water across. Men stand above the conduits filling canoes with water for a fee.

## 8. THE PALACE OF AXAYACATL — the Spanish quarters

The dead Speaker's palace, next to the current Speaker's own residence, entered through a great court. Whitewashed masonry, great halls and chambers, painted borders, cedar beams, **beds of matting under cloth canopies**. A sealed room somewhere in it holds the old Speaker's treasure. The Spanish take the inner rooms. **The thousand Tlaxcalan porters sleep in the outer courts** among stacked bundles, cannon, and horse-lines — that is Olin's home for the second half of the book: a cold court, straw, snoring men, and Spanish voices echoing off plaster from the rooms inside. Light at night: braziers and small fires, deep blue shadow, whitewash going grey.

## 9. THE TLATELOLCO MARKET — the great set piece

An arcaded square **twice the size of the main square of Salamanca**, with **more than sixty thousand people in it in a day**, and — the detail that makes the page — **every commodity has its own street, strictly enforced.**

Streets for: gold and silver and copper; stone, bones, shells and feathers; hewn stone, adobe and timber; **birds** — turkeys, quail, ducks, doves, parrots, owls, eaglets, falcons, kestrels — and the whole prepared **skins of birds of prey with feathers, heads, beaks and claws**; rabbits, hares and **small edible dogs**; a **herb street** with shops "like apothecary shops"; **barbers** cutting hair; cookshops; **porters standing for hire**; charcoal, braziers, sleeping mats; onions, garlic, cresses; maguey honey and pulque; **skeins of dyed cotton stacked in colour blocks "like one of the silk markets of Granada"**; painters' pigments; deerskins; glazed painted pottery; maize bread; fish fresh, salted, cooked, raw; **and salt.**

**Order is the theme here, not chaos.** Everything is sold by measure and nothing by weight. There is a **court at one side of the square where ten or twelve judges sit** hearing disputes, and inspectors who walk the aisles and **break false measures in front of the seller.**

And, honestly, in the same square: **enslaved people brought in for sale, tied to long poles by collars around their necks.** Rendered plainly, at middle distance, no closer.

## 10. THE SACRED PRECINCT

A walled enclosure so large "a village of five hundred houses could be built inside it," containing about **forty towers**, the greatest of them taller than the cathedral tower of Seville, approached by a long stair. Whitewashed and painted, banners, braziers of copal smoke. At the summit, **twin shrines side by side** — one to the war god, one to the rain god — each brightly painted, the war god's in cochineal red and black.

**Priests in black, hair never cut or combed, matted and clotted.** Inner chapels are dark, closed to everyone but the priests, and **the walls are black with dried blood.** The smell of copal and blood; Bernal Díaz compares the reek to a slaughterhouse.

**The tzompantli:** a rack "full of skulls and large bones set in perfect order, which one could look at but could not count." **Distance and silhouette only** — never a close study, never a face.

**Sacrifice discipline for this location, absolute:** aftermath, testimony, and implication only. A scrubbed stone. A priest's hands. Xiuhtototzin's flat matter-of-fact explanation. Olin's face while he listens. **Never the act.**

## 11. THE BIRD AND ANIMAL HOUSES

The most astonishing place in the book, and the page where the reader falls for the city.

**Ten pools** in one court — **salt water for the sea-birds, fresh for the lake birds**, each drained and refilled through pipes. Birds of every kind, in their thousands, each species with its own keepers who know its food. The fish-eating birds alone are fed **two hundred and fifty pounds of fish a day.** **Three hundred men work on the birds** — plus doctors whose only job is sick birds. Galleries run above the pools so the Speaker can come and watch.

A second court **paved like a chessboard**, filled with **individual latticed cages for birds of prey, from kestrel to eagle**, each cage with a perch for night and a perch for sun.

And rooms of heavy timber cages holding **jaguars, foxes and every kind of cat** — with **another three hundred men** to keep them. Also apartments for people the Speaker keeps at court: albinos, dwarfs.

Light: green, wet, dappled, feathers everywhere, deafening bird noise implied by composition.

## 12. THE AUDIENCE — the Speaker's own palace

**At dawn every single day, six hundred lords and men of rank come to the palace** and wait — filling halls and corridors without entering his room, their own retainers filling two or three great courts and spilling into the street, and they stay until night. The larder and cellar stand open to all of them. **Six hundred nobles waiting all day, every day, for nothing in particular:** that is the scale of the thing our boy has walked into, and it should be staged as an oppressive, beautiful crowd of embroidered cotton seen past Olin's bare shoulder.

Great mat-floored hall, whitewash, painted dados, cedar beams, low dais, feather screens, copal smoke.

## 13. THE ROOFTOP AT NIGHT — the last page

A flat plastered rooftop of the Axayacatl palace complex above the sleeping city. Below: straight dark canals holding broken reflections, the lake stretching to the mountains, and **thousands of small lights** — braziers, canoe lamps, temple fires — laid out on the ruled grid. The twin shrines lit high on the great temple. Cold, still, enormous. One barefoot boy, small, at the parapet.

**Turquoise at maximum, and for the last time in the series at this intensity.** In Book Two it starts to drain.
