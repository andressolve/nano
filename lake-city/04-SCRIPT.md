# 04 — SCRIPT
## THE LAKE CITY, Book One: The Boy Who Wanted the War

Cover + 22 pages. **All lettering below is VERBATIM.** Copy it character-for-character into the `LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:` block. Do not paraphrase, do not "improve," do not add captions that aren't here.

**Reading the page headers:**
- **GRID** — paste this into the LAYOUT block.
- **REFS** — the `imagePaths` to attach to the `edit_image` call. Look at each one before writing the prompt.
- **T** — density tier (see `01-STYLE-GUIDE.md`).

**Two standing rules:**
1. Speech-bubble strings carry **no quotation marks** — the bubble is the quote.
2. Caption boxes are **Olin's narration**, first person, past tense, off-white box with dark serif text.

**LETTERING:**
- P1, caption box, upper left: `The lords of our four towns argued for two days.`
- P1, speech bubble, tail to the lord in the white cotton mantle: `We have hated the Mexica for a hundred years and we have not moved them one step. These strangers move things.`

- P3, speech bubble, tail to the young captain standing in the middle: `And when the lake city falls, what stands between these men and us? Nothing. We will be the last thing left to take.`

## PAGE 7 — I heard it

**GRID:** THREE panels: two equal panels across the TOP, one WIDE panel across the BOTTOM.
**REFS:** `ref_olin.png`, `ref_tototl.png`
**T:** T5

⚠️ **MODERATION + EDITORIAL: no violence is depicted on this page.** The camera never goes inside the wall. If this page is refused, the fix is to move the camera further out and reduce the smoke — not to soften words.

- **P1** — Pre-dawn, grey. The boy sitting up sharply among sleeping porters in the field camp, head turned toward the city, listening.
- **P2** — The enclosure wall seen from the field, whitewashed parapets, smoke rising past them into a colourless sky. Foreground: the backs of standing men, all facing the wall, all still.
- **P3** — WIDE. Later, full daylight. Tlaxcalan warriors streaming back out of a gateway into the fields carrying rolled cotton cloth and bundles of feathers, some leading roped captives. Mid-ground: the mounted Spanish captain, one arm raised, ordering the captives released. The boy stands to one side holding an empty water jar, watching. The older porter beside him with empty hands.

**LETTERING:**
- P1, caption box, upper left: `I did not see what happened inside Cholula. I heard it.`
- P1, caption box, lower right: `Malintzin had been told the Cholulans meant to kill the strangers in their sleep. Whether that was true, I still do not know.`
- P2, caption box, upper right: `It began before the sun was properly up, and it did not last long.`
- P3, speech bubble, tail to a Tlaxcalan warrior carrying rolled cloth: `Move, boy. There is cotton in there.`
- P3, speech bubble, tail to the older porter standing empty-handed: `Don't touch it.`
- P3, caption box, lower left: `Our own companies went in. They came back out with cotton, with feathers, and with captives on ropes. Then the strangers made them give the captives up, and that was the only time that day I saw our men truly angry.`
- P3, caption box, lower right: `Thousands died in Cholula. No two people have ever agreed how many.`

**NOTE — accuracy:** Tlaxcalan looting and captive-taking is documented, as is Cortés forcing the captives' release. Do not assign single-author blame for the massacre itself. (`RESEARCH.md` §8)

## PAGE 16 — Mostly from your country

**GRID:** THREE panels: two equal panels across the TOP, one WIDE panel across the BOTTOM.
**REFS:** `ref_olin.png`, `ref_xiuhtototzin.png`
**T:** T4

⚠️ **MODERATION + EDITORIAL: aftermath and testimony only.** Scrubbed stone, priests, distance. **No body, no blade, no act.** The skull rack appears only in silhouette in the background of P1, small. If refused: pull the camera back further and drop the rack entirely.

- **P1** — The top of the great temple: two brightly painted shrines side by side, one in cochineal red and black. Braziers of copal smoke. A wide platform of pale scrubbed stone, wet from washing. Two black-robed priests with long matted uncut hair at the edge of frame. Far background below, small and in silhouette, a rack of pale shapes.
- **P2** — Close two-shot of the boys, the Mexica boy explaining without any cruelty at all, simply informing.
- **P3** — WIDE. The two boys small on the platform, backs to us, looking out over the whole shining city and lake spread below them.

**LETTERING:**
- P1, caption box, upper left: `Then he took me up the great temple, because he was proud of it.`
- P1, speech bubble, tail to the boy in the long white cotton mantle: `Two shrines at the top. Rain on the left. War on the right.`
- P1, caption box, lower right: `The steps had been washed. The stone at the top had been scrubbed pale. The priests wore black and their hair had never once been cut or combed.`
- P2, speech bubble, tail to the barefoot boy: `Where do the captives come from?`
- P2, speech bubble, tail to the boy in the long white mantle: `Mostly the wars. Mostly from your country.`

# BACK MATTER — for the reader's end interstitial

**A note on what is true.** Put this in the reader after page 22, before the quiz, in the user's voice as the collection's editor:

> Olin is invented. Xiuhtototzin is invented. Everyone else in this book was a real person, and everything that happens to them happened.
>
> A thousand Tlaxcalan porters really did walk into Tenochtitlán with the Spanish in November 1519, to carry the guns and the baggage and clear the road — one of the soldiers who was there wrote it down, and he also wrote down that Tlaxcalans inside the city were the ones passing warnings to the interpreters. Tlaxcala really had been ringed in for generations without salt or cotton; a Tlaxcalan lord said so himself, and it was written into a letter to the King of Spain. The gold-soled sandals, the swept road, the glass beads traded for shell collars hung with golden shrimps, the sixty thousand people in the market, the twelve judges, the broken measures, the two water channels with one kept dry for cleaning, the three hundred men who worked only on the birds — all of that is from people who stood there and saw it.
>
> Three things in this book are honestly uncertain, and the book says so rather than pretending:
> - **Nobody knows how many people died at Cholula.** The eyewitness numbers range from three thousand to thirty thousand and none of them can be trusted. And the Tlaxcalans, who had their own reasons to want Cholula punished, did their share of the looting and the captive-taking.
> - **Nobody is sure the Speaker was really a prisoner** when the Spanish said he was. Historians now doubt it. That is why Olin cannot answer his friend's question.
> - **The Speaker's famous welcome speech, where he supposedly hands his empire over, is almost certainly not something he said.** It appears in accounts written by people who needed it to be true. So it is not in this book. Neither is the story that the Mexica thought the Spanish were returning gods — there is no good evidence anyone believed that, and the one place it comes up here, the Speaker is laughing at it.
>
> Two lines really were reported by men who were in the room, and they are the only quotations in the book: **"You and your brothers are in your own house. Rest awhile."** And: **"Look at me. I am flesh and bones, the same as you and as everybody. And I am mortal."**

# THE QUIZ — 5 questions, WHY not WHAT

Correct answers shuffled **b / c / a / d / b**. Distractors are substantive and of similar length. Feedback text should teach, not just confirm.

**Q1. Why had Olin never tasted salt before he reached the market at Tlatelolco?**
a) Salt was reserved for nobles by law throughout the Nahua world, and he was a commoner.
b) ✅ The Mexica had ringed Tlaxcala in for generations, so no salt could be traded into his country at all.
c) Tlaxcala was too high and too dry for the salt pans that the lake towns used.
d) Salt was used only in temple offerings, and commoners were forbidden to eat it.
> ✅ Tlaxcala was never conquered — but it was surrounded. A Tlaxcalan lord himself described being unable to buy salt or wear cotton, and choosing to stay hungry and free. That blockade, not a law, is what Olin's whole hatred is made of.

**Q2. Why were the Tlaxcalans let into Tenochtitlán when Cholula had refused them entry?**
a) The Speaker wanted to show the Tlaxcalan lords how much stronger his city was.
b) They had fought for the Spanish at Cholula and were now treated as soldiers rather than servants.
c) ✅ They came in as porters and road-clearers, not as a war party — a thousand carriers rather than ten thousand warriors.
d) A truce had been agreed between Tlaxcala and the Mexica for the length of the strangers' visit.
> ✅ Tlaxcala offered ten thousand warriors. Cortés took one thousand men to haul the cannon and the baggage. That is exactly why the city's gates opened: nobody lets in an enemy army, but everyone lets in the luggage. It is also why Olin — a porter — ends up inside every room that matters.

**Q3. On the causeway, every great lord was barefoot and only the Speaker wore sandals. Why does that detail matter so much to Olin?**
a) ✅ Because rank in his world was worn on the body, and his own bare feet and short coarse cloak marked him as nothing at all.
b) Because going barefoot was a religious act of humility that outsiders were not permitted to perform.
c) Because gold on the feet meant the Speaker had taken more captives in war than any other man there.
d) Because it proved the lords were servants rather than rulers of their own cities.
> ✅ Nahua law regulated who could wear cotton, how long a cloak could be, and who could wear sandals in a city — and breaking those rules could cost your life. Olin's knee-length maguey cloak is a legal statement about his worth. So when he and Xiuhtototzin compare cloth, they are really comparing what each of them is allowed to be.

**Q4. Why can't Olin answer when Xiuhtototzin asks whether the Speaker is a prisoner?**
a) He has been ordered by Malintzin not to discuss anything he overhears in the palace.
b) He does not yet know that the lord from the coast has been burned in the square.
c) He is afraid that telling the truth would put his friend's family in danger.
d) ✅ Because nothing visibly changed — the Speaker still governed, still heard petitions, still had his household — and yet everything had.
> ✅ Historians today doubt that Moctezuma was truly a captive at this point; no Spaniard even wrote to the king claiming they controlled the city. The book keeps Olin's honest uncertainty instead of tidying it into a clean arrest, because the uncertainty is the historically accurate answer.

**Q5. Olin tells Xiuhtototzin the strangers only want gold. Why is that the most important moment in the book?**
a) Because it is the first time he has understood the strangers' real plan.
b) ✅ Because he knows the shape of what is coming, and choosing to say nothing makes him part of it.
c) Because Malintzin has instructed him to keep the Tlaxcalan lords' intentions secret.
d) Because he has finally decided that his loyalty belongs to Tlaxcala rather than to his friend.
> ✅ He wanted this war before he ever saw the city. By the last page he has seen it, loved it, tasted its salt, and stood on its temple — and he still says nothing. He is not a witness to what happens next. He is one of the reasons it happens.
