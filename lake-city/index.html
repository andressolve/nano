After the story

A note on what is true.

Olin is invented. Xiuhtototzin is invented. Everyone else in this book was a real person, and everything that happens to them happened.

A thousand Tlaxcalan porters really did walk into Tenochtitlán with the Spanish in November 1519, to carry the guns and the baggage and clear the road — one of the soldiers who was there wrote it down, and he also wrote down that Tlaxcalans inside the city were the ones passing warnings to the interpreters. Tlaxcala really had been ringed in for generations without salt or cotton; a Tlaxcalan lord said so himself, and it was written into a letter to the King of Spain. The gold-soled sandals, the swept road, the glass beads traded for shell collars hung with golden shrimps, the sixty thousand people in the market, the twelve judges, the broken measures, the two water channels with one kept dry for cleaning, the three hundred men who worked only on the birds — all of that is from people who stood there and saw it.

Three things in this book are honestly uncertain, and the book says so rather than pretending:

  • Nobody knows how many people died at Cholula. The eyewitness numbers range from three thousand to thirty thousand and none of them can be trusted. And the Tlaxcalans, who had their own reasons to want Cholula punished, did their share of the looting and the captive-taking.
  • Nobody is sure the Speaker was really a prisoner when the Spanish said he was. Historians now doubt it. That is why Olin cannot answer his friend's question.
  • The Speaker's famous welcome speech, where he supposedly hands his empire over, is almost certainly not something he said. It appears in accounts written by people who needed it to be true. So it is not in this book. Neither is the story that the Mexica thought the Spanish were returning gods — there is no good evidence anyone believed that, and the one place it comes up here, the Speaker is laughing at it.

Two lines really were reported by men who were in the room, and they are the only quotations in the book: "You and your brothers are in your own house. Rest awhile." And: "Look at me. I am flesh and bones, the same as you and as everybody. And I am mortal."

Five questions · why, not what

Test your understanding.

Choose the best explanation. Each answer opens a short note about why the detail matters.

1. Why had Olin never tasted salt before he reached the market at Tlatelolco?

Choose an answer first.

Tlaxcala was never conquered — but it was surrounded. A Tlaxcalan lord himself described being unable to buy salt or wear cotton, and choosing to stay hungry and free. That blockade, not a law, is what Olin's whole hatred is made of.

2. Why were the Tlaxcalans let into Tenochtitlán when Cholula had refused them entry?

Choose an answer first.

Tlaxcala offered ten thousand warriors. Cortés took one thousand men to haul the cannon and the baggage. That is exactly why the city's gates opened: nobody lets in an enemy army, but everyone lets in the luggage. It is also why Olin — a porter — ends up inside every room that matters.

3. On the causeway, every great lord was barefoot and only the Speaker wore sandals. Why does that detail matter so much to Olin?

Choose an answer first.

Nahua law regulated who could wear cotton, how long a cloak could be, and who could wear sandals in a city — and breaking those rules could cost your life. Olin's knee-length maguey cloak is a legal statement about his worth. So when he and Xiuhtototzin compare cloth, they are really comparing what each of them is allowed to be.

4. Why can't Olin answer when Xiuhtototzin asks whether the Speaker is a prisoner?

Choose an answer first.

Historians today doubt that Moctezuma was truly a captive at this point; no Spaniard even wrote to the king claiming they controlled the city. The book keeps Olin's honest uncertainty instead of tidying it into a clean arrest, because the uncertainty is the historically accurate answer.

5. Olin tells Xiuhtototzin the strangers only want gold. Why is that the most important moment in the book?

Choose an answer first.

He wanted this war before he ever saw the city. By the last page he has seen it, loved it, tasted its salt, and stood on its temple — and he still says nothing. He is not a witness to what happens next. He is one of the reasons it happens.
