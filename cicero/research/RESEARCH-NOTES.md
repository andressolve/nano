# Cicero — Research Notes (web-verified)

**Purpose:** Ground-truth archive for the Cicero biography. Built research-first per `bio.md` §2 and the
gold-standard bar ([[feedback-brilliant-exemplars]]). Every date, sequence, and quote below was checked
against web sources (June 2026), not model knowledge. Editorial-honesty flags are marked **⚑**. No fake
ancient quotes — quotes are reproduced verbatim with source, and reported/literary quotes are hedged.

Primary sources behind the modern accounts: Cicero's own speeches, letters (esp. *Ad Atticum*), and treatises;
Sallust (*Bellum Catilinae*); Plutarch (*Life of Cicero*); Seneca the Elder (*Suasoriae* 6, citing the
historian Aufidius Bassus) and Cassius Dio for the death.

---

## 1. The one-sentence window

*A small-town nobody with no famous name and no army climbed to the top of Rome on the power of his voice
alone — and in the end discovered that words cannot stop legions; the hands that wrote against a warlord were
nailed up beside his head.*

Window: birth at Arpinum, **3 January 106 BC** → murder near Formiae, **7 December 43 BC** (age 63).

## 3. The Catilinarian conspiracy (63 BC) — verified detail

- **Who:** Lucius Sergius **Catilina** (Catiline), a patrician who had lost the consular election for 62 to Cicero's circle, plotted an armed coup. ⚑ His support rested on **real grievances** — crushing debt, dispossessed Sullan veterans, rural poverty. Don't render him a cartoon: he was a dangerous man riding a genuine crisis.
- **The speeches:** **Four** *Catilinarian Orations*. The **First** was delivered **7 or 8 November 63 BC** in the **Temple of Jupiter Stator**, after assassins sent to kill Cicero failed.
  - **Verbatim opening (genuine):**
    > *Quō ūsque tandem abūtēre, Catilīna, patientiā nostrā? Quam diū etiam furor iste tuus nōs ēlūdet?*
    > "How long, Catiline, will you go on abusing our patience? How much longer will that madness of yours mock us?"
  - **"O tempora, o mores!"** ("Oh, the times! Oh, the morals!") is also from the First Catilinarian. **Genuine.**
- **The fatal decision:** In December Cicero arrested five conspirators in the city (Lentulus Sura and others). On **5 December 63**, after a Senate debate in which **Caesar argued against execution** and **Cato argued for it**, the Senate (under the emergency *senatus consultum ultimum*) had them **strangled in the Tullianum prison — without trial.** ⚑ **This is the load-bearing irony of the whole biography:** executing Roman citizens without the right of appeal (*provocatio*) was legally explosive. His finest hour planted the seed of his exile five years later (§2, 58 BC).
- He was voted **"pater patriae"** ("father of the fatherland"). **Genuine** honorific.

## 4. The Philippics & the miscalculation (44–43 BC) — verified detail

- **14 speeches** against **Mark Antony**, delivered **September 44 BC – April 43 BC**.
- **Name:** Cicero consciously modeled them on **Demosthenes' "Philippics"** against **Philip II of Macedon** — the Athenian orator who tried, and failed, to rouse a free city against an autocrat. ⚑ The parallel is deliberate and tragic: Cicero knew he was casting himself as the last voice of a dying republic.
- **The miscalculation (the spine's hinge):** Cicero threw his weight behind the **young Octavian** (Caesar's 19-year-old heir) as a counterweight to Antony — legitimizing Octavian's private army. He is said to have remarked that the young man should be "praised, honored, and removed" (*laudandum, ornandum, tollendum* — reported, hedge as tradition). Instead **Octavian reconciled with Antony**, formed the Second Triumvirate, and handed Cicero over. ⚑ The word that beat Catiline and Verres could not out-maneuver men with legions.

## 5. The word carried into Latin — the philosophy (45–44 BC)

⚑ **This is what makes Cicero more than a politician and gives the book its intellectual milestone.** Shut out of public life by Caesar's dictatorship and gutted by Tullia's death, Cicero spent 45–44 BC translating the schools of Greek philosophy into Latin for a Roman audience — and **had to invent the vocabulary to do it.**

- He coined roughly **150 Latin philosophical terms** by translating Greek concepts: *qualitas* (quality), *quantitas* (quantity), *essentia* (essence), *humanitas*, and many more.
- Words we still use that trace to his coinages/translations include **quality, quantity, individual, vacuum, moral, property, induction, element, definition, difference, notion, comprehension, infinity, science, image, species**.
- Key works of the burst: *De Finibus*, *Tusculanae Disputationes*, *De Natura Deorum*, *Academica*, *De Divinatione*, *De Fato*, ***De Officiis*** ("On Duties," 44 BC — his most influential book for later centuries). Earlier: *De Oratore* (55), *De Re Publica* (54–51), *De Legibus*.
- ⚑ **Petrarch's rediscovery of Cicero's letters in 1345** is traditionally credited with helping spark the Renaissance — a clean way to land the "the word outlived the armies" theme without overclaiming.

## 6. The death (7 December 43 BC) — verified detail

Main source: **Plutarch**, *Life of Cicero*; the reported last words come from **Seneca the Elder** (citing the historian **Aufidius Bassus**); the most lurid detail (Fulvia) is from **Cassius Dio**.

- Proscribed by the Triumvirate, Cicero tried to flee by sea from his villa near **Formiae**, hesitating, turning back more than once (⚑ the vacillation persists to the very end — but so does courage).
- Betrayed by **Philologus**, a freedman of his brother **Quintus**. His own slaves would not give him up.
- The killers: **Herennius** (a centurion) and **Popillius** (a military tribune).
- ⚑ **The dignified ending (use this register):** Plutarch says Cicero leaned his head **out of the litter and bared his neck** to the sword, telling the soldiers to strike — facing death without resistance. Whatever his lifelong waverings, he met the end bravely. Reported last words (frame as *"by one tradition"*):
  > *Ego vero consisto. Accede, veterane, et, si hoc saltim potes recte facere, incide cervicem.*
  > "I'll stop here. Come, soldier — and if you can at least do this one thing properly, cut through this neck."
- **Beheaded; his hands cut off** — ⚑ on Antony's order, **the hands that had written the Philippics** — and head + hands **nailed to the Rostra** in the Forum, the very platform from which he had spoken. The single most important image in the book: *the word, and what power did to the hands that wrote it.*
- ⚑ Cassius Dio adds that **Fulvia** (Antony's wife) jabbed his dead tongue with a hairpin. **Later, sensational, single-source — DO NOT depict** (gory, and dubious). The Rostra image alone carries the meaning.
- ⚑ **Closing irony (verify framing, traditional):** Cicero's son **Marcus**, as suffect consul in **30 BC**, reportedly helped announce Antony's death and the fall of his statues — the family name had the last word over Antony. Optional grace note for a finale, hedge as tradition.

## 7. Honest portrait — the man (do NOT sand this off)

Per [[feedback-let-the-subject-be]], render the real personality, not a hero cut-out:

- **Vain — provably so.** He wrote an epic poem praising his own consulship (*De Consulatu Suo*) containing the notorious line **"O fortunatam natam me consule Romam!"** ("O fortunate Rome, born in my consulship!") — ⚑ **mocked by the ancients themselves** for its jingly vanity. Also from the same self-praise: **"Cedant arma togae"** ("Let arms yield to the toga" — i.e., let the soldier defer to the statesman) — ⚑ genuine, but it's Cicero praising *himself*, and it too was ridiculed. Use these as honest character, not as neutral wisdom.
- **Vacillating.** His shifts in the civil war and his hesitation at the end are real and well documented; even sympathetic historians note "the vacillation of his political conduct in desperate crises, and the whining despondency of his times of adversity." He could be a coward in a crisis.
- **But self-aware and humane.** He could laugh at his own vanity; the letters to Atticus show a man arguing with himself. He chose the Republic over the safe alliance (declined the Triumvirate, 60 BC) and died for the choice.
- **The letters are the intimate voice.** ~900 letters survive, mostly to **Titus Pomponius Atticus** — his lifelong friend, banker, sounding board. They are the most candid surviving record of any ancient Roman's inner life: ambition, fear, grief over Tullia, doubt about Pompey. ⚑ This is the device that lets the book show the *private* man behind the public orator — a real, ownable narration source (the letters are genuine; do not invent letter-text, paraphrase honestly).

## 8. ⚑ Editorial-honesty flags (carry into the script)

1. **No fake ancient quotes.** Many "Cicero quotes" online are misattributions or loose paraphrases (e.g. "A room without books is like a body without a soul" — loose paraphrase; "The enemy is within the gates" — not a verbatim line). Use ONLY the verbatim quotes vetted in §3–§7, each rendered in Latin with an English helper per the project rule.
2. **Reported quotes get hedged.** The last words (§6) and *laudandum, ornandum, tollendum* (§4) come from later sources — frame as "by one account / by tradition," never as documentary fact.
3. **He was rich, not a poor boy.** The *novus homo* story is about lacking a senatorial name and faction, not poverty. Don't run a rags-to-riches frame.
4. **Catiline had real grievances.** Debt crisis + dispossessed veterans. Don't flatten him to a pantomime villain; the danger was real *and* the causes were real.
5. **The execution without trial is the moral knot, not a clean triumph.** Hold both: it broke the conspiracy *and* it was a lawless act that justly haunted him. This honesty is the spine's engine.
6. **The civil-war choice was agonized, not heroic-clean.** Show the vacillation (the letters prove it).
7. **Don't overclaim "saved the Republic."** He stopped Catiline; he could not stop Caesar, Antony, or the legions. The Republic died anyway. That gap — word vs. army — IS the subject.
8. **The death is dignified, not gory.** Bared neck, the hands and head on the Rostra (meaningful, not bloody). NO Fulvia hairpin. Register discipline as on the Rome-essay crisis pages.
9. **Dates as given are solid** (cross-checked), but where a source is a single ancient anecdote (Archimedes' tomb, Fulvia) treat as color/legend, not load-bearing fact.
10. **Mark Antony / Octavian framing:** Octavian is the *future Augustus* the two Rome essays end on — flag the tie-in, but keep him the cold young heir here, not yet emperor.

## 9. Cast (for ref planning) & connections

**Cicero across 3 age phases:** rising advocate (~26–36, clean-jawed, dark hair), consul (63 BC, ~43, the public orator in toga), old proscribed man (43 BC, 63, grey, worn). Roman male, clean-shaven throughout (no beard — late-Republican fashion), expressive face, the toga as his "weapon/uniform."

**Secondary named cast** (all handled by Strategy 1, ≤2 named faces/page, no composite anticipated):
- **Terentia** (wife), **Tullia** (beloved daughter), **Quintus** (brother), **Atticus** (friend/correspondent).
- **Catiline** (dangerous patrician), **Hortensius** (the orator he displaced), **Caesar**, **Pompey**, **Cato** (the rigid moralist), **Clodius** (the enemy tribune), **Mark Antony** (the warlord), **young Octavian** (cold heir).

**Connections to flag in the book:**
- Direct sequel to the two Rome essays (`why-rome-won/`, `rome-became-world/`): Cicero is the **human face of the Republic→Empire hinge** those essays describe from above — esp. *How Rome Became the World* P3 (armies loyal to generals → Caesar → Augustus). The essays watch the system; this book stands inside one man as it falls on him.
- The collection's existing `marcus/` (Marcus Aurelius, Stoic emperor, fiction) is the imperial/Stoic counterpoint; Cicero is the **Republican / oratory** counterpart — a new kind of mind for the bios (all prior bios are scientists/inventors/mathematicians).
