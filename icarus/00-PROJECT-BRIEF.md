## One-sentence window

The greatest craftsman in the world builds wings to escape a tyrant's prison, warns his son to fly the middle way — and watches him climb too close to the sun.

## Source of record

Ovid, *Metamorphoses* Book VIII (the Daedalus & Icarus + Labyrinth material), with Apollodorus for the Theseus/Ariadne thread. This is mythology — there are no real dates or quotes to verify; the discipline is staying faithful to the canonical shape of the story, not inventing beats.

## The engine (why this myth, why these kids)

Appetite-and-consequence — the vein the boys pull hardest toward (Prometheus, Midas, Persephone). Icarus is the purest form: a boy given the impossible gift of flight, warned plainly, undone by his own joy. The tragedy is not stupidity — it's *delight*. That's what makes it land instead of moralize.

Two spines run together:
- **Icarus's spine:** the intoxication of a new power, and the single warning ignored.
- **Daedalus's spine:** the father who could build anything — wings, a labyrinth, an escape from an island no ship could leave — and could not build a son who would listen. The man whose cleverness freed him and killed what he loved most.

The book belongs to Icarus (title, cover, the fall), but Daedalus carries the grief, and the last page is his.

## The middle way

Daedalus's warning is the intellectual heart, and it's genuinely interesting, not just a scold: **too low, the sea's spray soaks the feathers and drags you down; too high, the sun softens the wax and the feathers fall out.** Safety is a *narrow band between two real dangers* — a physical idea a kid can hold. Fly the middle. Icarus forgets the middle exists.

## Deliberate editorial choices

- **Open in Crete, not Athens.** Daedalus's backstory (the murder of his nephew Perdix, his exile) is omitted — it muddies the father we need on the last page and costs pages the arc doesn't need. One caption can nod to "a long way from home."
- **The Labyrinth and Minotaur are kept** (P2–P3): they're the most visually thrilling part for kids, they explain *why* Daedalus is imprisoned, and they show his cleverness before it's turned to wings.
- **Pasiphaë / the wooden cow is omitted** (adult, and not needed). The Minotaur simply exists and must be caged.
- **The fall is never a falling body.** Per narrative-mode moderation: the fall is rendered as flight failing — wings coming apart, feathers scattering on the wind, silhouette and aftermath (feathers on empty water). Never a body plummeting to its death.
- **The Icarian Sea is named** on the grief page — the real payoff of the myth is that the water still carries his name.

## Cast

- **Daedalus** — the craftsman/inventor, ~50s, grey-bearded. Protagonist's father; carries the grief.
- **Icarus** — the boy, ~12, dark curly hair. Title character.
- **King Minos** — tyrant of Crete, the jailer. Pages 1–4.
- **The Minotaur** — bull-headed monster in the Labyrinth. Pages 2–3.
- **Theseus & Ariadne** — the hero and the princess, single appearance (P3), handled in prose (no ref sheet).
