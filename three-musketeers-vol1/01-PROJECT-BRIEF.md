# The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, ~49 pages.
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** *Les Trois Mousquetaires*, Alexandre Dumas, 1844, chapters 1–22
(the arrival in Paris through the ball of the diamond studs). Consulted in the
Richard Pevear translation and the original French as evidence of incident,
never for wording.

---

## The engine

**A boy with nothing but nerve and a yellow horse picks a fight with three
men in one afternoon, and by nightfall they are the only family he has — and
the four of them together can outrun the most powerful man in France.**

People still tell this story for two reasons that are really one. The first
is the pleasure of a boy who has never once backed down walking into a city
that does not know him. The second is that the three men he was going to
fight turn out to be the three men who will stop, one after another, to hold
a door so that he can go on. The swordplay is the noise. The friendship is
the story. Both are protected.

**If the protagonist reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a
stretch, the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.
d'Artagnan is never calm. He is eighteen, he is from Gascony, and everything
that happens to him happens because he could not wait.

## The spine

A boy rides to Paris with a letter that will get him into the King's
Musketeers, loses it to a man with a scar before he arrives, and, chasing
that man through the streets, manages to challenge three Musketeers to three
duels in one afternoon. The duels never happen: the Cardinal's guards
interrupt, and the boy picks a side. Now he has three friends, an attic
room, and a girl downstairs who dresses the Queen. The girl carries a secret
the Cardinal wants, and one night she asks the boy to ride to London with a
letter, because the Queen gave the King's diamonds to an English duke and
must wear them at Monday's ball or be finished. Four leave. Three stop, one
by one, to hold the road. One arrives, finds two diamonds already stolen by
the man with the scar, and a duke who has them remade in two days. The boy
rides home, the Queen wears twelve, and the Cardinal counts. The last page
lands on a fork the reader can feel: a boy who came to Paris with a letter
now has three friends, a girl, and a queen's ring — and the most powerful man
in France has just read his name off the letter he lost on page four.

## What this volume is

- A complete story: the road to Paris, the friendship, the ride to London
  and back, the ball. No cliffhanger the reader must resolve elsewhere.
- A friendship book with a sword in every movement. The four are the
  spectacle; every set piece is either the four fighting together or one of
  them stopping so the others can go on.
- A chase book. Somebody is always riding, and the reader always knows who is
  ahead.
- A book whose villain never draws a sword and never raises his voice, and is
  more frightening for it.
- Funny. Porthos's baldric, Aramis's handkerchief, the yellow horse, and a
  boy who challenges three men to duels before lunch are jokes that land in
  pictures.

## What this volume is not

- Not a court drama. No King with lines, no Tréville, no antechambers, no
  explanation of who outranks whom. Blue cloaks and red cloaks hate each
  other; that is the politics.
- Not a romance. Constance and d'Artagnan are a held hand and a window. The
  Queen and the Duke are a secret felt in one line and never dramatized.
- Not Milady's book. She does not appear, is not named, and is not
  foreshadowed. The Cardinal's hand in this volume is Rochefort's, and the
  reader never has to learn a second enemy.
- Not a war comic and not a body count. Nobody bleeds, nobody dies.
- Not a lecture on seventeenth-century France.

**Collapses.** Milady's whole function in this arc — the theft of the two
studs in London — **merges into Rochefort**, so the Cardinal has one hand and
the reader hates one face. De Wardes (the pass at Calais) and Jussac (the
guards' leader at the Carmelites) **merge into Rochefort** as well: every time
d'Artagnan meets the Cardinal's sword, it is the same sword. Tréville's one
necessary function (the place d'Artagnan meets the three) becomes a shut gate
and a street; the letter that was addressed to him is addressed to "the
Captain of the King's Musketeers," who is never seen. The King's one necessary
function (demanding the studs at the ball) is done by the Cardinal, who asked
him to; the King is a crowned figure on a dais, no face, no balloon. Bonacieux
is cut entirely: Constance is unmarried and the house is hers to keep.

**Cut to nobody**: Milady, Tréville, the King (scenery), the Queen (scenery:
a figure seen across the ballroom wearing twelve diamonds, never heard),
Bonacieux, Planchet, Grimaud, Mousqueton, Bazin, de Wardes, Jussac, Cahusac,
Biscarat, Lord de Winter, the jeweler O'Reilly (scenery at his bench), the
innkeepers (scenery with no names), the Cardinal's guards (scenery: red
cloaks, no faces, no balloons; Rochefort speaks for them).

**Source names in chapters 1–22:** roughly forty. **Locked here: eight.**

## The audience promise

**What these readers already enjoy.** A hero who is the youngest in the room
and the first to draw. Three older friends who are each the best at
something. A villain who is always calm. A quest with a clock on it. A secret
that will ruin a queen. A man with a scar you keep meeting. Sword fights that
are funny and thrilling at once. Horses. A ball with a thousand candles and
one man counting.

**The repeatable pleasures.** d'Artagnan getting into a fight he should not
be in, and being right to. Each of the three peeling off to hold a door:
Porthos at the inn, Aramis at the bridge, Athos at the cellar, each with one
line. Rochefort and the boy: Meung, the Carmelites, the alley, Calais, the
ball. The running jokes: the yellow horse, the baldric, the handkerchief,
Aramis about to join the Church. Athos saying "good" when things are bad.

**The emotional promise.** The reader knows what it is to arrive somewhere
nobody knows you and to want, more than anything, to be let in. d'Artagnan
gets in the only way that works: by being the one who does not leave when
the others are outnumbered. And the reader gets to watch three grown men
decide, without discussing it, that a boy is one of them.

**The central dramatic question.** *Can four people who met on Tuesday beat
a man who owns every road in France?* The book's answer is yes, once, and he
now knows their names.

**Complexity budget.** Eight names, and two of them are titles (the Cardinal,
the Duke). Two countries, three cities: Paris, Calais, London. One clock: the
ball is Monday. Zero assumed knowledge: no reader needs to know who Richelieu
was, what a Musketeer is, or why France and England matter; each is a person
or a place with a want when it arrives.

**What this project refuses to become.** A history lesson. A court intrigue
with more doors than swords. A book in which the four sound alike. A book
with a wound in it. A setup for Milady.

## Length

Working count **49** (46 at greenlight; the script gate added the handoff page, split the ask from the news, and split the count from the name), reached by cutting: twenty-two source chapters reduced
to seven movements; forty names to eight; Tréville, the King, Bonacieux, and
Milady cut to nobody or to scenery. The owner asked for the mid-forties on
2026-09-07. The count follows the script and will not shrink by compression.
If it grows past the high forties, the arc boundary for a split is after
Movement IV (the oath and the departure), and the owner decides.

## The reader

A first-time reader who will never read the source. They must never have to
solve the adaptation, supply a missing causal step, or recognize a character
the book has not introduced. "Recoverable," "eventually clear," and
"understandable after looking back" are revision flags, not passing grades.
