# Volume Architecture — 49 pages

France and England, 1625, in one spring. Three cities: Paris, where the four
meet; Calais, where the road ends at the water; London, where the diamonds
are. One clock: the ball is Monday.

---

## I — THE YELLOW HORSE · pp 1–7

**Emotional entry:** hunger. **Exit:** a boy running down a Paris street
after a man with a scar, and a shoulder in his way.

A boy rides out of Gascony on a yellow horse that everybody laughs at, with
his father's sword, his father's letter to the Captain of the King's
Musketeers, and his father's one piece of advice: never refuse a fight. At
the inn at Meung a gentleman with a scar across his face laughs at the horse
from a window, and the boy draws on him in the yard, and the inn's men club
him from behind. When he wakes, the letter is gone: the gentleman read the
address, said "The Musketeers. Of course," and pocketed it, and rode for
Paris. So does the boy. Paris is bigger than the sky. At the Musketeers' gate
he has no letter, no name anyone knows, and no Captain who will see him, and
the gate shuts. Across the street, on a black horse, is the scar.

**The turn:** the boy who left home with a letter arrives with nothing but a
grudge, and the grudge is enough.
**Page-turn out:** he is running, and there is a man in his way.

## II — THREE DUELS AT NOON · pp 8–16

**Emotional entry:** fury. **Exit:** four men walking home abreast, and the
boy's name said by all three.

Running after the scar he collides with a Musketeer's bandaged shoulder;
the man, Athos, is white with pain and very quiet: noon, behind the
Carmelite convent. He runs on and tangles in another Musketeer's cloak, and
the cloak comes off a baldric that is gold in front and plain leather
behind, and the enormous man, Porthos, would kill him for having seen it:
one o'clock, same place. He runs on and picks up a lady's handkerchief a
third Musketeer is standing on and pretending not to own, and the elegant
man, Aramis, is icily grateful: two o'clock. The scar is gone. He sits on a
bench for an hour with three duels and no friend in the world. At noon Athos
is at the field, and then Porthos and Aramis arrive as his seconds, and the
three of them work out that this boy has challenged all of them, and they
laugh, and the boy does not. Then five red cloaks come around the wall of
the convent, the Cardinal's guards, led by the scar himself, who says "The
Gascon," and the fight is three against five and one of the three is
one-armed. The boy says he does not have the uniform but he has the heart,
and picks a side. It is the best fight of his life. Porthos puts a guard in
the trough. Aramis pins a hat to the wall. Athos fights one-handed and does
not lose. The boy gets the scar in front of him at last, and the scar, when
the guards break, retreats: "Twice we've met, boy." "Third time you stay."
Athos asks his name. They walk home four abreast.

**The turn:** the three men he was going to fight are the three men who fight
beside him.
**Page-turn out:** where does a boy with three Musketeers and no money sleep?

## III — THE GIRL DOWNSTAIRS · pp 17–22

**Emotional entry:** warmth. **Exit:** a cat on a red lap, and an order to
bring back two diamonds, only two.

The boy takes the attic in a house in the Rue des Fossoyeurs, and the girl
who lets it to him, Constance, dresses the Queen every morning and comes
home every night with a basket and a key, and he is finished the moment she
looks up the stairs. Weeks: fencing with Athos at dawn, Porthos's stories,
Aramis's Latin, the four inseparable, and Constance on the stair. One night
men in the street take Constance, and the boy goes out of the attic window,
and it is one boy against four men in the rain with a lantern, and he wins.
Rochefort is watching from the shadows and does not come in: "Twice." Constance
cannot tell him why they want her. He says then don't; he'll be upstairs.
In a red room with one candle, a cat, and a stolen letter on the desk, the
Cardinal listens to Rochefort's failure and does not care about the girl. He
already knows what she carries. The Queen gave the Duke of Buckingham twelve
diamond studs, the King's gift. Go to London. Bring me two. Only two. "And
then?" "And then the King will give a ball."

**The turn:** the Cardinal is not after the girl. He is after the Queen, and
he has already moved.
**Page-turn out:** what does a cardinal want with two diamonds?

## IV — ALL FOR ONE · pp 23–27

**Emotional entry:** dread. **Exit:** four riders through the Saint-Denis
gate at dawn, and a girl at a window.

Constance comes up the attic stair white as paper. The King gives a ball on
Monday and the Queen is to wear the twelve diamonds, and the Cardinal is the
one who asked him for the ball, and the diamonds are in London on the Duke
of Buckingham's shoulder. If she cannot wear them, the whole court will know
she gave them away. Someone has to ride to London with a letter. The boy
says: me. She says it is four days each way and the Cardinal owns every inn
on the road. He says he has three friends. In Athos's bare room the four
decide in a page. Athos asks whether they know what the letter says. No.
Good; then nobody can make them tell. Porthos has never beaten an
Englishman. Aramis observes that four are more than one and the Cardinal
will not let four through, and Athos says that is why it takes four: so that
one arrives. They put their swords together and say the thing. At dawn
Constance sews the letter into the boy's coat and does not say come back,
and the four ride out of Paris.

**The turn:** four men leave for London, and only one has to arrive.
**Page-turn out:** the Cardinal owns every inn on the road.

## V — THE ROAD · pp 28–35

**Emotional entry:** speed. **Exit:** a boy on a boat's rail watching the
scar climb out of the harbor with two diamonds in his hand.

Chantilly: a stranger at the inn raises his cup to the Cardinal and invites
Porthos to do the same, and Porthos drinks to the Queen, and stays: go, he
will be an hour. Beauvais: men mending the road are not mending the road;
Aramis's horse goes down at the bridge and Aramis puts his back to the rail
with six in front of him: go, he'll hold the road. Amiens: the innkeeper
says the boy's coin is false and there are red cloaks in the yard, and Athos
backs down the cellar steps with a sword in one hand and a barrel behind
him: ride, d'Artagnan, I have the wine and I have the door. The boy rides
alone through the night counting friends he left behind and comes over the
hill to the sea. Calais: the harbor is closed by the Cardinal's order, and
only a bearer of the Cardinal's pass may sail, and a rider is coming off the
boat from England with a pouch in his coat and a scar on his face. "Third
time, boy." The duel on the quay, on the gangplank, on the ropes, and it
ends with Rochefort in the harbor and the pass in the boy's hand. As the
boat pulls out the boy sees the scar climb the harbor stairs and open his
fist to check what he still has, and two diamonds catch the light. The boy
does not know what they are. The reader does.

**The turn:** each friend stops to hold a door, and the boy goes on alone.
**Page-turn out:** the scar had two diamonds in his hand.

## VI — LONDON · pp 36–40

**Emotional entry:** awe. **Exit:** a box with twelve diamonds in it closing
in a boy's hands, and a duke who could not finish his sentence.

London is a river of masts and a white palace, and a boy with mud to his
waist at its gate, and the richest man in England comes down the stairs
himself when he sees the seal on the letter. Buckingham reads it and goes
white and opens a casket he keeps in the room where he sleeps: ten. Two are
gone. A masked man at his ball a week ago, with a scar under the mask. The
boy says he fought him this morning at Calais and he was carrying them to
the Cardinal. Buckingham understands the trap in one breath: she will wear
ten, and the Cardinal will give her the other two in front of the court.
Then she will wear twelve. He sends for his jeweler. Two days for the
diamonds, two for the road; the boy will sleep on the boat. The jeweler's
fire, two days, a duke who does not sit down. He puts the box in the boy's
hands and starts to say "Tell her" and does not finish.

**The turn:** two diamonds are missing, and a duke decides to make them.
**Page-turn out:** the ball is Monday, and it is Saturday.

## VII — TWELVE · pp 41–49

**Emotional entry:** held breath. **Exit:** four men walking away down a
Paris street, hats on, and a red figure at a window above them.

At the Louvre's back stair the boy puts the casket into Constance's hands
and she counts to twelve; she has ten minutes. Then the ballroom on Monday night, a thousand candles. The Cardinal has the pouch in
his sleeve and Rochefort behind him with his arm in a sling, and Constance in
the gallery watching the doors, and the Cardinal tells Rochefort, quietly,
what happens next: she will wear ten, and he will give her the other two,
and the King will count. The Queen enters. Twelve. The Cardinal counts. He
counts again. Rochefort swears he cut two. The Cardinal says then someone
made two, and turns to look at the doors, and in the last one is a boy with
mud to his waist. "Who is that?" "The Gascon. From Meung." The Cardinal takes
a folded letter from his sleeve, the one from page four, and reads the name
at the bottom. "d'Artagnan." In the gallery Constance gives the boy a ring
from the Queen's own hand: she counted to twelve too. That night the three
come home to the attic one by one: Porthos, who won and then ate the inn;
Aramis, limping, who held the bridge; Athos, who smells of a hundred barrels
and says "Did you arrive?" and is told "I arrived," and says "Good." In the
morning the four eat breakfast on the grass behind the Carmelites, where
they first met, with the yellow horse grazing. The last page is the four
walking away, and above them, in a window, a man in red.

**The turn:** the Cardinal counts to twelve and reads the boy's name off the
letter he stole.
**Page-turn out:** (none: the book ends.) The promise is that the man in red
now knows who they are.

Spectacle runs above the ~13% target on purpose: the owner asked for the
swordplay loud, and every spectacle page here is a fight, an arrival, an
oath, or the Queen walking in. It relaxes no rule; the target is a balance,
not a ceiling. The script gate should still ask of each spectacle page
whether a dramatic page would do more.

**No movement runs more than five dramatic pages without a prose or spectacle
page.** Longest runs: 7–10 (four), 21–25 (five), 44–47 (four). The prose and spectacle
pages are what buy the dramatic pages their room; they cannot be cut for
budget.

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
The loss of the letter (p. 4) gets its own waking page (p. 5) before Paris.
The three collisions are one page each. The fight (p. 14) and the scar's
retreat (p. 15) are separate from the walk home (p. 16). The news (p. 23), the ask (p. 24), the four's decision (p. 25) and the oath (p. 26) are four pages, not one. Each friend's door is its own page. The handoff (p. 40) is its own page before the ball. The count (p. 44) gets its consequence in the room before the name (p. 45); the name is not the
ring (p. 46), the ring is not the homecoming (p. 47), and the homecoming
gets its morning (p. 48).

**One dominant turn per page.** Every row above names the one thing that
changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence comes back here,
not to the prompt.
