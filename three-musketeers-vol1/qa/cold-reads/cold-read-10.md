# Cold read — pages 1–10

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands without outside documentation. At ordinary reading pace I understood who the lead is, what he wants, what was stolen from him, why he cannot complete his errand, and how his pursuit of the thief accidentally commits him to three duels.

## Retell of the story so far

In Gascony in April 1625, eighteen-year-old d'Artagnan leaves home for Paris. His father gives him an old yellow horse, a sword that has been in the family, and a sealed letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers, who was once his father's friend. His father's last advice is never to refuse a fight and to place the letter directly in the Captain's hand.

After eleven days on the road, d'Artagnan reaches an inn or village where several men ridicule his conspicuous old horse. He challenges the chief mocker, a black-clad man with a red feather, but the man stays above him and treats him as a child. D'Artagnan charges into the inn to reach him. One of the inn workers knocks d'Artagnan unconscious with a stool or chair. While he lies helpless, the black-clad man finds the sealed letter, recognizes that it is addressed to the Musketeers, takes it, and orders the workers to put d'Artagnan in the straw.

D'Artagnan wakes in the stable and discovers the letter is missing. The black-clad man has already ridden toward Paris with it, so d'Artagnan immediately follows. When he reaches Paris, the size and indifference of the city overwhelm him: hundreds of people are present, but nobody looks up.

At what appears to be a Musketeers' compound, d'Artagnan tells a guard that he has come to see the Captain, that his father knew him, and that his letter was stolen. Without the letter or even a name the guard recognizes, d'Artagnan is refused entry. Then he spots the thief—the man from Meung—riding through the street and runs after him.

The chase causes one collision after another. First d'Artagnan bangs into the injured shoulder of a tall Musketeer. The man insists that d'Artagnan has now stopped and must give satisfaction; they arrange a duel for noon behind the Carmelite convent. Next d'Artagnan becomes tangled in or falls against a large Musketeer's cloak and exposes the fact that the man's ornate baldric is gold only on the visible front. The humiliated man challenges him for one o'clock at the same place. From this exchange I learned that the first duelist is Athos; the large man says he will take whatever Athos leaves of d'Artagnan.

Finally, d'Artagnan picks up a lace handkerchief beside a blond Musketeer and politely says that the man dropped his lady's handkerchief. The blond man denies owning it and is angered because the remark embarrasses him in front of his friends. He too challenges d'Artagnan. D'Artagnan answers that noon and one are already taken but two o'clock is free. By the end of page 10, his failed pursuit has left him with three back-to-back duels.

## Cast

- **D'Artagnan:** Eighteen, slim and youthful, with unruly black hair, a battered broad-brimmed gray-brown hat and feather, a mustard-brown coat, boots, and a sword. He is the protagonist, leaving home to seek the King's Musketeers. He is proud, literal about insults, quick to draw his sword, and too intent on the thief to avoid giving offense.
- **D'Artagnan's father:** An older, weathered man with gray hair and beard, dressed in worn brown country clothes. He sends his son toward Paris with the horse, sword, letter, and a code of courage.
- **The old yellow horse:** Pale yellow or cream, shaggy and visibly old. It is d'Artagnan's inherited mount and the object of the first insult.
- **The man from Meung / the thief:** A mature, dark-haired man with a curled mustache, black clothes and cloak, a large black hat with a bright red feather, and later a black horse. He mocks d'Artagnan, steals the letter while the boy is unconscious, and becomes the immediate antagonist d'Artagnan pursues into Paris.
- **The inn or stable workers:** Large, rough men in shirts and leather aprons. They laugh at d'Artagnan and his horse; one knocks him unconscious, and they obey the black-clad man's order to move him into the straw.
- **The compound guard:** A helmeted man in dark armor stationed at the gate. He refuses d'Artagnan entry when the boy cannot produce the stolen letter or a useful name.
- **Athos:** A tall, lean Musketeer with shoulder-length dark brown hair, mustache and pointed beard, a black hat with a white feather, a blue cross-marked cloak or tabard, and an already injured shoulder/arm. He is the first man d'Artagnan offends in Paris and schedules the noon duel.
- **The large Musketeer:** A broad, heavy man with abundant curled reddish-brown hair and mustache, red sleeves, a blue Musketeer garment, a decorated baldric, and a black hat with a red feather. He boasts loudly and is acutely sensitive about the baldric being decorated only in front. He schedules the one-o'clock duel. His name is not stated on these pages.
- **The blond Musketeer:** A fine-featured young man with long blond curls, a black hat with a white feather, a blue Musketeer garment, and an elaborate white lace collar. He is embarrassed when d'Artagnan publicly associates him with a lady's lace handkerchief and schedules the two-o'clock duel. His name is not stated on these pages.
- **Other Musketeers and Parisians:** Men in matching blue, cross-marked garments occupy the compound and the street, while the dense city crowd mostly ignores d'Artagnan. Two Musketeers witness the handkerchief exchange.

## Causal chain

D'Artagnan's father gives him a letter of introduction -> the horse attracts mockery -> d'Artagnan follows his father's injunction never to refuse a fight -> an inn worker knocks him unconscious -> the black-clad man steals the letter and rides toward Paris -> d'Artagnan follows him -> the missing letter and missing useful name prevent d'Artagnan from seeing the Captain -> d'Artagnan spots the thief outside -> he gives chase on foot -> he strikes Athos's injured shoulder and accepts a noon duel -> the continued rush exposes the large Musketeer's false-front baldric and produces a one-o'clock duel -> the next encounter publicly links the blond Musketeer to a lady's handkerchief and produces a two-o'clock duel.

## Backtracking, rereading, or guesses

No page required backtracking or rereading.

On page 7, the narration "No letter. No name anyone knew. No Captain." is compressed enough that "No Captain" could mean either that the Captain is unavailable or that, as far as the gatekeeper is concerned, d'Artagnan has not identified any Captain he can be sent to. I took the whole passage to mean that d'Artagnan cannot establish a valid person or purpose at the gate. This was a momentary interpretive guess, but it did not obscure the story event: the door is closed to him because he lacks the stolen introduction and adequate identifying information.

On page 9, I inferred that the large Musketeer's name has not yet been given. He names Athos as the tall man with the bad shoulder, but nobody addresses the large man by name. On page 10, I likewise did not assign a name to the blond Musketeer.

I did not misidentify any character and did not need later pages to correct an earlier identification.

## Text or attribution failures at reading pace

None. Every speech balloon could be assigned to a visible speaker from its placement, tail, and conversational response. The prose was readable at pace. The page 7 wording noted above was the only syntactically abrupt passage, and its practical meaning was still clear.

## What I expect next

I expect d'Artagnan to reach the field behind the Carmelite convent at noon and discover that the three men he has challenged are connected—probably the central Musketeers promised by the title. The three separate duels may be interrupted by a common enemy or authority, forcing d'Artagnan to fight alongside them rather than successively against them. That shared fight would plausibly earn their respect and give him a new route into the Musketeers despite the stolen letter. I also expect the black-clad thief and the letter to remain part of the larger plot rather than being resolved immediately.
