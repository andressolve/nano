# Page intents

Reader-facing goals, not image-generation specifications. Shown to both
builder and critic. Exact text and staging stay in the script and the prompt.

Format — one section per page, header exactly `

## PAGE 1

Spectacle. A white road between ochre hills under a hot April sky, and riding toward the reader, filling the lower half of the page, a boy of eighteen on an old buttercup-yellow horse: a grey hat with a broken feather, an ochre doublet, and a sword far too long for him. Chin up, entirely pleased with himself. Far behind, small, a farmhouse and a gate. His shadow runs ahead of him down the road. The title card sits over the sky; one caption names Gascony and the year. The dominant turn is simply this: a road out of Gascony, a boy, a yellow horse, a sword too long for him. d'Artagnan owns the page and the horse shares it. The reader turns because a boy this pleased with himself on a horse this yellow is going somewhere. The landscape is scenery; the horse's colour and the boy's chin are the page.

## PAGE 2

Illustrated prose, two locations because the turn is the leaving: the farm gate at dawn, then the road north over days. The dominant image is the gate: a grey-haired father in a worn coat with one hand flat on the yellow horse's neck, looking up at the boy in the saddle, and in his other hand a folded cream letter with a red wax seal. The boy looks down at him. The smaller image is the road: the yellow horse going up a long hill in rain and coming down the far side in sun, the boy's hat pulled low. The prose field carries the three gifts and the one rule, and names the boy for the first time. The dominant turn is the three gifts and the one piece of advice, then the road north. The father owns the moment; the boy receives it. The reader turns because the boy has a letter, a sword, a horse, and a rule about fights, and Paris is full of fights. The farm stays subordinate to the two hands: the father's on the horse, the letter in the other.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. The inn yard at Meung: packed dirt, a well, a stone trough, a stable door, and above the yard a first-floor window with the shutters open. d'Artagnan swings down from the yellow horse at the trough. Leaning on the sill above him, a man in black with a red feather and a pale scar from temple to mouth laughs down at the horse, and two of the inn's men laugh with him. The boy turns, hand on hilt, and invites the man to come down and laugh at him; the man does not fight children, or anyone before dinner; the boy draws and heads for the inn door, and behind him the two men pick up a stool and a broom handle. The dominant turn is the scar laughing at the horse and the boy drawing on him. Rochefort owns the height; d'Artagnan owns the page. The reader turns because the man is not coming down, so the boy is going up. The yard is scenery; the window above and the drawn sword below are the page.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. The same yard. d'Artagnan at the foot of the inn stairs with his sword up, shouting up the stairwell, does not see the two inn men behind him, one with a stool raised. The stool comes down across the back of his head with one sound, and he goes forward on his face, the sword skidding. Then the man with the scar has come down after all: he crouches by the boy, who is out cold face down in the dirt, and draws the folded cream letter with its red seal out of the inside of the boy's coat. Standing, he reads the address to the light, says the Musketeers, of course, and opens his own black coat to put it away, telling the men to put the boy in the straw. The dominant turn is the scar reading the address and pocketing the letter. Rochefort owns the page; the boy is on the ground for all of it. The reader turns because the man with the scar has the letter and the boy does not know it yet. The knock is a knock, not a wound: no blood, ever.

Dramatic. Dramatic. ## PAGE 8

Dramatic. A Paris street corner. d'Artagnan, at a dead run after the scar, goes straight into the sling of a very tall Musketeer, and the tall man goes white. This is Athos: very tall, very still, grey eyes, his good hand holding his bad shoulder, while the boy, already two steps past, looks back over his own shoulder for the man who has vanished. The tall man observes that the shoulder was hurt before the boy arrived; the boy apologises and says he cannot stop; the tall man says he has stopped. Face to face, the boy offers satisfaction and never refuses a fight; the tall man names the field behind the Carmelite convent at noon and tells him to bring a friend to stand with him; the boy has none. The dominant turn is Athos's shoulder and the appointment at noon. Athos owns the stillness, d'Artagnan the heat. The reader turns because the boy has a duel at noon with the tallest man in Paris and has lost the scar. The street is scenery; the sling and the two faces are the page.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic. A second corner. Porthos, enormous, red-brown curls and a waxed moustache, fills the street telling two blue cloaks a story about the Cardinal with his own cloak thrown back to show a baldric embroidered in gold; the boy, ducking past at a run, goes straight into the cloak. On the ground, tangled in it, he sees what the whole street now sees: the back of the baldric is plain buff leather, no gold at all. The boy says so. Porthos, scarlet, fists on hips, will not have the street told about the back of his baldric; the boy offers one o'clock behind the Carmelites since noon is taken; Porthos asks who has him at noon; a tall man with a bad shoulder; Athos! Porthos will take whatever he leaves. The dominant turn is the cloak coming off the baldric. Porthos owns the page; the boy is on the ground for most of it. The reader turns because that is two duels an hour apart and the boy is still running. The two listening Musketeers are scenery.

## PAGE 10

Dramatic. A third corner. Aramis, slim, blond, immaculate, stands with two blue cloaks by a wall with one boot planted on a white lace handkerchief bearing a small embroidered monogram. The boy, slowing at last, sees it, stoops, and pulls it out from under the boot with the observation that it is a lady's. Aramis takes it between two fingers, icy, denying he dropped anything, that it is his, that he has seen it, and that he was standing on it on purpose; the boy asks then why he is taking it. Two fingers at his moustache, perfectly polite, Aramis explains the boy has embarrassed him and will have to be killed at two o'clock; two is free, noon and one are taken; how busy you are. The dominant turn is the handkerchief and the appointment at two. Aramis owns the page's manners; the boy owns its innocence. The reader turns because that is three duels on one field and not one friend in the world. The two listening cloaks are scenery.

Dramatic. The field behind the Carmelite convent at noon: long grass, a high wall of yellow stone, one big tree, a stone trough, and a gap in the wall. ## PAGE 14

Spectacle. The whole fight in one picture, top to bottom: Porthos lifting a red-cloaked guard bodily off the ground and into the trough; Aramis's blade pinning a guard's hat to the yellow wall through the brim, the guard bareheaded and astonished beneath it; Athos, one-handed, stepping aside from a lunge so neatly the guard runs straight into the tree; and in the middle, fast, the boy in ochre with his hair flying, driving a guard backward across the grass with the guard's own sword already lying behind him. Two sound cues and one caption. No blood anywhere, nobody hurt, everybody losing. The dominant turn is the fight itself: trough, hat, one arm, and a boy. The four own the page together. The reader turns because four against five is going the four's way and the scar has not drawn yet. Rochefort is at the edge, sword still sheathed, watching.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. Rochefort's sword is out at last, and the boy turns from his beaten guard to face him: Meung, the inn yard, you laughed at my horse and took my letter; I took a letter, I don't remember a boy. Steel on steel, the boy fast, the man surprised, while behind them the guards are leaving through the gap in the wall, one wet, one hatless, one holding a pulled arm, and Porthos waves them off with his hat. The reader turns because the scar is gone again but this time he had to run. Bloodless: nobody is touched.
