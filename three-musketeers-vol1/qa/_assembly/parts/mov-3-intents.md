## PAGE 17

Dramatic. A narrow street, a narrow house, a door at the foot of a steep wooden stair. Constance on the doorstep in grey and white, a small gold crown on her apron bib, keys at her waist, a basket on her arm, and the boy on the cobbles with the yellow horse and his hat in his hands. He says Athos sent him for the attic; two livres a week and a steep stair, can he pay it; not this week, next week he will be famous; everyone who comes to Paris says that. The stair, from the bottom: she has gone up ahead with the candle and turned to look down, he is at the foot looking up, and the crown on her apron catches the light; he asks about it; she dresses the Queen every morning and lets attics to Gascons who can't pay. The attic: a window, a bed, a table, a nail; he hangs his father's sword on the nail and promises to pay double; Constance, double is four livres, goodnight. The dominant turn is the attic, and the girl who dresses the Queen, and the boy finished the moment she looked down the stair. The reader turns because he is finished and she has already gone downstairs.

## PAGE 18

Illustrated prose, two locations because the weeks are the turn: the Carmelite field at dawn, and the stair. The dominant image is the field: Athos, the sling gone, teaching the boy with blades crossed and his boot nudging the boy's back foot into place, neither of them talking. The smaller image is the stair: Constance at the bottom with her basket and her keys, looking up; the boy at the top, looking down. The prose carries the four inseparable, Porthos's stories, Aramis's Latin, Athos's offer to walk him through the gate and the boy's refusal, and every evening the look up the stair that neither of them mentions. The dominant turn is weeks going by and the girl looking up the stair every evening. The four own the mornings; Constance owns the evenings. The reader turns because every evening she looks up the stair, and this is the last evening on which nothing happens.

## PAGE 19

Dramatic, two locations because the jump is the turn: the attic window, and the street below. Night, rain. Above, the boy at the open window in his shirt, cleaning the sword. Below, a cart, a lantern on a bracket, and four men in plain cloaks closing on Constance at her own door, one hand reaching for her mouth, the basket falling; she shouts for help and gets half his name out. He goes out of the window, not the stair: one hand on the sill, the sword in the other, dropping two floors through the rain onto the cart roof with a bang. He lands between the men and the door, sword out, hair in his eyes, and across the street in a dark doorway a fifth figure in black with a red feather stands with his arms folded and does not move. The dominant turn is the men taking Constance and the boy going out of the window. The reader turns because it is one boy, four men, one lantern, and rain.

## PAGE 20

Spectacle. The fight in the rain in one picture: the boy in his white shirt a blur in the middle; one man already sitting in the puddle; one running; one losing his sword into the air; the lantern knocked off its bracket and falling, still lit; Constance flat against her own door with the basket held in front of her; and across the street, in the doorway, the black figure with the red feather, arms folded, watching. One sound for the lantern, one caption. The dominant turn is one boy against four men, one lantern, and rain, and the lantern going out. The boy owns the page; the watcher in the doorway owns its edge. The reader turns because the lantern is out and the man across the street has not moved. Bloodless: men fall, run, and lose swords; nobody is cut.

## PAGE 21

Dramatic. The men are gone. Rain. The boy, breathing hard, sword lowered; Rochefort stepping out of the doorway into what light there is, hands empty, scar and red feather. Three times you've drawn on me, Gascon, and I'm still not staying; I said the third time you'd stay, draw; not in the rain and not for a seamstress, she isn't what I was sent for, she only carries it. He walks away up the street, unhurried, and the boy shouts after him: carries what? The doorway: Constance shaking, picking up the basket, the boy beside her wet through. What did he mean, what does she carry? The Queen's letters, she carries them for her, where and to whom she can't tell, it isn't hers to tell. Then don't tell me; I'll be upstairs, tonight and every night, until they stop coming. They won't stop. Then neither will I. The dominant turn is Rochefort's one word too many, the letters, and the boy staying anyway. The reader turns because the men were sent for the Queen's letters and the girl will not say where they go.

## PAGE 22

Dramatic. A red room with one candle and no window. The Cardinal in a chair: a thin man in a red robe and red skullcap, a short pointed grey beard, a grey cat on his lap, one long hand stroking it; on the black desk before him a folded cream letter with a broken red seal, the letter from Meung. Rochefort standing, wet from the rain, hat in his hands. A caption names the Cardinal and says the scar and every red cloak answer to him. The girl got away, a boy stopped them, the Gascon; the Cardinal has the father's letter, does not need tonight's, has read the Queen's letters for a year and knows what she sent: twelve diamond studs, the King's gift, on the Duke of Buckingham's shoulder; go to London, bring two, only two. Why not all twelve? He would miss twelve; nobody counts to two. And then? And then the King will give a ball. The dominant turn is the order: two diamonds, only two, and a ball. The Cardinal owns the page without raising his voice. The reader turns because he knows what a ball has to do with it and you do not yet. This is the heaviest page in the book: nine strings; lanes before faces.
