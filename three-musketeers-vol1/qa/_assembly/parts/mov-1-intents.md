## PAGE 1

Spectacle. A white road between ochre hills under a hot April sky, and riding toward the reader, filling the lower half of the page, a boy of eighteen on an old buttercup-yellow horse: a grey hat with a broken feather, an ochre doublet, and a sword far too long for him. Chin up, entirely pleased with himself. Far behind, small, a farmhouse and a gate. His shadow runs ahead of him down the road. The title card sits over the sky; one caption names Gascony and the year. The dominant turn is simply this: a road out of Gascony, a boy, a yellow horse, a sword too long for him. d'Artagnan owns the page and the horse shares it. The reader turns because a boy this pleased with himself on a horse this yellow is going somewhere. The landscape is scenery; the horse's colour and the boy's chin are the page.

## PAGE 2

Illustrated prose, two locations because the turn is the leaving: the farm gate at dawn, then the road north over days. The dominant image is the gate: a grey-haired father in a worn coat with one hand flat on the yellow horse's neck, looking up at the boy in the saddle, and in his other hand a folded cream letter with a red wax seal. The boy looks down at him. The smaller image is the road: the yellow horse going up a long hill in rain and coming down the far side in sun, the boy's hat pulled low. The prose field carries the three gifts and the one rule, and names the boy for the first time. The dominant turn is the three gifts and the one piece of advice, then the road north. The father owns the moment; the boy receives it. The reader turns because the boy has a letter, a sword, a horse, and a rule about fights, and Paris is full of fights. The farm stays subordinate to the two hands: the father's on the horse, the letter in the other.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. The inn yard at Meung: packed dirt, a well, a stone trough, a stable door, and above the yard a first-floor window with the shutters open. d'Artagnan swings down from the yellow horse at the trough. Leaning on the sill above him, a man in black with a red feather and a pale scar from temple to mouth laughs down at the horse, and two of the inn's men laugh with him. The boy turns, hand on hilt, and invites the man to come down and laugh at him; the man does not fight children, or anyone before dinner; the boy draws and heads for the inn door, and behind him the two men pick up a stool and a broom handle. The dominant turn is the scar laughing at the horse and the boy drawing on him. Rochefort owns the height; d'Artagnan owns the page. The reader turns because the man is not coming down, so the boy is going up. The yard is scenery; the window above and the drawn sword below are the page.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. The same yard. d'Artagnan at the foot of the inn stairs with his sword up, shouting up the stairwell, does not see the two inn men behind him, one with a stool raised. The stool comes down across the back of his head with one sound, and he goes forward on his face, the sword skidding. Then the man with the scar has come down after all: he crouches by the boy, who is out cold face down in the dirt, and draws the folded cream letter with its red seal out of the inside of the boy's coat. Standing, he reads the address to the light, says the Musketeers, of course, and opens his own black coat to put it away, telling the men to put the boy in the straw. The dominant turn is the scar reading the address and pocketing the letter. Rochefort owns the page; the boy is on the ground for all of it. The reader turns because the man with the scar has the letter and the boy does not know it yet. The knock is a knock, not a wound: no blood, ever.

## PAGE 5

Dramatic. Evening in the stable at Meung: d'Artagnan sits up in the straw with a hand on the back of his head, his hat beside him, his other hand inside his coat with the inside pocket turned out and empty, the yellow horse looking down at him from the next stall. Then the yard at dusk, empty: the boy standing in the middle of it looking up at the window, whose shutters are closed, the stable door open on one empty stall, nobody laughing now, nobody there at all. A caption says what the boy sees and that the inn watched the black horse go; the boy says the stable boy told him the man rode for Paris at noon, with the letter. Then the boy in the saddle at a gallop out of the gate: then so do I. The dominant turn is the letter gone and the boy riding after it. d'Artagnan owns the page alone. The reader turns because the letter that was going to open Paris is in the pocket of the man he most wants to fight.

## PAGE 6

Spectacle. From the top of the last hill: Paris, grey-blue slate roofs stacked to the horizon, a brown river, stone bridges with houses built on them, spires, smoke, and a road going down into it full of carts and people. In the foreground, small, the boy on the yellow horse with his hat off, staring. One caption. The dominant turn is Paris itself: the size of it, and that nobody looks up. The city owns the page; the boy is a small figure with his hat off. The reader turns because nobody here knows his name, and he has lost the one thing that would have told them. The crowd on the road is scenery: many small figures, no faces, no balloons.

## PAGE 7

Dramatic. A great arched carriage door in a stone wall, open on a courtyard full of blue cloaks, horses, and noise, and in the arch a porter with a halberd who does not move. d'Artagnan on the step, hat in his hands, small, explaining that his father was the Captain's friend and that a man stole the letter. The door closes in his face; behind it, blue cloaks laugh; a caption says the door closed the way doors close on boys. He turns on the step, and across the street, on a black horse, in black with one red feather, is the scar, looking straight at him and smiling. The boy shouts; the man turns the horse into the crowd without hurry, taunting him for being on foot; the boy is already running with a hand on his hilt, and at the edge of the last panel a tall blue-cloaked shoulder comes round the corner into his path. The dominant turn is the gate shutting and the scar across the street. Rochefort owns the taunt; d'Artagnan owns the running. The reader turns because he is running and there is a man in his way.
