Dramatic. A Paris street corner. d'Artagnan, at a dead run after the scar, goes straight into the sling of a very tall Musketeer, and the tall man goes white. This is Athos: very tall, very still, grey eyes, his good hand holding his bad shoulder, while the boy, already two steps past, looks back over his own shoulder for the man who has vanished. The tall man observes that the shoulder was hurt before the boy arrived; the boy apologises and says he cannot stop; the tall man says he has stopped. Face to face, the boy offers satisfaction and never refuses a fight; the tall man names the field behind the Carmelite convent at noon and tells him to bring a friend to stand with him; the boy has none. The dominant turn is Athos's shoulder and the appointment at noon. Athos owns the stillness, d'Artagnan the heat. The reader turns because the boy has a duel at noon with the tallest man in Paris and has lost the scar. The street is scenery; the sling and the two faces are the page.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic. A second corner. Porthos, enormous, red-brown curls and a waxed moustache, fills the street telling two blue cloaks a story about the Cardinal with his own cloak thrown back to show a baldric embroidered in gold; the boy, ducking past at a run, goes straight into the cloak. On the ground, tangled in it, he sees what the whole street now sees: the back of the baldric is plain buff leather, no gold at all. The boy says so. Porthos, scarlet, fists on hips, will not have the street told about the back of his baldric; the boy offers one o'clock behind the Carmelites since noon is taken; Porthos asks who has him at noon; a tall man with a bad shoulder; Athos! Porthos will take whatever he leaves. The dominant turn is the cloak coming off the baldric. Porthos owns the page; the boy is on the ground for most of it. The reader turns because that is two duels an hour apart and the boy is still running. The two listening Musketeers are scenery.

## PAGE 10

Dramatic. A third corner. Aramis, slim, blond, immaculate, stands with two blue cloaks by a wall with one boot planted on a white lace handkerchief bearing a small embroidered monogram. The boy, slowing at last, sees it, stoops, and pulls it out from under the boot with the observation that it is a lady's. Aramis takes it between two fingers, icy, denying he dropped anything, that it is his, that he has seen it, and that he was standing on it on purpose; the boy asks then why he is taking it. Two fingers at his moustache, perfectly polite, Aramis explains the boy has embarrassed him and will have to be killed at two o'clock; two is free, noon and one are taken; how busy you are. The dominant turn is the handkerchief and the appointment at two. Aramis owns the page's manners; the boy owns its innocence. The reader turns because that is three duels on one field and not one friend in the world. The two listening cloaks are scenery.

## PAGE 12

Dramatic. The field behind the Carmelite convent at noon: long grass, a high wall of yellow stone, one big tree, a stone trough, and a gap in the wall. Athos under the tree, arm in the sling, good hand on the pommel of his grounded sword. The boy arrives, hat off; Athos observes that most boys don't come; the boy says he said he would and that Athos's friends aren't here; they are. Porthos and Aramis come through the gap and stop dead: that's my one o'clock; that is my two o'clock; he's my noon. The three look at one another and laugh, Porthos with his head back, Aramis behind his hand, Athos with one corner of his mouth, and the boy does not laugh; and behind all four, coming through the gap, five red cloaks with white crosses and, in front of them, in black, the scar, who forbids duelling, will take their swords, and will take the Gascon himself. The dominant turn is the three working it out and laughing, and the red cloaks coming round the wall. The reader turns because it is five red against three blue, one of them one-armed, and the scar has just claimed the boy.

Dramatic. The field. Athos, Porthos, and Aramis draw in a line facing the five guards and Rochefort; the boy stands off to one side between the two groups with his sword still sheathed. Athos counts three of them, five of those, one of him in a sling, and says it will do; Porthos tells the boy to stand back, this is not his fight, he is not one of them. The boy draws his father's sword and steps into the line beside Athos: he hasn't got the cloak, he has the sword and the heart and he is standing here; four of us. Athos looks down at him properly for the first time and asks his name; d'Artagnan; then, d'Artagnan, the one on the left is yours, don't get hurt, I'd have to explain it. The dominant turn is the boy picking a side. d'Artagnan owns the page; Athos gives him his name back. The reader turns because he has picked the side with fewer swords. The guards and Rochefort wait, silent.

## PAGE 14

Spectacle. The whole fight in one picture, top to bottom: Porthos lifting a red-cloaked guard bodily off the ground and into the trough; Aramis's blade pinning a guard's hat to the yellow wall through the brim, the guard bareheaded and astonished beneath it; Athos, one-handed, stepping aside from a lunge so neatly the guard runs straight into the tree; and in the middle, fast, the boy in ochre with his hair flying, driving a guard backward across the grass with the guard's own sword already lying behind him. Two sound cues and one caption. No blood anywhere, nobody hurt, everybody losing. The dominant turn is the fight itself: trough, hat, one arm, and a boy. The four own the page together. The reader turns because four against five is going the four's way and the scar has not drawn yet. Rochefort is at the edge, sword still sheathed, watching.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. Rochefort's sword is out at last, and the boy turns from his beaten guard to face him: Meung, the inn yard, you laughed at my horse and took my letter; I took a letter, I don't remember a boy. Steel on steel, the boy fast, the man surprised, while behind them the guards are leaving through the gap in the wall, one wet, one hatless, one holding a pulled arm, and Porthos waves them off with his hat. Where did a farm boy learn that? From my father; where did you learn to run? Rochefort backs to the gap with his sword up, alone now: twice you've drawn on me, boy, twice is enough for one week; the third time, you stay. The dominant turn is the scar in front of the boy at last and the guards breaking. d'Artagnan owns the page; Rochefort loses ground on it. The reader turns because the scar is gone again but this time he had to run. Bloodless: nobody is touched.

## PAGE 16

Dramatic. Toward evening, a Paris street: four abreast, filling it, walking back into the city, Porthos with one huge arm round the boy's neck, Aramis dusting his cuffs, Athos on the outside with his good hand on his pommel as he walks, blue cloaks making way. The boy is carried along in the middle, grinning, hair in his eyes. Porthos wants to know if everyone saw him put the guard in the trough; Aramis says all Paris saw it and pride goeth; Athos says pride went into the trough and the boy fought well. A step apart, Athos asks where the boy is sleeping; a horse and no money, so with the horse. Porthos spreads his arms: tonight the boy eats with them and Porthos is paying, well, Aramis is paying; in theory. The dominant turn is four abreast and the boy's name in the three men's mouths. The reader turns because he came to Paris with a letter and nobody to give it to, and is walking home with three friends.
