## PAGE 41

Dramatic. A service door at the back of the Louvre, torchlight, kitchen noise behind it. Constance in the doorway in her grey with the apron off; the boy coming up out of the dark with mud to the waist and the closed casket under his arm. You're here; every night since you left she has stood in this door; is it—; twelve, count them. The casket open between them in torchlight, her lips moving over the double row, his eyes on her face and not on the box: ten, eleven, twelve, twelve; two of them are new, the scar cut two out at the Duke's ball and the Duke had two made and nobody can tell which, not even the Duke could. The box shut against her chest, she is already turning up the stair and he has sat down on the bottom step: she's dressing, she has ten minutes, go round to the front doors and stand where she can see you; where the Cardinal can see me; yes, that too. The dominant turn is the box changing hands and the count to twelve. Constance owns the count; the boy owns the watching. The reader turns because the box has changed hands and there are ten minutes.

## PAGE 42

Dramatic. A hall of gold and a thousand candles, a court in silk, a dais with two thrones and a crowned figure on one; beside the dais one column of red: the Cardinal seated in a chair set there for him, and on the arm of the chair under his hand a folded cream letter with a broken red seal, as if he had been reading it while he waited; behind the chair Rochefort with his right arm in a white sling; above, in the gallery, Constance in grey without the apron, both hands on the rail, watching the long shut doors. And the boy, Eminence, the Gascon reached the boat; a boy reached a boat, he has the father's letter and knows what he is, the Duke has ten, ten is ten. The Cardinal's long hand draws a small pouch half out of his red sleeve and pushes it back: she will come in wearing ten because ten is what she has, and he will go to her with the two she lost in front of everyone and the King will count. The gallery: Constance's hands white on the rail, the doors shut, a herald's staff rising: come on, come on, come on. The dominant turn is the pouch in the sleeve, the sling, and a plan said aloud in the wrong room. The reader turns because the doors are opening and the Cardinal has two diamonds in his sleeve.

## PAGE 43

Spectacle. The Queen enters: a figure in white and silver seen from the gallery over Constance's shoulder, from behind and above, walking the length of the hall through a court that bows like wheat, and on her left shoulder a blaze of diamonds in two rows, drawn large and bright. At the far end, small, the red chair, and the man in it leaning forward. One caption. The dominant turn is the Queen entering wearing twelve. The Queen owns the page without a face. The reader turns because the Cardinal is counting. The diamonds are one bright cluster, never a counting puzzle.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. The Cardinal's face, close, his lips moving, his hand on the pouch in his sleeve stopped: ten, eleven, twelve; twelve. Rochefort bending to his ear, sweating: he cut two, he swears it, they are in the sleeve; then someone made two. Then, from beside the red chair looking down the hall: the Queen has reached the dais and the crowned figure's hand is taking hers, the twelve blazing on her shoulder, the court coming up out of its bow like wheat standing after wind; in the near foreground, large, the Cardinal's long hand pushing the pouch all the way back into the red sleeve; and a small inset of Constance in the gallery with both hands coming off the rail. One caption. The dominant turn is the count failing and the pouch going back into the sleeve as the King takes the Queen's hand. The Cardinal owns the count; the room owns the consequence. The reader turns because the trick has failed and the man who never stands is getting up.

Dramatic. The Cardinal standing, for the only time in the book, and turning toward the long doors; in the last of them, a boy with mud to the waist, hat in hand, hair in his eyes, looking straight back at him across the whole ballroom. Who is that boy? The Gascon, from Meung. The Cardinal's hand takes the folded cream letter with the broken red seal from the arm of the chair beside him, unfolds it, and reads the name at the bottom: ah, the letter, a father asking a favour for a son; d'Artagnan. The dominant turn is the Cardinal standing, the boy in the doorway, and the name read off the stolen letter. The Cardinal owns the page; the boy owns the doorway. The reader turns because the most powerful man in France has just learned his name and said it quietly. The name spoken is a balloon; nothing on the letter is legible.

Dramatic. The gallery of the Louvre. Constance running its length; the boy coming up the gallery stair; they stop a step apart. You did it, she counted, the Cardinal counted, everybody counted; I told you I'd be famous. She takes a ring from a small purse at her waist, puts it in his hand, and closes his fingers over it with both of hers: from the Queen's own hand, taken off when she saw him in the door, and Constance sent running; she said tell him I counted to twelve too; she saw me? everybody saw you, you're wearing two countries. At the rail looking down: the red robe going out by a side door, and after it the sling. He knows my name now, the Cardinal; he looked straight at me and opened a letter, cream with a red seal; the boy has been chasing that letter since Meung and the Cardinal has read his name off it; then he knows the name of the boy who beat him; let him. The dominant turn is the Queen's ring and the boy's name in the Cardinal's mouth. Constance owns the page. The reader turns because there are three men who do not know yet that he arrived.

## PAGE 47

Dramatic. Late night, the attic. The boy on the bed with his boots still on and the ring on his hand; Constance a step inside by the window; a caption says they came home that night in the order they had stopped. Boots on the stair, and Porthos's head through the door with a second, stranger's hat crammed on top of his own: he won, ate the inn, rode after the boy, never caught him, rode back, did anyone see him, did you arrive? I arrived. Aramis behind him, limping, immaculate, the handkerchief still round his hand: he held the bridge till dark and then, as the Fathers tell us, walked, a long way on one leg; did you arrive? I arrived. Athos last, filling the doorway, hat in hand, looking at the boy; Porthos's face says what Athos smells like: did you arrive? I arrived. Good. Athos, you smell like a hundred barrels; a hundred and six, he counted them, it was a long week. The dominant turn is the three coming home and the same question three times. Athos owns the last word. The reader turns because all four are in one attic and it is nearly morning.

## PAGE 48

Illustrated prose. Morning, dew, the field behind the Carmelites: the four on the grass by the yellow wall with bread and a bottle, Constance's open basket between them, and the yellow horse drinking from the trough. A smaller image: the boy's hand, the ring on it, holding a piece of bread. The prose counts what he has the way he counted on the bench: the yellow horse nobody will laugh at again, his father's sword, a queen's ring he can never sell or explain, three friends who each held a door, and a man in red somewhere in Paris who knows his name; he came with a letter and calls it a fair trade. The dominant turn is breakfast on the grass and what the boy has now. The four own the page; the horse gets the trough. The reader turns because there is a man in red somewhere in Paris who knows his name and one page left to see him.

## PAGE 49

Spectacle. From above and behind: four men walking away from the reader down a narrow Paris street, four abreast, hats on: the small one in ochre in the middle with a hand on his hilt, the huge one with the red plumes, the slim one, the tall one on the outside. In the foreground at the top of the frame, a window with a thin figure in red in it, seated, watching them go, one hand stroking a grey cat on the sill. One caption and the end card. The dominant turn is the four walking away and the red figure who knows the boy's name. The four own the street; the Cardinal owns the window. The reader does not turn: the book ends, and the man in red will be there when there is a next page.
