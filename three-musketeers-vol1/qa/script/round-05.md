# Script gate — round 05 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds*

*(Saved verbatim by the orchestrating session from the script-critic agent's reply; the critic's toolset is read-only and could not write this file itself.)*

## Cold read

A farm boy of eighteen, d'Artagnan, is sent north by his father with an old yellow horse, a sword too long for him, a rule ("never refuse a fight"), and a sealed letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers. At an inn at Meung a man in black with a scar and a red feather laughs at the horse; the boy draws, is stooled from behind, and wakes to find the letter gone and the man ridden for Paris. In Paris the Musketeers' gate shuts on a boy with no letter, and the scar is across the street, smiling. Chasing him, the boy runs into three Musketeers in turn — a tall still man with a bad shoulder (Athos), a huge loud one whose baldric is only gold in front (Porthos), a neat blond one standing on a lady's handkerchief (Aramis) — and books a duel with each at noon, one, and two. At the field the three turn out to be each other's seconds, laugh, and are interrupted by the scar with five of the Cardinal's red cloaks. Told to stand back, the boy draws instead: "Four of us." They win bloodlessly; the scar runs; the boy walks home in the middle of three friends.

Athos sends him to an attic let by Constance, who dresses the Queen. Weeks pass; she looks up the stair every evening. One rainy night four men grab her in the street; the boy goes out of the window and beats them while the scar watches from a doorway and declines, again, to stay: she "only carries it." She carries the Queen's letters and will not say where. In a red room the Cardinal, who has been reading those letters for a year, sends the scar to London to cut two diamonds off the Duke of Buckingham's shoulder, "only two," because "the King will give a ball."

A week later Constance comes up the stair white: the King has asked the Queen to wear her twelve studs on Monday, and she gave them to Buckingham. There is nobody to send. "There's me. Are you asking?" The three ride with him for a reason none of them asks; they say "All for one" for the first time. On the road each stops so he can go on: Porthos at Chantilly over a toast, Aramis on the bridge at Beauvais against six, Athos behind a cellar door at Amiens. Alone, the boy reaches Calais, finds the harbour closed by the Cardinal, and the scar walking off the England boat with the only pass. They fight on the gangplank; the scar goes into the harbour with his arm hurt and two diamonds in his fist; the sergeant "decides he can read." In London Buckingham comes down his own stairs, counts ten, remembers a masked man with a scar on the stair at his ball, understands the trick, and has his jeweler make two in two days. The boy rides home, changing horses five times, and reaches Paris as the Louvre lights for the ball.

At the back stair he gives Constance the box: twelve. In the ballroom the Cardinal waits with the two in his sleeve and tells the scar "ten is ten." The Queen enters; the Cardinal counts to twelve, twice; "then someone made two"; he stands, sees a muddy boy in the door, and reads the name off the father's letter. Constance brings the Queen's ring: "tell him I counted to twelve too." That night the three come home in the order they stopped — "Did you arrive?" "I arrived." — and in the morning the four eat breakfast on the field where they met, while a man in red at a window knows the boy's name.

Where I stopped on the cold read:

- **p30, the caption.** "The horse got up and went home; he did not." I read it twice. First reading: Aramis did not get up. I checked the picture description to see if he was down. He is not. Second reading: he did not go home. Both are grammatical; the first is the alarming one.
- **p41.** Constance is standing in the kitchen door of the Louvre at the exact minute he arrives. I looked back to p24 and p27 for where they agreed on it and found nothing.
- **p47.** Aramis says he "went home" from the bridge on the first night out; the caption over him says the three "came home that night in the order they had stopped." I reread to see which was true.
- **p22, "And then?"** I did not know what a ball had to do with two diamonds. That one I was meant to carry, and it pays on p38 and p42; I stopped but I was not lost.

Nothing else made me look back. The calendar (Thursday at Calais, Saturday night the jeweler, Monday sunset at the gate) holds without arithmetic.

## Is it alive?

**I — The Yellow Horse (1–7).** Yes. The father's three sentences at the gate are a whole character in fourteen words. Rochefort's "You don't ride a flower to Paris" and the boy's "Then you'll be late for dinner" set both voices in one exchange. The door closing "the way doors close on boys" is the first time I felt for him, and it is on p7 — exactly where the movement needs it. Appetite is total: he wants the fight, the Captain, the letter, in that order, and says the biggest thing first.

**II — Three Duels at Noon (8–16).** The best movement, and funny in a way that is not written for adults over the children's heads. "That was my shoulder. It was hurt before you arrived." / "You've stopped." Porthos's baldric, Aramis's "I dropped nothing... and I was standing on it by accident" / "Then why are you taking it?" The three-way "That's my one o'clock!" and then the red cloaks coming through the gap before anyone can finish laughing. "Four of us" is enacted, not announced, and Athos's "Don't get hurt. I'd have to explain it" is the ledger's want-from ("from d'Artagnan, nothing") done as a line. The attribution-strip test passes on every page here: nobody could mistake Porthos for Athos on the walk home.

**III — The Girl Downstairs (17–22).** Heat, and restraint about it. "Next week I'll be famous." / "Everyone who comes to Paris says that." The window drop is the right choice for this boy. p21 is the strongest subtext page in the book — "Then don't tell me. I'll be upstairs" — and p22 is the coldest: the cat, the hand that does not stop, "Nobody counts to two." The reader learns the trick before the heroes do, which is what makes the next twenty pages tense rather than merely eventful.

**IV — All for One (23–27).** The ask is written as an ask that will not be made — "That isn't no" — and it is the moment the girl becomes an active chooser: she sends him, knows what she is doing, and sews the letter in with her own hands. "You haven't asked me why. Not one of you." / "Why would we? You're going. That's the why." The oath lands because nobody had said it before. p27's "did not say come back, because she did not want to hear him promise" is the sentence I would quote from the volume.

**V — The Road (28–35).** The three stops escalate correctly — a toast, a bridge, a cellar — and each man stops in character (Porthos for pride, Aramis for arithmetic, Athos for the letter, "not for me"). "I'll have his hat" pays off on p47 with the hat crammed on his head. The Calais quay is a real set piece for a ten-year-old: a closed sea, the only pass in the enemy's hand, a duel on a plank a foot wide, and "You stayed. I said you would." The sergeant's choice is enacted by caption as the volume rule requires, and it is the caption I liked best for that reader: "decided that he could read."

**VI — London (36–40).** Adequate leaning to good. p37–38 are arithmetic pages by necessity, and Buckingham's reconstruction of the theft is told, not shown (noted below, nonblocking). But the movement is rescued by two things: Buckingham coming down his own stairs "himself," and the unfinished "Tell her." "d'Artagnan. Nobody. I ride fast." is the boy's whole voice in five words. I felt something; not as much as elsewhere.

**VII — Twelve (41–49).** Yes. "Where the Cardinal can see me." / "Yes. That too." The count on p44 — "Ten. Eleven. Twelve." "Twelve." — does the thing the spectacle page before it promised. "Then someone made two" is a villain's best line: quiet, correct, beaten. "Tell him I counted to twelve too" is the emotional payoff and it is placed in Constance's mouth, where it belongs. p47's three "Did you arrive?" is the heart of the book and it is where I would have stopped reading to a seven-year-old and looked at him. The close, with the red figure at the window, is the correct final image.

**Would I read it?** Yes. I would read it to both of them. What remains is small and mechanical, but two of the three items sit on pages a child will feel most — the bridge and the homecoming — and neither is an ambiguity I want a seven-year-old to carry.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p30 — the caption can be read as Aramis not getting up.**
"The horse got up and went home; he did not." The first verb is *got up*; the elided completion a listener supplies is "he did not get up." Read aloud, to this audience, over a picture of one man against six at dusk with the first star out, that is the reading a child takes, and nothing between here and p47 corrects it: p32's prose leaves him "with six in front of him and his back to the rail," and he next appears seventeen pages later, limping. The reader must repair "he did not [go home]" against "he did not [get up]," and a bloodless book should not spend seventeen pages letting a seven-year-old think Aramis is dead. This is the round-history's new sentence; the intent (the horse went home, he stayed) is good and should be kept.
*Revision:* make the elided verb explicit. "The horse got up and went home without him. Aramis stayed." Or: "He held the road. He was still holding it when it got dark. The horse got up and trotted home; Aramis did not go with it."

**2. p41 — Constance is in the kitchen door at the exact minute he arrives, and the reader never saw them arrange it.**
"You're here. You're here. Is it—" The boy comes out of the dark to a service door at the back of the Louvre and she is standing in it. Nowhere on p24, p27, or p40 do they agree a door, a place, or a night. The reader must supply "they must have arranged this" — a first-time ten-year-old asks "how did she know he'd come to the back?" and a seven-year-old asks "how did he know where she was?" The ledger's "Never seen at the Louvre except at the back stair" is a planning fact; nothing in the script puts it in either character's mouth. A character is acting on information the reader never saw them acquire.
*Revision:* one balloon on p41, panel 1, which also buys heat: **CONSTANCE:** "You're here. You're here. I said the kitchen door, and I've stood in it every night since you left. Is it—" (about 19 words; p41 stays comfortably inside the dramatic budget). If the owner prefers to plant it earlier, p24 panel 3 can take it instead ("The kitchen door at the back of the Louvre. I'll be in it every night until you come.") — but do not put it in p27's or p40's prose, both of which are at the prose ceiling already.

**3. p47 — Aramis "went home" six days ago, and the caption says he came home tonight.**
The caption: "They came home that night in the order they had stopped." Aramis, arriving after Porthos, limping, the handkerchief still round his hand: "I held the bridge until it was dark, and then, as the Fathers tell us, I went home." Beauvais is one day from Paris; he stopped on the first night out; if he went home he has been in Paris since Wednesday and would not be arriving muddy on Monday night, nor asking "Did you arrive?" as if he had just walked in. Porthos's "Then I rode" has no object — rode where? A reader who has just been told the order of return has to repair: either the caption is wrong or Aramis is. The joke of "went home" is worth keeping, but it needs the road under it.
*Revision:* put each of the two on the road tonight, in his own voice, within the balloon ceiling. **PORTHOS:** "I won. Then I ate the inn. Then I rode after you and never caught you, so I rode back. Did anyone see me? Did you arrive?" **ARAMIS:** "I held the bridge till dark. Then, as the Fathers tell us, I walked. It is a long way on one leg. Did you arrive?" Athos's "It was a long week" then completes the caption's order without help. If the added words crowd the page, split p47 after Aramis (Porthos and Aramis on one page, Athos filling the doorway alone on the next); do not shorten the three "Did you arrive?" exchanges.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p38, panel 1 — the theft is narrated, not enacted.** "Three nights ago I gave a ball. Every door stood open. A masked man begged my pardon on the stair outside this door." The most dramatic thing on this page is Buckingham's understanding, which is shown; but the only theft in the book is told in two balloons. Consider a small memory inset in panel 1 — the masked man with the scar bowing on the stair, the Duke passing — so the boy's "The man with the scar" answers a picture. Not blocking: the reader is never lost, and the page's turn is intact.
- **p42, panel 1 — the father's letter on the arm of the Cardinal's chair at a ball.** It is there so that p45 can pay off, and it does; but a man who "does not need" the letter carrying it to the ballroom is a convenience the sharper ten-year-old may notice. If it ever bothers the owner, the Cardinal's p42 line "I have his father's letter" is enough and the letter could be produced from the red sleeve beside the pouch on p45. Either staging works.
- **p4 — "The Musketeers. Of course."** The "of course" assumes the reader knows the scar's side and the Musketeers' side are opposed, which is not established until p9 (Porthos) and p12 (the guards). Tone carries it, and the Cardinal's politics are to be felt not explained, so I leave it; a first-time reader will simply hear contempt, which is correct.
- **p46, panel 3** — the boy tells Constance what the reader watched on p45 ("He looked straight at me and opened a letter. Cream, with a red seal"). It is in character — he is frightened and pleased and saying the biggest thing first — and it gives Constance "Let him." Keep it; I note it only because it is the one place the book explains a picture the reader has just seen.
- **Ledger page references are one page stale after the round-5 split**: Rochefort's sling is earned on p35 (ledger says p36); the Cardinal stands on p45 (ledger says p44). Reconcile before the page contract is generated.
- **The King's ball is "Monday" said on a Monday night (p23).** The reader is never asked to compute this, and p38 fixes the week explicitly, so it holds; flagged only so nobody later "clarifies" it into arithmetic on the page.
- **Balance** at 31/8/10 (63/16/20) is fine; the eight prose pages are placed where the book needs breath (11, 18, 27, 32, 39, 40, 48) and none is a lettering workaround.
- **Five-word audit**: every balloon of five words or fewer is either a direct answer to a complete question in the same panel or has one visible referent. "And then?" on p22 is the one deliberate withhold, and the page-turn line owns it; it pays on p38 and p42. No failures.
