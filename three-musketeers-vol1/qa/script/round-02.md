VERDICT: REVISE

# Script gate — round 02 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds*

*(Saved verbatim by the orchestrating session from the script-critic agent's reply; the critic's toolset is read-only and could not write this file itself.)*

## Cold read

A boy of eighteen, d'Artagnan, rides north from Gascony on an old yellow horse with his father's too-long sword and a sealed letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers, and three rules: never sell the horse, never refuse a fight, put the letter in the Captain's hand yourself. At an inn at Meung a man in black with a scar and a red feather laughs at the horse from a window; the boy challenges him, gets a stool across the head from the inn's men, and wakes in the straw with the letter gone and the scar ridden for Paris. In Paris the Musketeers' gate shuts in his face because he has no letter and no name; across the street the scar smiles at him and rides into the crowd. Chasing him, the boy collides with three Musketeers in a row: a tall still one with a sling (Athos), a huge loud one whose gold baldric is plain leather in back (Porthos), and a slim blond one standing on a lady's handkerchief (Aramis). Three duels, noon, one, two, one field. At the field the three turn out to be each other's seconds and laugh; then the scar arrives with five red-cloaked "Cardinal's guards" to arrest them all and take the boy himself. Porthos tells the boy to stand back because he is not one of them; the boy draws anyway: "Four of us." They win, bloodlessly and joyfully; the boy fights the scar, who runs. The three walk him home and Athos sends him to an attic let by Constance, who dresses the Queen every morning and has a gold crown on her apron. Weeks pass; Athos teaches him at dawn; Constance looks up the stair every evening. One rainy night four men grab Constance in the street; the boy goes out the window onto a cart roof, knocks the lantern out, beats them in the dark. The scar, watching from a doorway, walks off: she isn't what he was sent for, "she only carries it." Constance: the Queen's letters; where and to whom she can't say.

Then a red room: a thin man in red with a cat, addressed as "Eminence," holds the boy's father's letter and tells the scar to go to London and cut two of the twelve diamond studs the Queen gave the Duke of Buckingham. "Nobody counts to two. And then the King will give a ball." Constance comes up white: the ball is Monday, the Queen must wear the studs, she gave them to Buckingham, the Cardinal smiled. She has the Queen's letter to Buckingham and nobody to carry it. "Are you asking?" "I've no right to ask." "That isn't no. I've got three friends." The three ask nothing about the letter and agree, "All for one." Constance sews the letter into his coat. On the road Porthos stops at Chantilly to fight a man who toasts the Cardinal; Aramis holds a bridge alone against six; Athos barricades himself in a wine cellar at Amiens and says "Ride." The boy reaches Calais alone, finds the harbour closed by the Cardinal's order, and meets the scar coming back from England with the Cardinal's pass and a pouch. They fight on a gangplank; the scar goes into the harbour; the boy takes the pass ("It says the bearer"), and from the boat sees the scar's fist open on something that glints twice. In London the Duke comes down the stairs himself, reads, opens the casket: ten, two holes. A masked man with a scar at his ball three nights ago. The Duke sees the trap: she wears ten, the Cardinal produces two. He has his jeweler make two more in two days; the boy rides back and reaches Paris as the Louvre lights up for the ball. At the back stair he gives Constance the box: twelve. In the ballroom the Cardinal, pouch in sleeve, waits for ten; the Queen enters; he counts twelve; "Then someone made two." He stands, sees a muddy boy in the door, draws the father's letter and reads the name: d'Artagnan. Constance brings the Queen's ring: "tell him I counted to twelve too." That night the three come up the attic stair in the order they stopped: "Did you arrive?" "I arrived." Breakfast on the field; the boy counts what he has; four abreast down a street, watched from a window by a man in red who knows his name.

Where I stopped on the cold read: p22, wondering who the man addressed only as "Eminence" was and how he knew about the studs; p35, asking whether the golden man was the Duke (he is never called Buckingham on any London page); p37, when the boy said "He had two diamonds in his fist" after the p34 caption had told me he did not know what was in the hand; p40, when Constance announced "Two of them are new" with no way to know it; p44, when the boy said the Cardinal "read it off my father's letter," which the boy has never been shown to know exists in the Cardinal's possession; and p18, where a page declared as two locations stages three. Also a brief stall at p45, wondering how all three friends reached the attic on the same night within minutes of each other.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a script I would read aloud to a seven-year-old and enjoy myself, and the counting motif — the bench, "Nobody counts to two," "Ten. Eleven. Twelve," "Then someone made two," "tell him I counted to twelve too," "A hundred and six. I counted them," the breakfast — is a real spine, not a decoration. Movement by movement:

**I — The Yellow Horse (1–7).** Alive. The boy's appetite is the engine from the first caption and the scar's want (to amuse himself, then to spite) is felt in every line ("I don't fight anyone before dinner"). The empty pocket on p5 and "Then so do I" are the right size. p7's "The door closed the way doors close on boys" is the book's voice arriving.

**II — Three Duels at Noon (8–16).** The best comedy in the script. The three collisions each carry a distinct want-from (Athos wants his shoulder left alone; Porthos wants the street not to have seen the baldric; Aramis wants the handkerchief back and the subject changed), and the attribution-strip passes cleanly — cover the names on p12 and you still know who says "He's my noon." p13 is the heat of the whole book in one panel: "You're not one of us." / "Four of us." p16 has real warmth and Aramis's "In theory" is a voice, not a writer.

**III — The Girl Downstairs (17–22).** Alive, and the subtext is the best in the script: Constance's "Double is four livres. Goodnight" and p18's "neither of them said anything about it." The rescue is enacted, the lantern caption is exactly what narration is for. p22 is a superb villain page in voice ("He would miss twelve. Nobody counts to two") — its two problems are introduction and knowledge, not life (findings 2).

**IV — All for One (23–26).** Alive. p23's "Are you asking?" / "I've no right to ask." / "That isn't no" is the emotional hinge and it is earned. p24's "You haven't asked me why" / "You're going. That's the why" is Porthos exactly. p26's "did not say come back, because she did not want to hear him promise" — I felt that.

**V — The Road (27–34).** Alive, and structurally the smartest movement: three sacrifices in three registers (pride, wit, stillness), then p31 counts them. The Calais duel on a gangplank is the set piece the audience wants. "The sergeant ... decided that he could read" is a perfect way to obey the no-balloons-for-scenery rule.

**VI — London (35–39).** Competent-to-good rather than thrilling; this is the one movement where the mechanics (ten, two holes, three nights ago, Thursday, Monday, two days plus two) sit closest to the surface. It is rescued by Buckingham's heat ("Who are you, boy?" / "Nobody. I ride fast." and the unfinished "Tell her"). I did not feel nothing; I felt slightly briefed on p37. Not blocking, but see the nonblocking note on p37.

**VII — Twelve (40–47).** Alive. The Cardinal's "Ten is ten," the count, "Then someone made two," the one time he stands, and the three "I arrived"s are the payoff the whole book promised. p46's second counting closes the bench on p11 and p47 is the right last image.

Would I read it? Yes. Would Francisco and Sebastian? Yes — and Sebastian will say "Four of us" for a week.

## Mandatory findings

**1. Page 18 — three locations on a page declared as two.** The header says "two — the field at dawn, and the stair," but panel 2 is "A tavern table: Porthos mid-story ... d'Artagnan laughing with his mouth full." Field, tavern, stair is three locations, which the mandate calls a blocker (it renders as equal bands and a montage). The reader loses nothing if the tavern goes: the prose already carries Porthos's stories and who paid. Prescribe: cut panel 2; the page becomes the dawn field (dominant) and the stair, which is also the page's actual turn (every evening she looks up).

**2. Page 22 — the man in red is never named on the page, and he acts on information the reader never saw him acquire.** (a) In rendered text this man is only ever "Eminence" (p22, p41, p43). "The Cardinal" has been a name in other people's mouths since p9 ("if the Cardinal wants my sword"), p12 ("the Cardinal's guards"), and will be on p23, p32, p40; but nothing on the page ties that name to the thin man with the cat until p44, when the boy says "The Cardinal" while "the red robe" goes out a door. A seven-year-old does not know "Eminence" is how you address a Cardinal, and the boy's final beat ("a man in red ... who knew his name") depends on the reader knowing his. Prescribe: one caption on p22 panel 1 that names and places without explaining his politics, e.g. "The Cardinal, in his red room. The man with the scar answered to him, and so did every red cloak in France." (b) "The Queen has given the Duke of Buckingham twelve diamond studs" — nothing shows how he knows this; "I have something better now" implies he just learned it, but the only thing on his desk is the father's letter. Prescribe: one line that enacts the source of his knowledge without a lecture, e.g. before "The Queen has given...": "I have read her letters for a year, Rochefort. I know where she sends them, and I know what she sent." That also makes the earlier attacks on Constance's basket make retroactive sense and tightens the threat to the letter sewn into the coat on p26.

**3. Page 35 — Buckingham is never named on the page.** The caption calls him "the richest man in England"; p36–38 call him nothing; p40 calls him "the Duke." The reader has been told (p22, p23) that the studs are with "the Duke of Buckingham, in London," and must infer that this golden-haired man on a marble stair is he. That is recoverable, which is a revision flag, and the fix is free. Prescribe: p35 caption: "London. He had the mud of two countries on him and a letter with the Queen's seal, and the Duke of Buckingham, the richest man in England, came down the stairs himself."

**4. Page 37 — the boy states a fact the script told the reader he does not have.** p34's caption: "The boy didn't know what was in the other hand. It caught the light, twice, and the hand closed." p37: "I fought him at Calais this morning. He had two diamonds in his fist." A first-time reader stops: he said he didn't know. The inference is the scene's pleasure and should be made in the room, where Buckingham can complete it. Prescribe: d'Artagnan: "The man with the scar. The Cardinal's man. I fought him at Calais this morning. He came up out of the harbour with something in his fist. It flashed. Twice." Buckingham's "Then I see it" follows and now actually does the seeing.

**5. Page 40 — Constance knows something nobody has told her.** "Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Twelve. Two of them are new." She was told on p23 only that the Queen gave the studs away; she has not heard of the theft or the jeweler, and the previous page insists "even the Duke could not tell which they were." A reader must either assume an off-page conversation or decide she can see what the Duke could not. Prescribe: she counts to twelve and stops; he supplies it: "Twelve. Two of them are new. The scar cut two out at the Duke's ball, and the Duke had two made, and nobody can tell which. Not even the Duke could." Then her "You're here. You're here" on panel 1 and "She's dressing. She has ten minutes" on panel 3 stand as written. This is also the one place a first-time reader gets the whole mechanism restated in a friend's ear before the count on p43, which the ballroom pages need.

**6. Pages 43–44 — the boy knows the Cardinal has his father's letter, and nothing has shown him learning it.** The reader saw the letter on the Cardinal's desk on p22; the boy did not. On p44 he says, as fact: "He knows my name now. The Cardinal. He read it off my father's letter." From the ballroom door he saw a man in red stand, look at him, and read a paper. Prescribe, on p44 panel 3, make the inference audible and his: "He knows my name now. The Cardinal. He looked straight at me and opened a letter — cream, with a red seal. I've been chasing that letter since Meung. He's read my name off it." Constance's reply stands. (On p43 itself, consider having panel 4's letter come from his hand or the chair rather than "the same sleeve" as the pouch; the sleeve is doing a lot of work. Not a blocker.)

## Nonblocking observations

- **Five-word audit, all 47 balloons:** none fails. Two deserve a note. p12 "They are." depends on panel 2 showing Porthos and Aramis arrive — it does, in the next panel, so the referent is uniquely determined. p16 "In theory." is a joke a seven-year-old may not get on the page; it is closed by p18's prose ("paid for the dinner, and pretended Aramis had") and recurs as Aramis's voice on p24, so it is voice, not compression. Everything else — "You've stopped," "Four of us," "Ride," "Ten," "Twelve," "Then someone made two," the three "I arrived"s — is a direct answer to a complete question or a visible action in the same panel.
- **p41 want-from.** The Cardinal's opening two balloons ("She will come in wearing ten, Rochefort, because ten is what she has. And I will go to her with the two she lost...") are a plan recited to the one man who already knows it; addressing him by name is the tell. The page still works as gloating, but it would be stronger if the recital were an answer: put Rochefort's worry first ("And the boy, Eminence? The Gascon reached the boat"), then the Cardinal's plan as his dismissal of it, then "Ten is ten." Same words, now driven by Rochefort's fear.
- **p37 logistics.** "It's Thursday. The ball is Monday." / "Two days for the diamonds, two for the road. Sleep on the boat." is arithmetic dialogue. It is short, it is the deadline the reader needs, and "I don't sleep" pays off on p38, so it passes, but if the page is ever restaged, the day-count could move to a p38 prose line and leave the boy and the Duke the human exchange.
- **Text budgets.** p23 (~93 words, 9 balloons), p24 (~92, 9 balloons, four speakers), and p37 (~96, Buckingham's panel-1 balloons alone ~48) are over the 45–80 comfort band and inside the ~105-with-lanes ceiling. Not a finding; flag for the prompt writer to reserve lanes, and for p37 consider splitting Buckingham's three panel-1 balloons across panels 1 and 2. p21 and p22 sit at ~85.
- **p45 convergence.** The three arrive on the night of the ball, minutes apart, from Chantilly (Wednesday), Beauvais (Wednesday), and an Amiens cellar ("a week"). No child will object, and the page is the reward of the book, but one caption would give it shape without explaining it: "They came home that night in the order they had stopped." Optionally, Constance in the doorway could be the reason ("I sent to the barracks the moment I had the box").
- **p30 → p31 the horse.** The boy leaves Amiens by the back window with red cloaks in the yard, and p31 opens with him riding. A clause in p31's first line ("He took the first horse in the back paddock and rode all night") closes the question a ten-year-old will ask.
- **p30 Athos's voice.** The ledger says Athos has "the fewest words on any page he is on" and "never explains"; on p30 he has the most words and explains ("He says the coin is false. It isn't. He was told to say it"). The innkeeper has no balloon, so someone must carry it; a caption ("The coin was good. The innkeeper had been told to say it was not") would keep Athos silent and level, which is the whole point of him.
- **p11 "everyone said."** "The three best swordsmen in Paris, everyone said" — he has spoken to nobody in Paris but a porter. "The three best swordsmen in Paris, from the look of them" or "the way the blue cloaks had made way for them" would be true to what he has seen.
- **p43 the sleeve.** See finding 6's parenthetical: pouch and letter from "the same sleeve" is one convenience too many. The letter in his hand from the start of the page, or beside him on the chair, costs nothing.
- **Ledger drift, not script defects.** p47 has the Cardinal at a window; the ledger says "never seen outside a chair except once, p43" — specify seated at the window, or amend the ledger. p40 has Constance at the Louvre's back stair; the ledger says "never seen at the Louvre except in the gallery." Rochefort's sling is listed for pp 41–43 but appears on p44 too. All three should be reconciled before references are planned.
- **Balance.** 29/8/10 is 62/17/21 — spectacle heavier than the 13% guide, which for this audience and this book (three set-piece fights, a gangplank, a ballroom entrance) is the right side to err on. Every consequential beat has its aftermath, including the protagonist's (p46).
- **Owner constraints honoured.** Bloodless throughout (trough, hat, tree, puddle, harbour); the Cardinal's politics are felt through red cloaks, a closed harbour, and "Ten is ten," never explained; scenery has no balloons and its provocations (the toast, the false coin, the sergeant) are enacted by action and caption.
