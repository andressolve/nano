VERDICT: REVISE

# Script gate — round 04 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds*

*(Saved verbatim by the orchestrating session from the script-critic agent's reply; the critic's toolset is read-only and could not write this file itself.)*

## Cold read

A Gascon farm boy, d'Artagnan, eighteen, is sent to Paris by his father with three things: an old yellow horse, a sword too long for him, and a sealed letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers, plus a rule, never refuse a fight. At an inn at Meung a scarred man in black laughs at the horse from a window; the boy challenges him, is knocked cold from behind with a stool by the inn's men, and the scarred man takes the letter out of his coat and rides for Paris. The boy wakes, finds the pocket empty, and rides after him. At the Musketeers' gate, with no letter and no name, the door shuts on him; across the street sits the scar, who rides off into the crowd. Chasing him, the boy collides with three Musketeers in turn, Athos (bad shoulder), Porthos (half-gold baldric), Aramis (a lady's handkerchief), and makes duels with all three, at noon, one and two. At the field all three arrive at once and laugh; then the scar comes through the wall with five of the Cardinal's red-cloaked guards to arrest them for duelling. The boy, told he is not one of them, draws and stands in the line, "Four of us," and Athos asks his name. They win bloodlessly; the scar runs, and the boy promises that the third time, he stays.

Athos sends him to Constance, who lets attics and dresses the Queen every morning. Weeks pass; Athos teaches him at dawn; every evening Constance looks up the stair. One rainy night four men seize Constance at her door; the boy goes out the window onto a cart roof, scatters them, and finds the scar watching from a doorway: she isn't what he was sent for, "she only carries it." She carries the Queen's letters and will not say where. The Cardinal, in a red room with a cat, has the father's letter on his desk, has read the Queen's letters for a year, and knows she gave the King's twelve diamond studs to the Duke of Buckingham. He sends the scar to London for two, "only two," and then "the King will give a ball." A week later Constance comes up the stair: ball on Monday, the Queen must wear the studs, the Cardinal smiled. She has the Queen's letter to the Duke and no one to carry it. The boy asks to be asked. He tells the three; nobody asks why; they cross swords, all for one. Constance sews the letter into his coat and does not say come back.

On the road each friend stops in turn so the boy can go on: Porthos at Chantilly over a toast to the Cardinal, Aramis holding a bridge against six, Athos behind a cellar door at Amiens when a good coin is called false. The boy reaches Calais alone; the harbour is closed by the Cardinal's order; the scar walks off the England boat with the only pass and a pouch. They fight on the gangplank; the scar goes into the harbour, the pass falls, the sergeant decides he can read, and the boy sails, not knowing that the two flashes in the scar's fist are diamonds. In London Buckingham comes down his own stairs, reads the letter, opens the casket: ten and two holes. A masked man with a scar was on the stair at his ball three nights ago. Buckingham sees the trap, sends for his jeweler, who makes two in two days; the Duke cannot finish "Tell her." The boy rides two days back and reaches Paris as the Louvre lights for the ball. At the back stair Constance counts twelve. In the ballroom the Cardinal, with two studs in his sleeve, counts the Queen's shoulder: twelve. He stands, for the only time, sees a muddy boy in the doorway, and reads the boy's name off the father's letter. Constance brings the Queen's ring, "tell him I counted to twelve too." That night the three come home in the order they stopped, each asking "Did you arrive?" Breakfast on the field; the boy counts what he has. Last page: four walking away, and a man in red at a window who knows the boy's name.

Where I stopped:

- p5, panel 2: I read "rode for Paris at noon" in the caption and then again in the balloon directly under it, and had to check whether the second was telling me something new. It was not.
- p15: Rochefort says "Twice we've met." I had counted three: Meung, the Musketeers' gate (where he spoke to the boy), the field. I paused to decide the gate did not count, then hit "Three times now" on p21 and "This is the fourth" on p33 and had to re-decide each time.
- p24, panel 1: "She wrote to him tonight." The previous page named the King, the Duke, and the Cardinal in one balloon. I resolved "him" to the Duke from "rides to London," but I resolved it; it did not arrive resolved.
- p31: "one of the boy's coins." The boy had no money on p16 and could not pay two livres on p17; nobody has since given him any. I wondered, briefly, where the coin came from.
- p44: the count I had turned p43 for was over in two balloons, and the page had moved through the mechanics and on to the name before I had felt the Queen win. I felt the name; I did not feel the count.
- p45: I wondered how the ring got from the Queen's hand on the floor of the ballroom to Constance in the gallery in the time it took the boy to climb one stair. I did not stop long.

Nothing else made me reread. I never guessed at who anyone was, and I never needed the docs for a motive.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is the rare script that would be a pleasure to read even before pictures, and I want to say that before the findings.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** Alive. The horse joke is cruel in the right way; "You don't ride a flower to Paris" is a line only Rochefort would say. The stool from behind, the letter lifted from an unconscious boy's coat with "What does a farm boy carry that's worth all that shouting?", and then p5's turned-out pocket: that is a real gut punch at page 5. The want is a boy's want, total and unembarrassed. The door on p7 "closed the way doors close on boys" is the narration doing exactly what §6 allows. Only p5's doubled fact (Finding 1) is dead weight.

**Movement II (pp 8–16).** The best comic architecture in the book. Three collisions, three humiliations, three voices established in one balloon each: Athos "You've stopped," Porthos "Nobody sees the back of my baldric and tells the street about it," Aramis "How busy you are." Strip the names on p12 and you still know "That's my one o'clock!" from "That is my two o'clock." from "He's my noon." Heat on p13 is precise: he is told he is not one of them and draws anyway, and the cost of "Four of us" is that the boy has just given the side with fewer swords one more thing to lose. "Don't get hurt. I'd have to explain it" is Athos's whole character. Subtext is everywhere ("In theory."). I laughed on p16.

**Movement III (pp 17–22).** Constance is the best-written voice in the script: "Everyone who comes to Paris says that." "Double is four livres. Goodnight." p18's stair, with nobody saying anything about it, is heat without a word. p21 has real stakes between two people in a doorway: she will not tell him, he says then don't, and stays. p22 is chilling and economical; "Nobody counts to two" is the Cardinal's politics felt, exactly as the owner asked, never explained. The cat that does not stop is the right image.

**Movement IV (pp 23–27).** "There's me. Are you asking?" / "That isn't no." is the emotional turn of the book and it is enacted as a negotiation between two people who both want the same thing and cannot say it. p25 "Good. Then nobody can make us tell" is Athos to the bone. p26 is earned by pp 8–25 and by nothing else, which is how it should be. p27's "did not say come back, because she did not want to hear him promise" is the best sentence in the script.

**Movement V (pp 28–35).** The three stops are structurally beautiful because each is in character: Porthos stops for his vanity, Aramis stops with a joke, Athos stops with the fewest words on the page. p32's counting them is the boy's interior at the right length. Calais: a sign, the only pass in the world walking off a boat in the hand of the one man he has sworn to make stay. The gangplank is a set piece Francisco will turn back to. The sergeant "decided that he could read" is the volume rule (scenery silent) turned into a joke.

**Movement VI (pp 36–40).** Adequate, and I should be honest that this is the movement where I felt least. It is a mechanism: count ten, deduce the trick, make two. Buckingham has a voice (fast, warm, "Send for my jeweler!") and p36's stairs and p39's unfinished "Tell her" give the movement its heart. But the trap is now stated three times in the book, by the Cardinal (p22), by Buckingham (p38), and by the Cardinal again (p42), and p38's "Then I see it. She wears ten, and the Cardinal steps forward..." is the one place where a character says the plot out loud to the reader. It passes because it is Buckingham's moment of understanding and the ledger wants him to have it, and because Sebastian (7) will be glad of it. It is not a blocker. It is the flattest page in the book.

**Movement VII (pp 41–48).** p41 is excellent: her lips over the box, his eyes on her face, "Where the Cardinal can see me." / "Yes. That too." p42–43 build correctly. p44 is where I have my one structural finding (Finding 5): the climax the reader has carried since p22 is dispatched in one small panel and the page is already about the letter. p45's ring line is lovely. p46's "Did you arrive?" three times, and "A hundred and six. I counted them," made me feel something, which is the test. p47 rhymes with p11 and p48 leaves the right man at the right window.

Attribution-strip: passes on every dramatic page. Want-from: passes in every scene; no scene exists to inform. Logistics register: none; the plans on p25, p38 and p41 are short and carried by want. Aphorism register: none; p13's "I've got the heart" is the closest and it is the boy's own boast, not the writer's. Appetite: the protagonist is never serene for a page. Heat, subtext, voice: present. Would I read it: yes, and I would read Volume II.

## Mandatory findings

**1. Page 5, panel 2 — the caption and the balloon carry the same fact.**
Caption: "The gentleman with the scar had ridden for Paris at noon, and everyone in the inn had watched him go." Balloon directly beneath: "He took it. The stable boy says he rode for Paris at noon. With my letter." A first-time reader reads "rode for Paris at noon" twice in one panel and stops to check whether the second is new information. §6 forbids narration that repeats what dialogue makes clear, and this is the page's gut punch; the doubling flattens it. Revise: let the caption keep what only it can carry (the shut window, the black horse, that the inn watched) and give the fact to the boy once. For example, caption: "The window was shut. The black horse was gone, and everyone in the inn had watched it go." Balloon unchanged.

**2. Pages 15, 21 and 33 — the count of encounters is off by one against what the reader has seen.**
p15 Rochefort: "Twice we've met, boy." p21: "Three times now, Gascon." p33 d'Artagnan: "I said the third time you'd stay. This is the fourth." The reader has watched them meet at Meung (p3–4), at the Musketeers' gate (p7, where Rochefort speaks to him: "Still chasing, boy?"), at the field (p15), in the street (p21), and on the quay (p33): five. Every time a number is spoken, a ten-year-old who has been told to track "the man from Meung" has to decide that the gate did not count, and this is a book whose climax is a man counting correctly. The promise "the third time, you stay" and its payoff "You stayed. I said you would" (p35) are a running thread and must add up on first read. Revise, either way: (a) count blades, not meetings, so the gate is naturally excluded: p15 "Twice you've drawn on me, boy. Twice is enough for one week." / p21 "Three times you've drawn on me, Gascon. And I'm still not staying." / p33 as written; or (b) count the gate and renumber (three / fourth / fourth-and-fifth) throughout. Option (a) is cleaner and keeps the boy's promise intact. Reconcile the ledger's Rochefort arc (which currently says "Twice" in the alley and "Third time" at Calais) to whichever is chosen.

**3. Page 24, panel 1 — "She wrote to him tonight" has two possible antecedents across the page turn.**
The last balloon on p23 is "the Cardinal smiled when the King asked her to wear them," and the panel before it names the Duke of Buckingham. p24 opens "She wrote to him tonight. Find someone to carry it, she said." Read aloud, a listener asks "wrote to whom, the King?" The reader resolves it from "rides to London" on p23 panel 3, which is a repair, and it is on the page where the quest is launched. §7.4. Revise: "She wrote to the Duke tonight. Find someone to carry it to London, she said. There isn't anyone."

**4. Page 31, panel 1 — the boy pays with a coin the book has told us he does not have.**
"The innkeeper holding one of the boy's coins up to the light" and "He says my coin is false!" On p16 he has "a horse and no money"; on p17 he cannot pay two livres; on p18 Porthos pays for every dinner. Nobody gives him money before he rides. Mechanical continuity of money is named in §3b, and the reader who has enjoyed the running joke of his poverty pauses here. Revise, either: make the coin Athos's ("The innkeeper holding one of Athos's coins up to the light" / D'ARTAGNAN: "He says your coin is false! And there are red cloaks in the yard."), which also sharpens the Cardinal's pretext, since it is a Musketeer's coin being refused; or add one clause to p27's prose: "Athos had found the boy something faster that nobody laughed at, and put a purse in his saddlebag without saying whose it was."

**5. Page 44 — the climax of the book, the mechanics, and the major revelation share one page, and the count gets no consequence before the plot moves on.**
Panel 1: "Ten. Eleven. Twelve." / "Twelve." Panel 2: "I cut two, Eminence... They're in your sleeve." / "Then someone made two." Panel 3: the Cardinal stands and sees the boy. Panel 4: the letter and the name. The reader turned p43 for "the Cardinal is counting"; the count fails in two balloons, the ingenious mechanic is explained in the next panel, and the page's dominant image and turn belong to a different revelation, the name. §4.8 and §3f are explicit: mechanics and a major revelation on one page is a finding, and rescue receives reaction before the plot moves on. As written the Queen's victory is never enacted on the page: no King, no court, no Constance reacting; it is reported by Constance on p45 ("She counted, and the Cardinal counted, and everybody counted"), which is the narrated-not-enacted failure at the one place it matters most. Revise: split p44 into two dramatic pages. **p44 (the count):** the Cardinal's lips moving, "Ten. Eleven. Twelve." / "Twelve."; Rochefort sweating, "I cut two, Eminence. I swear it. They're in your sleeve." / "Then someone made two."; the dominant panel is the consequence in the room, seen from the Cardinal's chair: the Queen reaching the dais and the crowned figure's hand taking hers (scenery, no balloon), the court's bow finishing, and the Cardinal's long hand pushing the pouch all the way back into the red sleeve; an inset of Constance in the gallery, her hands coming off the rail. Turn: the trick has failed and the Cardinal cannot step forward. Page-turn: the man who never stands is getting up. **p45 (the name):** the Cardinal standing, for the only time in the book, and turning toward the doors; the boy in the last of them; "Who is that boy?" / "The Gascon. From Meung."; the letter from the arm of the chair; "Ah. The letter. A father asking a favour for a son." / "d'Artagnan." Renumber 45–48 to 46–49. The balance becomes 31/8/10 of 49, still within the mandate.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p42, panel 1.** The father's letter on the arm of the Cardinal's chair at a ball reads as placed for p44 rather than brought for a reason. It does no harm to a child, but one clause would motivate it: after Rochefort's "The Gascon reached the boat," give the Cardinal "A boy reached a boat. I have his father's letter; I know exactly what he is. The Duke has ten. Ten is ten."
- **p45 (p46 after the split), panel 2.** "She took it off when she saw you in the door" asks the reader to accept that the ring travelled from the Queen on the floor to Constance in the gallery in the time the boy climbed one stair. One phrase covers it: "She took it off when she saw you in the door and sent me running."
- **p38, panel 1.** "A masked man begged my pardon on that stair" — the bedroom has no visible stair. "On the stair outside this door" places the theft where p37 says nobody comes.
- **p29 → p30.** Aramis's horse goes down on the planks with a "KRAK." Sebastian (7) will ask whether the horse is all right, and the script never says. One clause in p30's caption ("The horse got up and went home; he did not.") or in Aramis's p46 line would settle it.
- **p47, page-turn line.** "One more page, and it has almost no words" is a note about the book, not a reason in the story. The prose already supplies the reason: "there is a man in red somewhere in Paris who knows his name."
- **p38.** The trap is stated three times (p22, p38, p42). This is the right number for a seven-year-old and one too many for a ten-year-old; if the London movement is ever tightened, Buckingham's "She wears ten, and the Cardinal steps forward with the two she's missing" is the line that could become an action (the glove hitting the table) rather than a sentence. Not a finding; London passes.
- **p46.** Constance is present and silent by the window all page. It works, and the ledger says she speaks with windows. Worth making sure the page prompt keeps her in the picture rather than letting the three fill the door.
- **Ledger reconciliation** (docs describe the script): Rochefort's arc still reads "watches in the alley: 'Twice'" and "'Third time, boy'" at Calais, and the sling note says the fall is p36 (it is p35; after the split it will be p35 with the sling on pp 43–46). Constance's "back stair (p 41)" and the Cardinal's "p 44 / p 48" references will need renumbering after the split.
- **Five-word audit.** Every balloon of five words or fewer was checked against the four questions; all pass. "You've stopped," "They are," "Four of us," "That isn't no," "Ride," "Yes. That too," "I arrived," and "Good" are all answers to complete questions or actions in the same panel and are forceful rather than compressed.
- **Text budget.** p22 sits near the ~105 ceiling for a dramatic page and passed the mechanical audit; nothing to do at this gate, but the p22 prompt should reserve its balloon lanes before the faces.
