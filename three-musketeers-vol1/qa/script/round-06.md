# Script gate — round 06 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds*

*(Saved verbatim by the orchestrating session from the script-critic agent's reply; the critic's toolset is read-only and could not write this file itself.)*

## Cold read

**What happens, as a first-time reader.** A Gascon farm boy, d'Artagnan, eighteen, is sent to Paris by his father with three things: an old yellow horse, a sword too long for him, and a sealed letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers, plus a rule never to refuse a fight. At an inn at Meung a man in black with a scar and a red feather laughs at the horse; the boy draws, gets a stool across the head from the inn's men, and wakes to find the scar has taken the letter and ridden for Paris. In Paris the Musketeers' gate shuts in his face (no letter, no name), the scar mocks him from across the street on horseback, and chasing him on foot the boy collides with three Musketeers in turn — Athos (bad shoulder), Porthos (baldric that is only gold in front), Aramis (a lady's handkerchief he denies) — and books duels with all three at noon, one, and two. On the field the three discover he has challenged all of them and laugh; the scar arrives with five of the Cardinal's red-cloaked guards to arrest them; the boy draws beside the Musketeers ("Four of us"), they win bloodlessly, and the scar retreats ("I took a letter. I don't remember a boy."). The boy takes an attic from Constance, who dresses the Queen. Weeks pass. Men in plain cloaks try to snatch Constance in the rain; the boy goes out of the window onto a cart roof and beats them while the scar watches from a doorway and declines to fight ("She only carries it"). Constance admits she carries the Queen's letters. In a red room the Cardinal, with the boy's stolen letter on his desk, tells the scar he already reads the Queen's letters and knows she gave the King's twelve diamond studs to the Duke of Buckingham; go to London, cut two, "and then the King will give a ball." Seven nights later Constance comes up the stair: the King has ordered the Queen to wear the studs on Monday; the Cardinal smiled. She has the Queen's letter to the Duke and no one to carry it. The boy volunteers; she asks without asking; the three Musketeers agree without asking why; "All for one." Constance sews the letter into his coat. On the road the Cardinal's men peel the friends off one by one — Porthos at a toast in Chantilly, Aramis on a bridge at Beauvais, Athos in an inn cellar at Amiens — and the boy rides alone to Calais, where the harbour is closed by the Cardinal's order and the scar is stepping off a boat from England with the only pass and something in a pouch. They duel on a gangplank; the scar goes into the harbour; the boy takes the pass ("It says the bearer") and sails, seeing but not understanding two flashes in the scar's fist. In London the Duke comes down his own stairs, reads the letter, opens the casket: ten, two holes. He remembers a masked man with a scar on the stair at his ball three nights ago; the boy names him; the Duke sees the trick and has two made in two days. The boy rides home, dodging the towns where his friends fell, and reaches the Louvre's kitchen door on Monday at sunset. Constance counts twelve. At the ball the Cardinal waits with the two in his sleeve; the Queen enters with twelve; the Cardinal counts, counts again ("Then someone made two"), stands for the first time, sees a muddy boy in the last door, and reads the name off the father's letter: d'Artagnan. The Queen sends her ring by Constance ("tell him I counted to twelve too"). The three friends come home in the order they stopped, each asking "Did you arrive?" Breakfast on the field where they met. Four abreast down a street, and a man in red at a window who knows the boy's name.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed.** Very few places, and none I could not resolve on the page itself:

- p23, "She gave them away." I wanted a reason the Queen would give the King's gift to an English duke. The page does not give one, and the boy does not ask. I decided the book meant that — the not-asking is what p25 is about, and p36/p39 let me feel the answer — but I noted the wondering. (Nonblocking, below.)
- p42, the father's letter on the arm of the Cardinal's chair at a ball. I asked "why would he have that with him?" The line "I have his father's letter; I know what he is" answered it a beat later. (Nonblocking.)
- p46 panel 3, the boy telling Constance what I had just watched on p45. A recap, not a stop.
- Nowhere else. I never went back to check a page.

**Paraphrase test after each movement** passed in plain language: I could say who acted, what they wanted, what they chose, and why we go to the next scene, without notes.

## Is it alive?

**Movement I — The Yellow Horse (pp 1–7).** Alive. The three gifts and three rules at the gate (p2) are a real hook, and every one of them pays off later without anyone pointing. Rochefort's voice is set in his first two balloons ("I don't fight children. I don't fight anyone before dinner." — the second sentence is the one word too many, exactly as the ledger promises). "Let's see how far a Gascon gets on his manners" is a villain wanting something from the boy in the room. The appetite is the boy's — to be let in — and p7's door closing "the way doors close on boys" hurts.

**Movement II — Three Duels at Noon (pp 8–16).** The best movement in the book. Three collisions with three different comic engines (a shoulder, a baldric, a handkerchief), each Musketeer given a want-from in his first panel (Athos: to be left alone; Porthos: for nobody to have seen; Aramis: for nobody to have seen *and* to keep the handkerchief). Subtext everywhere: "I dropped nothing... and I was standing on it by accident" / "Then why are you taking it?" p12's triple reveal and Porthos's "Athos, I like him" are earned. p13 "Four of us" is the boy's choice, chosen, not reported. "I took a letter. I don't remember a boy." is the cruelest, best line in the book. Attribution-strip passes: cover the names on p16 and I can still tell Porthos from Aramis from Athos in one balloon each.

**Movement III — The Girl Downstairs (pp 17–22).** Alive, with heat. p17's rent negotiation is a flirtation neither admits to ("Double is four livres. Goodnight."). p18's last sentence is the book's romance stated in a window, and p19's "d'Art—" cut off is the cheapest, most effective proof she thinks about him. Rochefort with folded arms in the doorway across the street through p19–20 is genuinely unsettling. p21 has stakes between the two people present (she will not tell him, and he offers to stay anyway). p22 is the Cardinal's whole character in a cat and one hand: "Nobody counts to two." The politics are felt through "So did every red cloak in France" and never explained.

**Movement IV — All for One (pp 23–27).** Alive and moving. p24 is the emotional center of the Constance thread: she asks without asking and he hears the no that isn't there ("That isn't no"). p25's "You haven't asked me why. Not one of you." / "Why would we? You're going. That's the why." is the heart of the book and it is Porthos who says it, which is right. p27's hands flat on the chest over the stitched letter, "did not say come back, because she did not want to hear him promise" — I felt that.

**Movement V — The Road (pp 28–35).** Alive. Each stop has its own note: Porthos's vanity turned into courage (a toast), Aramis choosing the bridge with the handkerchief on his hand, Athos level behind a barrel ("There are always red cloaks in the yard"). p32's "He counted them as he went, because it kept him awake" plants the counting motif the finale runs on. Calais is a proper set piece: the scar with the only pass, the sergeant who "decided that he could read," the two flashes the boy sails away from.

**Movement VI — London (pp 36–40).** Good, not electric — and I want to be honest that it is the one movement where the boy is mostly a witness. But it is not merely correct. The two holes in the casket (p37) is a real discovery a seven-year-old will feel in the stomach; "d'Artagnan. Nobody. I ride fast." is the boy's voice intact; the masked man on the stair with the scar under the mask is the connection kids love making; "Send for my jeweler!" is a want acted on fast; and p39's "'Tell her,' he said, and stopped" is the Duke's whole secret in four words. p40's "the red cloak looked past him for a Musketeer" and "He said their names at every milestone instead" are alive prose. I would not send this back.

**Movement VII — Twelve (pp 41–49).** Alive and the payoff earns everything. Counting is the spine: Constance counts (p41), the Cardinal counts (p44), the Queen counted (p46), Athos counted barrels (p47), the boy counts what he has (p48, rhyming with p11). "Then someone made two." is the villain losing in four words. "Who is that boy?" is the one question the Cardinal does not know the answer to, and the book made me wait 45 pages for it. The three "Did you arrive?" / "I arrived." exchanges are the friendship stated three times without a speech. "A hundred and six. I counted them. It was a long week." is Athos being funny for the first time, which is the right place.

**Would I read it?** Yes. I would read it to a seven-year-old and expect to be asked to keep going.

**Presence-test summary.** Want-from: named for every speaking character on every dramatic page (see the movement notes). Subtext: present on every dramatic page but p38, which is a discovery page and may be sincere. Attribution-strip: passes for all eight. Enacted-not-narrated: the theft is the only major event off-page, and it is handled correctly as a memory inset with the Duke doing the seeing (p38); the boy's fights, the counting, and the Cardinal standing are all shown. Heat: present in every two-hander. Appetite: the boy wants on every page he is on; his three passive pages (p37–39) are watched over by a Duke who wants louder than he does. Logistics dialogue: none blocking (p25 panel 1 and p38 panel 3 are schedule lines, but each is a character pushing, and each ends on a voice button — "The Cardinal will try to stop me," "I don't sleep."). Aphorism: none. Aramis's "as the Fathers tell us" is a tic and is undercut every time he uses it.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p23 — the Queen's reason.** "She can't. She gave them away." / "She gave them to the Duke of Buckingham, in London." No reason is offered, and the boy does not ask; I take that as deliberate, because p25 makes not-asking the point, and pp 36 and 39 let the reader feel the answer. A seven-year-old will still ask "why would she?" out loud. If the owner wants to give that question a shape without answering it, one clause of Constance's ("To a friend she isn't allowed to have. Don't ask me more; it isn't mine to tell.") would turn an omission-shaped silence into a secret-shaped one, in her established voice from p21. Not required.

2. **p42 — the father's letter on the arm of the chair.** It is a plant for p45, and "as if he had been reading it while he waited" plus "I have his father's letter; I know what he is" motivates it just enough. If the builder is tempted to make the letter small there, p45 rescues it in dialogue ("Ah. The letter. A father asking a favour for a son."), so there is no tiny-prop dependency. Leave as is.

3. **p46 panel 3 — the boy's recap.** "He looked straight at me and opened a letter. Cream, with a red seal. I've been chasing that letter since Meung. He's read my name off it." The reader watched this on p45, and it is a stretch that he could see cream and a red seal across the whole ballroom from the last door. The recognition matters (it is the letter thread closing from the boy's side, not the Cardinal's), so keep the beat; if it is touched at all, let it be recognition rather than description — e.g. "He opened my father's letter. I'd know it across a country." — and leave Constance's "Let him." untouched, which is the best line on the page.

4. **p22 density.** At roughly 102 words it is the heaviest dramatic page in the script and the one most likely to need protected balloon lanes in the prompt. It is within budget and the caption is doing necessary introduction work; nothing to cut. Flagging only so the page contract reserves the lanes.

5. **p49 staging vs ledger.** The ledger says the Cardinal is seated at the window on p49, the cat on the sill; the script's panel note says "a thin figure in red in it ... one hand stroking a grey cat on the sill" without saying seated. Since p45 is meant to be the only time he stands in the book, the page contract should say "seated" explicitly so the builder does not stand him up.

6. **p31 — Athos says "Ride" twice.** The ledger's "never repeats" is contradicted, and rightly; the second "Ride." alone in its own balloon is the stronger moment. Reconcile the ledger to the script, not the reverse.

7. **p21 → p22, a small tonal seam.** Rochefort was "sent for" the letter Constance carried (p21) and the Cardinal then says "I don't need tonight's" (p22). It reads as the Cardinal's indifference, which is in character, and a first-time reader does not stop there. No change; noted only so nobody "fixes" it into a logistics explanation.

8. **Voice, for the ledger.** Every character passes attribution-strip, and the strongest single voice discovery of the draft is that the thematic line ("Why would we? You're going. That's the why.") belongs to Porthos, the loudest man, rather than Athos, the wisest — which is why it does not read as the writer talking. Protect that in revision; do not let it migrate to Athos.
