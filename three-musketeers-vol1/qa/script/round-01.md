VERDICT: REVISE

# Script gate — round 01 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds*

*(Saved verbatim by the orchestrating session from the script-critic agent's reply; the critic's toolset is read-only and could not write this file itself.)*

## Cold read

A farm boy of eighteen, d'Artagnan, is sent north by his father with an old yellow horse, a sword too long for him, a letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers, and the rule "never refuse a fight." At an inn in Meung a man in black with a scar laughs at the horse; the boy draws, is knocked cold with a stool, and wakes to find the letter stolen and the man gone to Paris. In Paris the Musketeers' door shuts on him for want of the letter, and the scar rides past, smiling. Chasing him, the boy collides with three Musketeers in turn — a tall one with a hurt shoulder, a huge one with a baldric that is only gold in front, a neat blond one standing on a lady's handkerchief — and books three duels at noon, one, and two. On the field all three arrive at once and laugh; then the scar arrives with five of the Cardinal's red cloaks. The boy draws beside the three, says "Four of us," and they win without blood. The scar runs. The boy walks home with three friends and no money.

He rents an attic from Constance, who dresses the Queen. Weeks pass. Four men grab Constance in the street; the boy goes out the window, beats them in the rain, and the scar, watching, says she "only carries it" and leaves. The Cardinal, in a red room with a cat, sends the scar to London to cut two of the twelve diamond studs the Queen gave the Duke of Buckingham, so the King can count at a ball. Constance comes up the stair white as paper: the ball is Monday, the studs are in London. The boy rides with the three. Porthos stops at Chantilly to fight a toast, Aramis stops at Beauvais to hold a bridge, Athos stops at Amiens behind a cellar door. The boy reaches the sea alone, finds the harbour shut by the Cardinal, beats the scar on a gangplank, takes his pass, and sees two diamonds glint in the wet man's fist. In London Buckingham counts ten, remembers a masked man with a scar, and has two made. The boy rides home. At the ball the Queen walks in wearing twelve; the Cardinal counts, stands for the only time, asks "Who is that boy?", and reads the name off the father's letter. Constance gives him the Queen's ring. Three friends come home one by one — "Did you arrive?" "I arrived." Breakfast on the field. A man in red at a window knows the boy's name.

I understood the whole story on one pass. It is clean, funny, and fast. Places I stopped:

- p21→22: "She only carries it." / "I wanted what she carries." Carries *what*? Never answered.
- p23→24: the boy holds up a sealed letter from the Queen. Nobody gave it to him on the page.
- p23 vs p37: "four days each way" versus a ride that takes a day and a half and "two for the road."
- p34: the sergeant reads the pass, has just watched the boy dump the Cardinal's man in the harbour, and lets him sail. And the pass is signed "Richelieu," a name that appears nowhere else.
- p36→37: "Someone has been in this room" becomes "a masked man bumped my shoulder at a ball," and the Duke who "looks at them every night" has not noticed two missing for three nights.
- p37: d'Artagnan says "Rochefort." He has never heard the name.
- p39→41: the boy comes through the Paris gate; the Cardinal waits; the Queen walks in wearing twelve. How did the box get from the boy to her shoulder? Constance in the gallery is still praying "Come on" — for whom?
- p40: Rochefort's arm is in a sling. Nothing put it there.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is the first script I have read at this gate where I did not have to hunt for a pulse. The counting motif — twelve, ten, two, "Nobody counts to two," "the King will count," "tell him I counted to twelve too," "a hundred and six, I counted them" — is a real engine and it is carried by objects and mouths, never by narration telling me it matters. Movement by movement:

**I — The Yellow Horse (1–7).** Alive. The father's three gifts and one rule are a perfect setup for a 7-year-old, and "Never refuse a fight" is a rule the whole book will punish and reward. Rochefort's "You don't ride a flower to Paris" and the stool from behind give the boy a grievance with heat in it. Page 7's door "closing the way doors close on boys" is the emotional spine of the book stated once and never again. Appetite: total.

**II — Three Duels at Noon (8–16).** The best comedy in the script and genuinely built from want: Athos wants the boy to stop, Porthos wants nobody to have seen the baldric, Aramis wants the handkerchief back and to be believed. "You've stopped." / "I don't have any." / "How busy you are." — attribution-strip passes cleanly; I can tell the three apart with the names covered. Page 13 is the turn of the book and it is enacted: the boy steps into the line, gives his name only when asked, and Athos gives him the one on the left. "Don't get hurt. I'd have to explain it" is Athos in nine words. Page 16 has the warmth it needs; "So, with the horse" is the boy's voice.

**III — The Girl Downstairs (17–22).** Alive, and the heat is correctly kept under the words. Constance never says a soft thing and is soft on every page — "Everyone who comes to Paris says that," "Double is four livres. Goodnight," "neither of them said anything about it." The window jump is the right choice for the boy. The Cardinal's study is the best-written villain scene: he never raises his voice, "Nobody counts to two" is a line only he could say, and the reader gets his politics as a feeling ("The King's own gift to her, on an Englishman's shoulder") without a sentence of explanation. The protected claim is honoured. The one hole here is what Constance carries — see finding 1.

**IV — All for One (23–26).** Alive. "Are you asking me?" / "I have no right to ask." / "That isn't no." is the subtext test passed in three balloons. "Good. Then nobody can make us tell" and "You're going. That's the why" earn the oath on 25 so that the famous line lands as a consequence rather than a quotation. The sewing on 26 — "did not say come back, because she did not want to hear him promise" — is the tenderest thing in the book and exactly the right length.

**V — The Road (27–34).** Alive, and structurally the cleverest movement: three friends peeled off one by one, each in his own voice, each stop a want and not a plot function ("I drink to the Queen. And to nobody else in this room"; "As the Fathers tell us: no"; "There are always red cloaks in the yard"). Page 31 counting them at the milestones is the book's method turned into feeling. The gangplank is a set piece Francisco will want. The seams are mechanical, not emotional — findings 3, 4, 5.

**VI — London (35–39).** The flattest stretch, though not dead. Buckingham is drawn fast and warm and "Tell her" unfinished is right. But the movement is one dramatic beat (ten, two holes) followed by two consecutive prose pages, and the return road — after eight pages establishing that the Cardinal owns every inn — meets no one at all. I did not feel dread on 39; I felt the writer clearing the table for the ball. Acceptable, and noted below as nonblocking; the mechanical contradictions on 36–37 are findings.

**VII — Twelve (40–46).** Page 42 is the best page in the script: three numbers, one question the Cardinal does not already know the answer to, a name read off a father's letter. Page 44's "Did you arrive?" three times is exactly the reward the road promised, and "A hundred and six. I counted them" closes the motif in Athos's voice. Page 45's second counting is honest and the last image is the right one. What is missing is the physical handoff that makes 41 possible — finding 8 — and it is the one gap in the book the reader must invent for himself at the moment he is least willing to.

Would I read it? Yes. Would I hand it to a ten-year-old? After this round. No dead registers: I found no logistics page and no aphorism exchange; "I've got the heart" on 13 is the nearest, and it is a boy's line, not the writer's. The protagonist is never serene for a page. Every page has a real reason to turn.

## Mandatory findings

**1. Pages 21–22 — what Constance carries is never named.**
Rochefort: "She isn't what I was sent for. She only carries it." The boy shouts "Carries what?" and the page turns on the question. The Cardinal then says "I did not want the girl, Rochefort. I wanted what she carries, and I no longer need it." Two consecutive pages ask the question aloud and the book never answers it. A first-time reader must guess "the Queen's letters" from page 24's sealed letter and page 26's sewing — recoverable, therefore failing. It also leaves the kidnapping without a motive the reader can state. Prescribe: give the Cardinal the answer in his own quiet register on 22, panel 1 — e.g. "I wanted the Queen's letters. The girl carries them to London under her bread. I have something better now." (adjust wording; the point is the object). Optionally let Constance half-say it on 21: "I carry her letters. That's all of it I can tell you."

**2. Page 23 → 24 — the Queen's letter appears from nowhere, with a deadline nobody set.**
On 23 Constance brings news and asks without asking; no letter is mentioned. On 24 the boy holds "a small letter sealed in blue wax with a crown" and says "I have to take a letter to London by Saturday." The reader must repair that Constance fetched a letter from the Queen between pages and that someone chose Saturday. Prescribe: on 23, panel 3, put the letter in Constance's hand before she speaks — "She wrote to him tonight. I was to find someone to carry it. There isn't anyone." — so that "Are you asking me?" is asked over the object. Let the deadline come from her or from the boy's own arithmetic on 24 ("If I'm in London by Friday I'm back by Monday").

**3. Pages 23, 24, 31–32, 37 — the travel arithmetic contradicts itself.**
Constance: "It's four days each way." The ride as shown: Chantilly noon, Beauvais dusk, Amiens evening, all night, Calais at dawn, London the same day ("I fought him this morning at Calais"). Buckingham: "Two days for the diamonds, two for the road." A book whose climax is a man counting cannot have its own numbers disagree; Francisco will count. Prescribe: fix one figure and make every other line obey it. Simplest: 23 becomes "It's two days' hard riding to the sea, and a sea to cross, and the Cardinal owns every inn on the road"; 24's "by Saturday" becomes whatever day that ride actually yields; 37's "two for the road" then agrees.

**4. Pages 32–34 — the sergeant's compliance is a missing causal step.**
The sergeant of the Cardinal's guard watches the boy put the Cardinal's man in the harbour, then reads the pass the boy picked up off the plank and lets him sail. The reader is asked to accept the plot's central hinge on a technicality nobody on the page acknowledges. It is a joke that needs a straight man. Prescribe: give the sergeant one balloon on 34, panel 2, that makes his choice his own — "It says the bearer. I can read." — with Rochefort visible in the water shouting behind him. This is the one place a scenery figure earns a line, and it converts a hole into a laugh.

**5. Page 34 — "Richelieu" is a name the reader has never been given.**
The pass reads "Let the bearer cross. — Richelieu." The man in red is only ever "the Cardinal" or "Eminence"; the harbour sign says "BY ORDER OF THE CARDINAL." A 7-year-old does not know that Richelieu is the Cardinal and will ask who signed the pass. Prescribe: sign it "— The Cardinal," or introduce the surname once in the study on 22 in Rochefort's mouth so the signature pays off. Do not leave the only appearance of a name on a printed object.

**6. Pages 36–37 — two contradictory accounts of how the two studs went missing.**
36: "I keep them here, where I sleep, and I look at them every night." … "Someone has been in this room." 37: "Three nights ago I gave a ball. A masked man bumped my shoulder and begged my pardon." The page-turn promises a burglary; the next page says they were cut off his shoulder in public — and a man who looks every night would have seen ten three nights ago. The reader has to reconcile it; the Duke never does. Prescribe: choose one mechanism and let both pages agree. Cleanest: keep 36's "Someone has been in this room," cut "I look at them every night," and make 37 "Three nights ago I gave a ball. Every door in this house stood open, and a masked man with a scar begged my pardon on the stair outside this room." That keeps Rochefort's scar as the tell and makes the Duke's realisation a realisation, not a correction.

**7. Page 37 — d'Artagnan says "Rochefort."**
"Rochefort. The Cardinal's man. I fought him this morning at Calais." The name has been spoken only in the Cardinal's study (22, 40), where the boy was not. Nobody on the road, at the field, in the rain, or on the quay says it; Rochefort never gives it. The boy acts on information the reader never saw him acquire. Prescribe: "The man with the scar. The Cardinal's man. I fought him this morning at Calais, and he had two diamonds in his fist." The boy naming him only as "the scar" is truer to their relationship anyway; the Cardinal is the one who names people.

**8. Pages 39 → 41 — the handoff of the diamonds to the Queen is missing, and Constance's knowledge on 40 is undetermined.**
39 ends with the boy coming through the gate at sunset. 40: the Cardinal waits with the pouch; Constance in the gallery watches the shut doors, hands white, "Come on. Come on. Come on." 41: the Queen enters wearing twelve. 43: Constance runs to him — "You came" — as if seeing him for the first time. Nowhere does the box pass from the boy's hands to anyone's. The reader must invent the delivery at the exact page where the whole book's promise is cashed, and cannot tell whether Constance's "Come on" is for the boy who has not come or for doors she knows will open on twelve. This is the largest gap in the script. Prescribe: add one dramatic page between 39 and 40 — a back stair or service door of the Louvre, the boy with mud to the waist, Constance with her apron off, the closed casket going from his hands into hers; three or four balloons at most ("Twelve?" / "Twelve. Count them." / she counts under her breath / "Go. She's dressing." ), and let the page's turn be the box changing hands. Then 40 stays as written but Constance's "Come on" is unambiguously aimed at the doors and the count; and 43's "You came" becomes exultation, not first sight. Renumber to 47 pages; the balance stays within tolerance.

**9. Page 40 — Rochefort's sling has no cause on the page.**
"ROCHEFORT, his right arm in a white sling." The last time the reader saw him he climbed out of the harbour soaked and opened his fist on two diamonds; no injury was shown or named. The ledger plans the sling as a mirror of Athos's; the script must earn it. Prescribe: on 34, panel 3, have him climb the harbour stairs one-armed, the other held against his chest, and let the caption or the boy's eye register it ("holding one arm the way Athos had"); or drop the sling. Bloodless either way.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p7, panel 4.** "Still on the flower, boy? Then you'll never catch me." The boy is on foot on the step, hat in hand; the horse is stabled (11 confirms). The taunt reads as if he is mounted. Consider "Still chasing, boy? On foot? Then you'll never catch me."
- **p10 and p17.** Aramis's handkerchief and Constance's apron both carry a small embroidered crown. A reader may conclude Aramis's lady is Constance or the Queen; the book never says. If it is a plant for a later volume, fine; if not, change one of the crowns.
- **p17.** "I'm told you have an attic." Told by whom? "Athos says you have an attic" costs nothing and binds the room to the friends.
- **p19, panel 1.** She shouts "Let go of me! Help! d'Art—" with "one hand already over her mouth." Stage the hand reaching, not landed.
- **p27 and p30.** Porthos narrates the stranger's toast; Athos narrates the innkeeper's "false coin." Both are enacted-not-narrated soft spots caused by the no-balloons-for-scenery rule. A single spoken "To the Cardinal!" and a single "This coin is false" would put the provocation on the page; if the rule holds, these are acceptable as written.
- **The Captain thread.** The boy is shut out for lack of a letter, gains three Musketeers as friends, and never again tries the door; 45 resolves it as "a fair trade." A ten-year-old will still ask why Athos does not simply walk him in. One clause in 18's prose ("Athos offered to walk him through the gate; the boy said not without the letter, and Athos said good") would close it and sharpen the boy's want.
- **p11.** "the three best swordsmen he had ever seen" — he has not seen them fence yet. "the three best swordsmen in Paris, everyone said" or similar.
- **p38–39, and the return road.** Two consecutive prose pages before the climax, and a return journey with no opposition after a whole movement establishing that the Cardinal owns every inn. It works as an exhale, and 40's "Ten is ten" explains the Cardinal's complacency after the fact. If the writer wants dread on 39, one image of a red cloak at a post inn looking at the wrong rider would do it; not required.
- **Balloon counts.** 9, 17, 21, 22, 24, 42, 44 carry eight or nine balloons each. The audit passes them on words; note them for the prompt stage so lanes are reserved before faces.
- **p45.** "A horse nobody would laugh at again" — the yellow horse is drinking from the trough in the same panel, so the referent is clear, but the sentence reads as if the horse changed rather than the boy. Consider "The yellow horse, which nobody would laugh at again, because of who was riding it."
- **Five-word audit.** All 45 short balloons pass with one exception, 40's "Come on. Come on. Come on." — its referent is fixed by finding 8. "In theory" (16) is a joke a 7-year-old will not get and does not need to; 18's prose closes it.
