# Sequence gate — pages 11–20

## Blind sequence read

D'Artagnan sits alone by the river and takes stock: he has little money, a horse he cannot afford, his father's sword, a stolen letter, and three duels due within two hours. Remembering his father's rule never to refuse a fight, he keeps his word and goes to the Carmelite field.

Athos is waiting there, and Porthos and Aramis arrive to discover that the same young Gascon has separately challenged all three of them. Their amusement is interrupted by Rochefort and the Cardinal's guards. Though Porthos tells him the fight is not his, d'Artagnan chooses the musketeers' side. Athos asks his name, accepts him into their line, and gives him an opponent.

The four rout the guards without bloodshed. Rochefort finally crosses swords with d'Artagnan, recognizes his skill but refuses him satisfaction, and retreats again. The musketeers celebrate the victory on the walk back into Paris, praise the boy, and turn his lack of lodging and money into an invitation to eat and stay among friends.

Through Athos, d'Artagnan finds an attic in Constance's house. He notices the crown on her apron and learns that she dresses the Queen. Weeks pass: Athos trains him, all four companions become inseparable, d'Artagnan still refuses to enter the Musketeers without his stolen letter, and a quiet mutual attachment grows between him and Constance on the stairs.

That settled rhythm breaks when four men seize Constance outside the house in the rain. D'Artagnan hears her, leaps from his attic window onto a cart, and puts himself between her and the abductors while Rochefort watches from across the street. In the ensuing four-on-one fight, d'Artagnan scatters the men and knocks the street lantern toward the puddle, turning darkness into his advantage.

## Script comparison

The visible sequence preserves the script's causal chain, emotional progression, exact text, and attribution across page turns. D'Artagnan remains the focal character throughout. Athos, Porthos, Aramis, Rochefort, and Constance remain materially identifiable, including Rochefort's return as the watching black-clad figure with the red feather.

Consequential continuity holds: the father's sword travels from reflection to alliance, training, and rescue; the yellow horse returns when d'Artagnan seeks lodging; Athos's sling is present during the first field encounter and gone after the passage of weeks; Constance's crown, keys, basket, house, and stair establish her role and routine before the abduction; the rainy street, cart, doorway, watcher, and lantern carry directly from page 19 into page 20.

Pacing is varied rather than a wall of dramatic scenes: reflective illustrated prose opens the run, dialogue builds the alliance, spectacle pays off the first fight, convivial aftermath follows, domestic dialogue establishes Constance, illustrated prose bridges several weeks, and the closing dramatic/spectacle pair launches the rescue. Page 18 is intentionally compressed, but it reads as a controlled passage of time and supplies the consequences of the earlier alliance as well as the growth of d'Artagnan's attachment to Constance. Both consequential action beats receive aftermath or continuation within the batch, and the final unresolved watcher provides a sound outward page turn.

No material sequence, continuity, focal, attribution, pacing, or page-turn failure warrants a complete-page redraw.
