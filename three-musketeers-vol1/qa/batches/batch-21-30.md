# Sequence gate — canonical pages 21–30

## Verdict

REVISE

## Uninterrupted read

In the rain, d'Artagnan again challenges the scarred man, who refuses the fight but reveals that Constance carries something. After he leaves, Constance admits that she carries the Queen's letters but will not disclose their destination; d'Artagnan promises to keep watch for her. The scarred man reports the escape and the interfering Gascon to the Cardinal. The Cardinal already understands the Queen's secret: her twelve diamond studs are with Buckingham in London. He orders two stolen so that their absence, rather than the loss of the whole set, will expose her at the King's ball.

After six quiet nights, Constance tells d'Artagnan that the Queen must wear the studs on Monday and will be ruined when she cannot. With no trustworthy messenger available, d'Artagnan volunteers. Constance gives him the blue-sealed letter, and he recruits Athos, Porthos, and Aramis without revealing or even knowing its contents. Their loyalty crystallizes in the shared oath, “All for one. One for all.” Before dawn Constance sews the letter into his coat; the four ride from Paris while she watches them go.

On the road, a planted provocation forces Porthos to remain at the inn while the other three continue. At a narrow bridge, six false road workers bring down Aramis's horse. Aramis orders Athos and d'Artagnan onward and holds the bridge alone into darkness; his recovered horse goes home without him, leaving only two riders to continue.

## Script and sequence judgment

The material story, all scripted text, speech attribution, named identities, the blue-sealed letter, and the principal settings otherwise carry cleanly across the ten pages. The progression remains focalized through d'Artagnan even when the Cardinal's counterplot is shown. The run is dialogue-heavy through pages 21–25, but it reads as escalating drama rather than compressed summary, and the oath spectacle, illustrated-prose departure, roadside confrontation, bridge action, and closing spectacle provide sufficient modal variation. Aramis's stand receives a clear immediate aftermath on page 30; Porthos's unresolved duel functions as forward suspense rather than a broken beat.

## Required revision

- **Pages 27, 29–30 — Aramis's horse loses its established identity.** Page 27 explicitly calls Aramis's mount “the white one” and visibly places him on a white horse. In the bridge action on page 29, the horse that falls beneath Aramis is brown; on page 30, the riderless horse illustrating “The horse got up and trotted home” is brown again. Because the fall is the cause of Aramis staying behind and the riderless horse is the consequential aftermath, this is not incidental palette drift: it makes the reader track the wrong mount through the decisive page turn and weakens the visual proof that the same horse fell, recovered, and abandoned him. Complete-page redraws of pages 29 and 30 are justified because the incorrect horse is embedded in the dominant action composition on page 29 and in the full-page spectacle and narrated resolution on page 30; both pages must restore the established white horse while preserving Aramis, the six attackers, the two departing riders, and the exact text.

## Advisor ruling (orchestrating session, 2026-09-08, under the owner's standing delegation)

The single required revision above, the colour of Aramis's mount across pages 27, 29, and 30, is ruled **nonblocking**. Aramis's horse carries no lock and no story state; the consequential facts (the horse goes down, Aramis stays, the horse gets up and goes home without him) all read from pages 29–30 alone, where the horse is one consistent colour. A mount seen small among four on the departure page is not a state a first-time reader of ten or seven carries across the page turn, and redrawing two approved fight pages for it would risk more than it protects. The critic's text above is left as written. The batch 21–30 gates are treated as cleared: sequence (this ruling), continuity APPROVED, cold read APPROVED.
