# Sequence gate — pages 1–10

## Uninterrupted visible sequence

D'Artagnan leaves Gascony for Paris on an old yellow horse, carrying his father's sword and sealed letter to the Captain of the King's Musketeers. His father tells him never to sell the horse, never to refuse a fight, and to deliver the letter himself. At Meung, a scarred man in black with a red feather mocks the horse. D'Artagnan challenges him and charges into the inn, but the inn men strike him down. The scarred man steals the letter and rides toward Paris on a black horse. D'Artagnan wakes, discovers the theft, learns where the man went, and pursues him.

Paris overwhelms D'Artagnan with its scale. At the Musketeers' gate, the loss of the letter leaves him without proof, a recognized name, or access to the Captain. He then spots the thief and runs after him. During the chase he crashes into Athos's injured shoulder and accepts a duel at noon behind the Carmelite convent. At the next corner, he exposes the ungilded back of Porthos's baldric and accepts a second duel there at one. At the third, he publicly exposes Aramis's connection to a lady's handkerchief and accepts a third duel there at two. By the end of the batch, the stolen letter has shut him out of his destination, the thief has escaped again, and his inherited rule never to refuse a fight has left him with three consecutive duels and no friend to stand with him.

## Gate judgment

The rendered sequence matches the batch script in material story, exact text, and attribution. D'Artagnan, Rochefort, Athos, Porthos, and Aramis are distinctly and consistently identifiable. The sealed letter, yellow horse, black horse, Rochefort's black clothing and red feather, Athos's injured shoulder, Porthos's baldric, and Aramis's handkerchief remain legible where each becomes consequential. Settings and movement across page turns are coherent: Gascony to Meung, Meung to Paris, the Musketeers' gate into the street chase, and the three successive collisions.

Focal integrity is maintained. The batch has a clear escalation from departure, to theft, to exclusion, to pursuit, to the accumulating duel appointments. The theft receives immediate emotional and causal aftermath on page 5 and continues to drive pages 6–8. Pacing does not collapse into compressed summary: the spectacle and illustrated-prose opening establish the journey efficiently, while the dramatic run from pages 3–10 gives the consequential turns room to land. Although the latter stretch uses dramatic pages, its visual and emotional actions vary enough to avoid reading as a monotonous wall of scenes. Each page turn advances or compounds the same active problem without a material continuity failure.
