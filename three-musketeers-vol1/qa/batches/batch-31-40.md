# Sequence gate — pages 31–40

## Uninterrupted read

At Amiens, an innkeeper falsely rejects Athos's good coin as red-cloaked men arrive. Athos takes the cellar door and orders d'Artagnan to ride for the letter, forcing him to leave the last of his friends behind. D'Artagnan rides alone through the night, keeping awake by counting the three men who stopped so he could continue, and at dawn sees the sea for the first time.

At Calais, the Cardinal has closed the harbour. Rochefort arrives with the only kind of pass that can cross it, recognizes and taunts d'Artagnan, and accepts his challenge. They fight on a narrow gangplank; d'Artagnan knocks Rochefort into the water, takes the fallen pass, and persuades the sergeant to honor its wording. As the boat pulls away, he sees an unexplained double flash in Rochefort's closing fist.

In London, the Queen's sealed letter brings Buckingham down to meet the mud-covered messenger himself. The letter warns that the King expects the diamond studs on Monday. Buckingham opens their casket and discovers two empty places. D'Artagnan connects the loss to Rochefort, the scarred Cardinalist whose fist flashed twice at Calais; Buckingham connects Rochefort to the masked intruder at his recent ball and understands the Cardinal's intended public trap. He orders a jeweler to reproduce the missing pair. The work takes two days and nights; Buckingham keeps watch while the exhausted boy finally sleeps. On Saturday night he entrusts the restored box to d'Artagnan and sends him back.

D'Artagnan guards the box through a rough crossing, passes the same Calais sergeant, changes horses repeatedly, and avoids the route where his abandoned friends remain. He reaches Paris at sunset on Monday with the Louvre already lit for the ball.

## Gate judgment

The visible sequence matches the script in material story, exact text, attribution, identity, focal point, and page-turn logic. The letter, Cardinal's pass, two stolen diamonds, and restored twelve-stud casket remain legible causal objects across their transitions. Settings and travel direction progress coherently from Amiens to Calais, London, Calais, and Paris. D'Artagnan remains the focal carrier throughout, while Athos's sacrifice, Rochefort's defeat and retained diamonds, Buckingham's realization, the jeweler's labor, and the return journey each receive an immediate consequence or aftermath.

Pacing is sustained without collapsing into compressed summary. The two illustrated-prose travel/work passages compress elapsed time while retaining decisive emotional beats; dramatic confrontation and deduction alternate with full-page spectacle, and the final travel montage converts urgency into a clear deadline arrival. No consequential beat is stranded, and no material transition failure justifies a redraw.
