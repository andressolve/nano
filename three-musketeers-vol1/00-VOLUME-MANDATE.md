## Owner constraints for this volume (2026-09-07)

- **Bloodless on the page.** Swordplay is loud, constant, and consequential;
  nobody bleeds, nobody dies, no wound is drawn. Defeat is disarming, a fall,
  a trough, a cellar door, a harbor. A page that shows blood or a wound fails
  the page gate whatever else it does.
- **The Cardinal's politics are felt, never explained.** No page explains
  France, England, the King's marriage, Spain, the Huguenots, the Church, or
  why a cardinal runs a country. The reader learns three things by watching:
  the man in red is always calm, he is always already in the room, and the
  Queen is the one person in France he does not command. That is the whole
  politics of the book, and it is enough.
- **Swordplay and friendship loud.** Every movement has steel in it, and the
  four are together, or reaching for each other, on more pages than not.

## The specific failure this volume must avoid

**Four hats, one voice — and the corridor of explanations.**

The source is talky. Adapted faithfully it fails in two ways at once. First,
the four become one voice in four bodies: everyone brave, everyone witty,
everyone ready with an epigram, so that with the speaker names stripped off
no reader can tell Athos from Aramis. The countermeasure is the ledger's
voice line for each of the four, enforced by the attribution-strip test at
every script round: Athos uses the fewest words on any page he is on; Porthos
puts himself in every sentence; Aramis says the opposite of what he is about
to do; d'Artagnan says the biggest thing first. A page where two of the four
could swap lines comes back to the ledger, not to the prompt.

Second, the corridor: Dumas explains the court through Tréville, the King,
the Cardinal, Bonacieux, and a dozen antechambers, and every one of those
explanations is a page with no steel and no want in it. The countermeasure is
structural: Tréville, the King, and Bonacieux are cut to nobody or to
scenery, the Queen is a figure the reader sees across a ballroom and never
hears, and the Cardinal appears on four pages in the whole book, each of
which ends with him having decided something. Nobody in this book explains
politics to anyone; they ride.
