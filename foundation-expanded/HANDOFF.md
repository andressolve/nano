## The Destruction Question

Francisco's question is editorially valuable. The current compressed version says that the Empire is falling but often advances before the reader can see what that means.

The answer is not to add arbitrary battles. In the first Asimov novel, collapse initially means:

- Imperial ships and messages stop arriving.
- Provinces break away and crown local rulers.
- Atomic science becomes ritual, then incomprehensible inheritance.
- Power plants and factories keep working until a part fails that nobody can repair.
- Governments retain uniforms and battleships while losing the systems that make civilization function.
- Hardin, Ponyets, and Mallow avert or win conflicts without destructive battles.

The absence of destruction is therefore partly the thesis. The expanded edition must make **decay, danger, and threatened destruction visible** without converting the story into a conventional war narrative.

Approved approach for Part One: dramatize the fall through a clearly labeled **mathematical projection** generated by Gaal's calculation. Show the causal chain - lost taxes and trade routes, shrinking fleets, failing food deliveries, darkening power sectors, civil conflict, and a future Trantor in ruins - as probability, not as an event already occurring.

The intended emotional idea is:

> Nothing had fallen yet. That was why almost nobody believed him.

Treat that as draft adaptation prose, not a verified Asimov quotation.

## Part One Pilot - Approved Story Spine

Working folder when production is authorized: `foundation-expanded-part1/`.

Working title: **FOUNDATION - PART ONE: THE MAN WHO SAW THE END**.

One-sentence window:

> A young mathematician reaches the world that rules the Galaxy, proves that it is already dying, and discovers that his teacher has turned their trial and exile into the first move of a thousand-year plan.

### Twenty-four-page allocation

- **Pages 1-5 - The world that cannot fall:** Trantor approach, Gaal's awe, descent into the all-metal world, forty billion people, and the contrast between apparent permanence and enclosed dependence. Page 1 should make the promise immediately: this magnificent world will fall.
- **Pages 6-9 - Hari Seldon:** meeting, Seldon's project, one person versus populations, and the basic rules and limits of psychohistory.
- **Pages 10-11 - What 92.5 percent means:** Gaal's calculation becomes a cinematic mathematical projection of cascading Imperial failure and future ruin. Explicitly frame it as probability, not prophecy or supernatural vision.
- **Pages 12-14 - Dangerous knowledge:** Gaal verifies the result, understands why the government is afraid, and is arrested with Seldon.
- **Pages 15-20 - The trial:** give accusation, evidence, Commission fear, Seldon's thirty-thousand-years versus one-thousand-years argument, and Linge Chen's intervention enough room to become a genuine contest rather than a summary.
- **Pages 21-23 - Exile as victory:** twenty thousand families, Terminus, Gaal's realization that Seldon engineered the choice, the Encyclopedia cover story, and the Second Foundation at Star's End.
- **Page 24 - The first move:** exile ships leave a Trantor still blazing with light. Nothing has visibly fallen, but the Plan is in motion.

Do not include Apple-only Gaal backstory, Raych romance, the Star Bridge, cloned emperors, or the *Deliverance* murder plot.

## Narrative Standard

Use the expanded Earthsea rule on every major scene:

1. Orient the reader in place, time, and participants.
2. Give the viewpoint character an immediate desire or question.
3. Dramatize interaction or action rather than summarizing it away.
4. Show a visible turn.
5. Carry reaction or consequence onto the page.
6. Bridge clearly to the next scene.

For Foundation specifically:

- Translate every abstract idea into a human choice and a visible system.
- Explain not only what changes, but why it matters.
- Keep enough native page text for Francisco and Sebastian to follow the causal chain; hard technical/political pages may need T4-T5 density rather than terse bubbles.
- Do not make psychohistory magical or individually predictive. It models the behavior of very large populations and works in probabilities.
- The Foundation itself remains the long-range protagonist even though each part has a human viewpoint lead.
- Preserve honest time jumps. Captions must state elapsed years and what changed during them.

## Visual Direction

Preserve the shipped Foundation register from `../foundation/01-STYLE-GUIDE.md`:

> 1970s British science-fiction paperback airbrush, monumental scale, smooth gradients and atmospheric depth, saturated teal, magenta, amber and space-black, chrome machinery, serious realistic anatomy.

The expanded edition changes pacing and clarity, not shelf identity. Use approved shipped pages as visual-register references. Preserve 3:2 landscape pages at 1536x1024 unless the user explicitly changes the reader format.

Part One should distinguish three visual states:

- **Imperial Trantor:** overwhelming bronze-teal scale, dense enclosures, amber grandeur, almost no nature.
- **Psychohistory projection:** the same physical world transformed through legible stages of systemic failure; visually spectacular but unmistakably analytical.
- **Trial and exile:** severe dark marble authority giving way to the cold scale of ships departing for the rim.
