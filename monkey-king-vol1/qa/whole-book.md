## 1. Blind account (written before the script was opened)

### Retell
A stone on Flower-Fruit Mountain cracks and a gold monkey climbs out. The troop's elder, Old Ma, puts her hand on his head: "You're here." He is faster and louder than the rest and for a long time nobody minds. At the waterfall she tells the rule: whoever goes through and comes back is king; nobody ever has. He goes. Behind the water is an iron bridge and a dry stone house with room for everyone. She crowns him Handsome Monkey King. That night by the fire he asks how old she is; she has seen three kings and they are all dead — only immortals don't die, and they live far to the west among men. He decides to find one and make him teach, so none of them has to die.

He rafts west, spends three years learning to walk upright and wear a coat, and an old woman points him to a gray mountain with one pine where the Master has taught for forty years and never taught anyone that. The Master refuses, then names him Sun Wukong. He sweeps for seven years, asking once a year, told "not yet." At the seventh year the Master raps him three times on the head and walks off hands behind his back; the students laugh; Wukong alone reads it: third watch, back door. At midnight he is given two things — the shape (become anything, keep your own) and the cloud (one somersault, a hundred thousand miles) — with a warning: use them to be admired and I will send you away alone. His first somersault is just to see. Then he turns into a pine for the students' applause and is expelled: never say you were my student.

Home after ten years and a season, Old Ma reads his face: thrown out. He resolves to make a name of his own; a king needs a weapon; the Dragon King under the eastern sea has a treasury nobody has asked. He walks into the sea. Ao Guang wants him gone, offers sword and halberd (toys), then the pillar that anchors the sea, to be rid of him. The pillar is warm and humming; it shrinks at his word to a needle behind his ear. He takes gold armor, plumed cap, cloud boots, tells Ao Guang to report that he came from a stone and cannot be killed. Ao Guang notices the sea is still up and writes to the Jade Emperor.

In Heaven, Laozi reads the letter; the Emperor says send soldiers; Laozi counters — a title is cheaper than soldiers, a monkey with a title holds still — and is told to fetch him personally. On the peak Wukong is thrilled that Heaven sent for him; he says yes before asking what the post is. Old Ma: you promised to teach us not to die; come back — that's the whole thing I want. The South Gate is open; he decides that means they want him. The Emperor makes him Keeper of the Heavenly Horses without looking at him. For a month he is genuinely good at it and happy. Then he asks Laozi what rank it is; Laozi does not lie: below rank, the post for someone who is not anyone yet. He smashes the desk and goes home.

He paints a banner: GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN. Heaven's whole army comes down and is gone by lunch. Laozi tells the Emperor to give him the words — words cost nothing, a house, no duties. Laozi reads the decree under the banner. Old Ma asks why go back up; because they said his name the way he wrote it and he wants to hear it up there. "Come back." "I always do." The gold house is empty and it feels like the stable with a better roof; the Emperor gives him the peach garden to guard. He eats the top peaches nobody was ever allowed. Laozi comes for banquet peaches and lets slip there is a banquet tonight for every immortal of rank; Wukong asks plainly if he is invited: no — the title was so that he would not need one. He eats the banquet alone, wanders drunk down the red corridor into Laozi's laboratory, eats five gourds of pills thinking they are sweets for Old Ma, and comes out sober and glowing. Laozi, no longer dabbing his brow: no more offers, send everything and send Erlang.

Erlang, three-eyed, with a hound, comes to see if the banner is true; Wukong asks which eye to hit first; Erlang laughs, and they fight — shape for shape, the first person who can keep up. Wukong hides as a temple with the flagpole (his tail) round the back; Erlang kicks the door; Wukong runs ("that was before the dog"). Laozi from above throws his iron ring — the last thing he owns — and it fells him. Chained, Wukong makes Erlang promise to say it took all of them.

Before the throne he refuses to kneel — equals don't kneel, you had it read out. Laozi's furnace burns forty-nine days and returns things to what they are made of; he wants his peaches and elixir back. "I'm made of a rock." Inside, Wukong fights for a day, then finds the one corner where the wind comes in and, for the first time in his life, waits. On day forty-nine the lid blows off; his eyes now hold fire. He walks into the hall and tells the Emperor to come down off the throne. Laozi has nothing left. The Emperor sends west to the Buddha, who owes Heaven nothing. The Buddha asks what he wants the throne for — so they can't ever say I'm nobody — and offers a bet: jump out of my hand and it's yours; fail and you go back to Earth and stay. Wukong somersaults to the end of the world, five pink pillars, signs the middle one and pees on it. Back on the palm, the Buddha turns his hand over: the writing is on his middle finger. Wukong bolts; the hand comes down beside his own mountain and becomes one, a paper seal on top, his head out. Five hundred years. Every year when the peaches come in, an old gray monkey climbs up with one, sits with him, and says the Buddha's words back: someone will come, wait. She does it for as many years as she has; then a younger monkey does, who was told to.

Every link is on the page. No step needs outside knowledge.

### Ending — what it cost and why the last image matters
He wanted to never die, then to never be nobody. He got both: he cannot die, and Heaven and the Buddha both know his name. The cost is five hundred years unable to move, the promise to teach the troop never kept, the banner across the water going pale, and Old Ma — the one who said "come back" every time — spending the rest of her life coming to him instead, until she runs out of years and someone younger does it because she told them to. The final image (her in the snow with a peach; his face in the rock; his own peak behind her) inverts the book's refrain: he cannot come back, so she comes; and the monkey who "never waited for anything" (p41) is now waiting, for a person. The last line is hers and the Buddha's, not his.

## 3. Movement acceptance (answered from pages only)

I (1–7): Wukong acts (dares, goes through, leaves). He wants to see what's behind the water, then to be king, then to not die. Old Ma wants a home and a king for the troop and tells him the truth about kings. Choice: go west. Why: kings die, immortals don't. Affected: the troop; Old Ma knows she caused it (p7). He now believes an immortal can be found and made to teach. Next scene: the raft — because the old woman pointed at the mountain. Passes.
II (8–14): The Master refuses, names, tests, teaches, warns, expels. Wukong wants the secret, then admiration. Choice: show off. Why: he wanted them to see. Affected: himself (nameless as a student), Old Ma (his face was wrong). He now believes he cannot die and must make a name of his own. Next: the Dragon King — Old Ma's line on p14. Passes.
III (15–19): Ao Guang wants him gone and offers the unliftable thing; Wukong wants the best weapon; the pillar obeys. Affected: the sea (still up), Heaven (the letter). He believes he is a king who cannot be killed. Next: Heaven's hall — because the letter arrived and Laozi proposed a title. Passes.
IV (20–29): Laozi wants him still; the Emperor wants quiet; Old Ma wants him back; Wukong wants to be sent for. Choices: stable, then the banner, then back up "because they said it." Affected: Laozi (promises he'll never need the hall), Old Ma (twice: "come back"). He believes the words are his win — and feels the stable again. Next: the garden, handed over by Laozi who knew. Passes.
V (30–34): Wukong wants what nobody was allowed, then an invitation; Laozi wants his peaches and a quiet monkey. Choice: eat the banquet; eat the elixir by mistake. Affected: Laozi's five hundred years; the Emperor's face in front of everyone. He now knows the title meant exclusion. Next: Erlang — because Laozi ends offers. Passes.
VI (35–38): Erlang wants a fight; Wukong wants an equal and gets one. Choice: run from the dog; Laozi's choice to throw the ring. Affected: Wukong chained; Erlang insists on the record. He believes it took all of them and wants it said. Next: the hall. Passes.
VII (39–48): He refuses to kneel (the title is the reason). Laozi wants his pills back; the furnace. Choice inside: to wait. Then: demand the throne "so they can't say I'm nobody." The Buddha's bet; he loses on his own writing. Cost: five hundred years and Old Ma's remaining life. He believes, at the end, that someone will come — because she said it back to him. Passes.
