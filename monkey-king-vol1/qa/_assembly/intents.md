## PAGE 1

Spectacle. Sunrise over an eastern sea, and a green mountain rising out of it, fruit trees down its flanks, a waterfall throwing itself off a cliff. At the bare summit an egg-shaped boulder taller than a man has split down the middle, and light is coming out of the split. A small gold figure is climbing out, one hand on the lip of the stone, looking straight out of the page at the reader. One caption over the sky says where we are and that nobody lives here. The dominant turn is the stone splitting and a monkey with light in his eyes. Wukong owns the page and does not yet know he is anyone. The landscape is vast and the figure small, but the eyes find you. The reader turns because something just climbed out of a rock and it is looking at them. The mountain's beauty stays subordinate to that gaze; nothing else alive.

## PAGE 2

Illustrated prose. On the slopes under a fruit tree, a small gold monkey stands in a crowd of brown ones, and the birds have left the tree because of the light in his eyes. Old Ma, gray, stooped, in her faded red shawl, has laid her hand flat on top of his head, and he is looking up at her. The prose field below carries the years: he has no mother, he has a rock that will not close, the troop comes to look, Old Ma says he is here, and he grows, faster and louder than any of them, not knowing what he is. The dominant turn is that he has no mother and the troop takes him, sealed by Old Ma's hand. Old Ma owns the page; Wukong receives it. The reader turns because he does not know what he is, and neither do they. The crowd stays subordinate to the one hand on the one gold head.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. A hot day at the pool below the waterfall, monkeys crowded on every rock. Old Ma, on the highest rock under her shawl, says nobody knows where the water comes from; Wukong, gold at the front of the crowd, says somebody should go and look. She names the prize: whoever goes through and comes back alive is made king, and nobody has ever done it. He checks the terms, king of all of them, and she confirms it. Nobody else moves. He goes, a gold streak into the white water, and the crowd shrieks. The dominant turn is the dare and the prize, and him going through the water before anyone can stop him. Wukong owns the page; Old Ma sets it in motion. The reader turns to see what is behind the water. The crowd and the rocks stay subordinate to the exchange and the leap.

## PAGE 4

Spectacle. From inside the waterfall: the falling water is a wall of silver seen from behind, an iron bridge crosses the pool, and beyond it a whole house is cut into the mountain, stone beds, stone bowls, stone stools, a stone hearth, all dry, all empty, big enough for hundreds. Wukong is tiny on the bridge, dripping, mouth open. One caption over the dry floor names what he has found. The dominant turn is the Water Curtain Cave itself, a stone house behind the fall. The place owns the page; Wukong is its first witness, and his smallness is the measure of its size. The reader turns because there is a whole home behind the waterfall and nobody has ever lived in it. No other monkey, no fire, no fruit, nothing that would make it already lived in; the emptiness is the point.

## PAGE 5

Dramatic. Wukong bursts back out through the water onto the rocks, drenched, shouting about the house before he has landed. Then the whole troop pours across the iron bridge into the cave, monkeys bouncing on stone beds and trying bowls on their heads, Old Ma crossing slowly on her stick, Wukong already up on the tallest stone stool with his arms out. She says a rule is a rule and tells him to bow his head. Her hand goes flat on his head; she names him Handsome Monkey King; he swears he will never tire of it; the crowd roars. The dominant turn is that he leads them in and Old Ma crowns him, and the joy of it. Wukong owns the page; Old Ma gives it. The reader turns because he is king of everything he can see, and this is the happiest anyone in the book will ever be. The furniture and the bowls stay subordinate.

## PAGE 6

Dramatic. Night, the feast over, a fire in the stone hearth, fruit skins everywhere, monkeys asleep in heaps. Wukong on his stool, laughing at nothing, asks Old Ma how old she is. She has seen three kings before him, and none went through the water. His face changes as he asks where they are, and she tells him without looking at him: kings die too, only the immortals don't. He wants to know who they are and where. Stories put them far to the west, over the sea, in the world of men; she never met one. He stands on the stool, eyes wet, fists shut, and says he will find one and make him teach him, so none of them has to die. The dominant turn is kings die too, his tears, and his decision to go. Old Ma owns the truth; Wukong owns the answer. The reader turns because he has just found out that he ends, and his answer is to leave. The fire and the sleepers stay subordinate to her not looking at him.

## PAGE 7

Illustrated prose, and the one page in this movement with two places, because the turn is the crossing itself. A raft of pine logs on an enormous gray-green sea, one small figure standing on it, not looking back, the green mountain going small behind him. Below, a monkey in a man's coat that is too big for him walks a dirt road on two legs toward a gray mountain with a single pine. The prose field carries the raft, Old Ma on the shore not telling him to stay because she knows she set him going, the season at sea, the years among men, the coat, the question asked of every old person, and the one old woman who points at the mountain where the Master has never taught anyone the thing he is asking for. The dominant turn is the raft, the sea, the years among men, and the Master's mountain. Wukong owns the page, alone. The reader turns because the Master has never taught it and the monkey has never taken no. The road stays subordinate to the small figure on the wide water.

## PAGE 8

Dramatic. Dawn in the Master's courtyard: white raked gravel, one pine, a cave mouth in a gray cliff, and a small bare monkey at the gate who has walked here from the far side of the sea. Subhuti, on the cave step, refuses him before he has asked and tells him to go home to his parents. The monkey has none. He came out of a rock, and he says so, flatly, as a fact anyone could go and check. That answer turns the page's weather: the Master's hooded eyes stop and consider. Asked what he wants, the monkey says the true thing, to not die, and offers to sweep the whole mountain for it. Subhuti turns away and over his shoulder gives him what nobody ever has: a name, Sun Wukong. The dominant turn is the rock-born answer and the naming. The staring students stay subordinate. The reader turns because the first gift of his life was his name.

## PAGE 9

Illustrated prose. Years pass in one image: the same courtyard in blossom, heat, falling leaves, and snow, and in every season the same small gold figure with a broom. In the last he sits at the back of a row of students with the broom across his knees, leaning forward, listening. The prose carries the weight: he swept for seven years, chopped and carried and sat at the back, and listened so hard his ears hurt; the students wrote and he did not, and remembering turned out to be better. He did not learn the thing he came for. Once a year he asked, and the Master said not yet. The dominant turn is the years of the broom and the monkey learning to listen. Nothing dramatic happens and nothing should; the page is patient by design. Subhuti, if seen, is small and far. The reader turns because seven times not yet makes them want to see what yes looks like.

## PAGE 10

Dramatic. Lessons on the gravel. Subhuti asks Wukong, at the back and bouncing on his heels, what he has learned in seven years; Wukong says every word, but not the thing he came for. Then the Master walks down between the rows and brings the handle of his whisk down on the monkey's head, three times, says he has learned nothing, and the students laugh behind their hands. That is the dominant turn: three taps on the head, the students laugh, and he alone understands. Subhuti walks away into the cave with his hands clasped behind his back, the students howl, and Wukong sits very still with one hand on his head, eyes wide, a small smile starting, reading the taps as a message: midnight, the third watch, the back door. The small round door in the cliff, shut, is quietly present. The reader turns because everyone laughed at the monkey and he was the only one who got the joke.

## PAGE 11

Dramatic. Midnight inside the cave, one candle. The round back door opens and Wukong slips in, tail first, and Subhuti, seated and waiting, tells him he is the first in forty years to understand a simple thing. Then the lesson, which owns the page: two fingers on the monkey's forehead, a bronze basin of water between them, and in the water the monkey's reflection becoming a hawk and then a pine. The shape, and the cloud. Wukong asks about dying, and the answer is the whole of immortality in one plain sentence: a thing that can be anything and be anywhere is very hard to kill. The dominant turn is the shape-change and the cloud, and their price. The price comes last, with the whisk raised for the flick that ends things: use them to be admired and I will send you away alone. Wukong, half out the door, grinning, swears he never would. The reader turns because he has been told exactly what not to do and is already grinning.

## PAGE 12

Spectacle. One image, no panels, almost no words. Dawn high over the western sea: a tiny gold figure upside down in mid-somersault on a puff of white cloud, the curve of the sea far below him, the sun enormous beside him. Far behind and below, the gray mountain with its single pine, already small. The dominant turn is the first somersault, over the sea, at the sun. One short caption over the sea says what this is: one somersault, a hundred thousand miles, and the first one he did just to see. Nothing else competes; the scale of sky and sun against the smallness of the monkey is the whole event. The reader turns because he can go anywhere now, and the first thing he does with that is show off.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Morning in the courtyard. A pine tree stands in the middle of the raked gravel where no pine was, and the pine has a tail, and the students in a ring around it are clapping. The pine unwinds into Wukong, bowing with his arms out, asking for something harder. Subhuti on the step says one word, his name, and his face is stone. That is the dominant turn: the pine tree, then never say you were my student, then out. Subhuti points at the gate. He said what would happen. Take the shape and the cloud and go, and wherever you go, never say you were his student. Wukong starts to explain what he only wanted and is cut off: I know what you wanted. Go. The students backing away are subordinate; Wukong very small is the last image. The reader turns because the one who gave him a name has told him never to say it, and he has to go home like that.

## PAGE 14

Dramatic. Home in one leap. A cloud comes down onto the peak of Flower-Fruit Mountain beside the split stone, monkeys pour up the slopes, and Old Ma is on the rock with her stick and red shawl and one flat hand over her eyes, his own gesture. Wukong jumps off the cloud before it lands, shouting that he did it, he cannot die, he will teach every one of them. The dominant turn is the homecoming, Old Ma, and the dragon king under the sea. Her hand flat on his head, she says the thing he did not: you were thrown out. She read it in his face. Never mind; he came back. Then he is up on the split stone looking east, hand over his eyes: fine, he will make a name of his own, and a king needs a weapon. Old Ma tells him the old ones spoke of the Dragon King's treasury under the eastern sea, and nobody has ever gone and asked. The reader turns because that sentence gets this monkey moving.

## PAGE 15

Spectacle. Wukong walks down into the Eastern Sea as if it were a staircase, upright and unhurried, green water giving way to blue around him, fish parting to let him pass. Far below, on the sand, a palace of crystal and white shell glows in the dark, and at the very back of it burns one dull red point, the only warm thing in the whole sea. The page belongs to Wukong's confidence: the sea does not resist him, and the reader feels the sheer drop between the small monkey and the vast palace that has no idea he is coming. One caption over open water carries the whole idea. The dominant turn is the descent itself, and the discovery of the crystal palace below. The fish, the coral, and the palace's ornament stay subordinate to the single image of a monkey walking downward through water toward a red glow. Nothing is spoken; nobody is met yet.

## PAGE 16

Dramatic. Ao Guang, the Dragon King, receives a dry monkey in his crystal hall and wants him gone politely. Wukong, tiny before the coral throne with one foot on its lowest step, announces himself as the Monkey King and demands the best weapon in the sea. The guards bring a sword and a halberd; he drops one and bends the other across his knee, unimpressed, while Ao Guang's fingers drum on the throne. Then Ao Guang leans forward with a slow smile and points back into the treasury toward the red glow, offering the one thing nobody has ever lifted, to be rid of him. The page belongs to Ao Guang's weary courtesy against Wukong's appetite. The dominant turn is the audience itself: the weapons are too light, and the pillar is named. The shrimp and crab guards and the coral furniture stay subordinate.

## PAGE 17

Dramatic. In the treasury, Wukong lays a hand flat on the black iron pillar and finds it warm and humming, as if it had been waiting for him. Then he talks to it, and it obeys: the pillar shrinks under his hand, gold bands sliding together, the sea floor shuddering, fish fleeing, Ao Guang's whiskers standing straight out in alarm. By the end it is a needle between finger and thumb, and he tucks it behind his ear while Ao Guang comes off his throne for the first time in the book to ask whether he knows what he has just done. He does: he has a staff. The page belongs to Wukong, and the world responding to him. The dominant turn is the pillar obeying him, growing smaller under his voice. Ao Guang's alarm frames it but does not own it. The treasury's other treasures stay subordinate.

## PAGE 18

Dramatic. Wukong is back before the throne with the staff full-size over his shoulder, and now he wants armor to go with it: gold, a plumed cap, boots that stick to clouds. Ao Guang asks who he should say took it, and Wukong gives his name and his claim: he came out of a stone and cannot be killed. Then the reader sees him armored for the first time, gold scale over red, tiger-skin, cloud-boots, two long plumes, turning in the water to admire himself in a crystal column, delighted. The page belongs to Wukong's joy in his new self. The dominant turn is the armor: the Monkey King now looks like one. What follows is aftermath: the hall empty, Ao Guang alone at a shell desk writing to Heaven, drily noting that the sea is still up, and sending the monkey's name upward for the first time. The guards retrieving the dropped sword stay subordinate.

## PAGE 19

Dramatic. Heaven, for the first time: the Hall of Jade, a throne room the size of a valley, and the Jade Emperor seated high with two fingers at his temple, wanting only quiet. Laozi, round and busy, stands on the step below with the Dragon King's shell letter, dabbing his brow, and reports that a monkey out of a stone has taken the pillar that holds the sea down, a monkey called Wukong. The Emperor shuts his eyes and orders soldiers; he does not want to hear the name again. Then Laozi turns with an idea, one finger up: give the monkey a small job up here, because a monkey with a title holds still, and titles cost less than soldiers. The Emperor agrees and sends Laozi to fetch him personally. The page belongs to the two men and the step between them. The dominant turn is Heaven deciding to manage the monkey. The hall's grandeur stays subordinate.

## PAGE 20

Dramatic. Heaven comes to the peak: Laozi, round and quick, hovering on a small cloud with a gold scroll, tells the armored monkey that the Jade Emperor has heard of him and is offering him a post. Wukong owns the page — he says yes before he asks what the job is, and the reader sees him already climbing onto the cloud. The dominant turn is Laozi's invitation and Old Ma's answer to it: she asks the one question he will not ask, "What is the post?", and then asks for the only thing she wants, that he come back. What changes: he is leaving again, this time invited. The reader turns because he took the offer blind and Old Ma noticed. Subordinate: the crowd of monkeys, Laozi's scroll, the scenery of the peak. Laozi is polite and warm and never touches the ground.

## PAGE 21

Spectacle. One image: the South Gate of Heaven, a gate the size of a city, gold on white cloud, its doors standing open, tower-sized guards on either side with faceplates down, and the palaces of Heaven going up forever behind it. At the very bottom, Laozi's small cloud arriving with a monkey in gold armor on it, mouth open, plumes tiny against the gate. The dominant turn is scale: this is the biggest thing he has ever seen. One caption owns the page's meaning — the gate was open, nobody stopped him, and he decided that meant they wanted him. What changes: he has entered Heaven and already believes it is his. ## PAGE 22

Dramatic. The Hall of Jade. Wukong stands on the lowest step of the throne, plumes up, staring around; the Emperor sits far above, not looking at him, fingers at his temple; Laozi stands between them on a middle step. The dominant turn is the appointment: Keeper of the Heavenly Horses, granted without a glance, and Wukong is thrilled by it. He asks whether it is a high post; Laozi answers a different question, "It is a post in Heaven," and the reader hears the evasion even though Wukong does not. What changes: the monkey has a job in Heaven and has already mistaken it for honor. The Emperor owns the decision; Wukong owns the joy; Laozi owns the dodge. The reader turns because the question was not answered. Subordinate: the hall's size, the pillars, the water floor.

## PAGE 23

Illustrated prose. The Heavenly stables: a long stable of white wood under a cloud roof, a thousand horses of every colour down its length, and Wukong asleep on the back of a black horse, plumes hanging over one ear, another horse eating from his open hand. A small desk by the door with a ledger on it, untouched. The dominant turn is happiness: he is good at this, the horses love him, and for a month he does not ask what rank he is. The prose field carries the page — his month of honest work, the wild ones ridden until they laughed, the frightened ones walked until they slept, the fat shining horses that were the ledger. ## PAGE 24

Dramatic. The stables. Laozi visits, admiring a fat horse; Wukong sits at the little desk with his feet up and asks, since everyone is talking, what rank Keeper of the Heavenly Horses is. The dominant turn is Laozi's answer, given without a lie: it is not a rank, it is below rank, the post one gives to someone who is not anyone yet. Laozi's face owns the page at that moment. Then the desk is in two pieces, the ledger is in the air, the horses rear, the staff is out at full length, and Wukong says the word — stable boy — and that down there he is a king. Laozi tries "it is a beginning" and is cut off. What changes: the wound is open and he is going home with it. The reader turns to see what he does with it. Subordinate: the horses, the hay, the wreckage.

## PAGE 25

Dramatic. Home on the peak. Wukong lands hard and tells Old Ma the truth — lord of horses, of nothing, they laughed at him quietly, which is worse. The dominant turn is the banner: he paints huge characters on red cloth while monkeys hold it flat and a pole goes up beside the split stone, and the words are readable — GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN. He asks Old Ma what you call someone Heaven cannot order around; she says there is no word for it; he says there is now. Then the banner flies over the peak and the troop shouts under it, Old Ma sitting beneath the pole with her knees up, saying they will come for that. He wants them to. What changes: he has written himself a title Heaven never gave him, in letters Heaven can read from the sky. The reader turns to see who comes. Subordinate: the crowd, the pole, the brush.

## PAGE 26

Spectacle. From the peak, looking up: the clouds torn open and Heaven's army pouring down in a spiral, thousands of soldiers in black lacquered armor with faceplates down, spears like rain. On the peak, one small gold figure under the banner, the staff grown to the size of a tree trunk in his hands. The dominant turn is the army's arrival against one monkey — and, in a strip across the bottom, the army going back up in a hurry, a few helmets falling. One caption owns the joke and the scale: they brought everyone, and it took him until lunch. What changes: Heaven's whole army has come and gone, and lost. The reader turns because Heaven now has to have a meeting about it. Subordinate: any individual soldier, any individual blow; the banner stays visible.

## PAGE 27

Dramatic. The Hall of Jade, and the Emperor is on his feet for the first time in the book, beads swinging, demanding of Laozi how his whole army lost to one monkey with a stick. Laozi, both hands up, owns the solution: he said the wrong title; give the monkey the one he wrote, Great Sage Equal to Heaven. The dominant turn is the Emperor's disgust — equal to Heaven, equal to me — answered by Laozi's cheapest argument: it is words, words cost nothing, give him the words, a house, no duties, and he will sit in it. The Emperor sits back down, fingers to his temple, and grants it on one condition: keep him out of this hall. Laozi promises he will never need to come in. What changes: Heaven has given a monkey the title Equal to Heaven as a way of not thinking about him. The reader turns to see it delivered. Wukong is absent.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. Laozi alone on his small cloud under the flying banner, reading a gold scroll to the whole troop: Sun Wukong is named Great Sage Equal to Heaven, with a house in Heaven and no duties — the monkey's own words, read off his banner. Wukong sits on the split stone, arms folded, staff across his knees, looking at the scroll. The dominant turn is the reading itself: Heaven saying his name the way he wrote it. Old Ma asks the sane question — you beat them, you have a mountain and a name, why go back up? His answer is the whole character: because they said it, and he wants to hear them say it up there. He steps onto the cloud; her hand is flat on his head; she says the two words again. What changes: he is going back to the people who laughed at him. The reader turns on "Come back" and "I always do."

## PAGE 29

Illustrated prose. A gold-roofed mansion on a cloud in Heaven, and Wukong sitting on the roof ridge with the staff across his knees, plumes drooping, looking out at the palaces going up forever; far away, terraces of peach blossom on a hill. The dominant turn is boredom and the handover: they gave him the words and a house and nothing to do in it, nobody visits, nobody sends for him, and winning feels like the stable with a better roof. Then the Emperor, wanting him busy, finds him a duty — a garden of peach trees to guard. The prose field owns the page; its last sentence is the trap: it was, Laozi did not say, a garden full of peaches, and he was a monkey. What changes: a monkey has been put in charge of the peaches, and Laozi knew. The reader turns to watch it happen. Subordinate: the architecture of the mansion, the distant hall.

## PAGE 30

Illustrated prose. The peach garden in late-afternoon light: three terraces of peach trees in three colours of blossom, and at the top, in the fork of the biggest tree, Wukong on his back with a peach the size of his head. The prose does the telling. The bottom trees make you wise, the middle ones make you live forever, and nobody remembers what the top ones do because nobody was ever allowed to eat one. He started at the top. The dominant turn is that he eats, one a day and then one an hour, and the stones pile up under the tree where the gardeners will find them, and he does not care, because it is his garden and nobody said not to. Wukong owns the page alone. The reader turns because someone is about to come and count. The blossom and the terraces stay subordinate to one monkey in one tree with one peach.

## PAGE 31

Dramatic. Laozi comes up the terrace with a basket and finds peach stones on the path, from the top trees that nobody in Heaven was ever allowed. Two gold feet hang out of the biggest tree. He needs forty peaches for his elixir and forty for tonight, and the word slips. Wukong drops out of the tree to land in front of him and asks what tonight is: the Emperor's peach banquet, every immortal of rank. The dominant turn is Wukong's question, that he is Equal to Heaven, that is a rank, where is his invitation, and Laozi's answer, that the title was so he would not need one. Wukong goes very still, without a smile, and asks it plainly. Laozi says no. Laozi owns the first half; Wukong takes the page at the stillness. ## PAGE 32

Dramatic. The Hall of Jade laid for the banquet and empty of guests: long tables, trays of peaches, jars of wine, covered dishes, the throne bare at the far end. The reader turns because he is drunk and has walked toward the one room in Heaven he should not enter. The feast's inventory stays subordinate to one monkey on one table.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic. Laozi's laboratory: a round room of black iron and cinnabar red, shelves of gourds and jars, the bronze furnace taller than a man at the center. Wukong sways in the doorway with his jar, recognizes the peach man's place, and goes looking. The dominant turn is the five gourds on the highest shelf, unstoppered, gold pills pouring into his palm like sweets, his face lit gold from below, eating them by the handful and planning to take some home to Old Ma. Wukong owns the page alone; Laozi is not here. The reader turns because he ate five hundred years of Laozi's work by accident, and it worked. The furnace is scenery and stays subordinate.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. The Hall of Jade wrecked after the banquet: tables over, trays empty, robed guests standing about with nothing to do, the Emperor at the throne. Laozi arrives at a run with five empty gourds and tells the Emperor where he was: counting. The monkey ate the elixir, five hundred years of work. The dominant turn is Laozi's face changing. The Emperor asks what now, another title, a bigger house, and the man who invented every offer says no more offers; he cannot be killed and cannot be managed; send everything, and send Erlang. Laozi owns the page; the Emperor's touch is the aftermath. The reader turns to meet the one person in Heaven who will enjoy this. The guests and the army stay subordinate to two faces.

## PAGE 35

Dramatic. The sky over Flower-Fruit Mountain, storm-lit, Heaven's army hanging in a ring of cloud around the peak and not attacking. In front of it, alone on a cloud, stands the one person Laozi said would enjoy this: Erlang, young, in silver armor with a single red plume, an eye open in the middle of his forehead, a lance upright, a lean gray hound at his heel. Then lance and staff meet, the shock throws the cloud outward, and Erlang says "Good." The dominant turn: Erlang introduces himself; the fight begins. The army stays scenery.

## PAGE 36

Spectacle, nearly silent. The duel of shapes, read top to bottom, from the sky over Flower-Fruit Mountain down to the pool under the waterfall. High up, a gold sparrow dives with a hawk on its tail, and the hawk has an eye in its forehead. In the pool, a gold fish flashes under the surface and a cormorant with a red crest strikes down through the water; this middle image owns the page. In the reeds at the pool's edge, a gold water snake, and a crane with a red plume stepping down onto it, one foot raised. ## PAGE 37

Dramatic, and funny. The slope of Flower-Fruit Mountain below the peak. A small temple stands where no temple was: red doors, a round window, an offering box, and behind it a flagpole that is furred, with a tuft on the end. Erlang, human again, walks round it with the lance on his shoulder, admiring it aloud, then says he has never seen a temple with the flagpole round the back. The hound sniffs the pole. The round window blinks and says it is a new style. Erlang announces he will kick the door in and hit whatever is inside. The temple bursts back into a monkey mid-leap, running up the slope toward the peak, Erlang after him, the hound after both, the two of them shouting about whether he said he never runs. The dominant turn: the temple and the flagpole; he runs. Something above him in the clouds has been waiting for exactly this.

## PAGE 38

Dramatic. The peak of Flower-Fruit Mountain. Wukong runs, looking back at Erlang, not up. High in the clouds, Laozi on his own small cloud holds out a dull iron ring the size of a bracelet, unsmiling, and asks forgiveness: he offered everything Heaven had, and this is the last thing he has that is his. The ring, grown to a cartwheel, comes down on the back of Wukong's head. He pitches forward, the staff flies from his hand, the hound is already in the air. Erlang wants it understood this was not him; Wukong counts them all, army, Erlang, dog, bracelet, and wants that said in the hall. The dominant turn: the ring from behind; the hound; chains; it took all of you.

## PAGE 39

Dramatic. The Hall of Jade, the whole court watching. Wukong stands in chains before the throne, the iron ring off his head, held by faceless soldiers, his staff lying on the lowest step where they dropped it, a tray of empty gourds beside the throne. Erlang, lance grounded, the hound at his heel, makes his report: it took all of them, and the monkey wanted that said. The Emperor rises to his feet for the second time in his life and uses the title he granted to order the monkey to kneel. Wukong owns the page: chin up, the soldiers' hands pressing on his shoulders and getting nowhere, he tells the Emperor of Heaven that he gave him the title, that equals do not kneel, and that he had it read out in front of everyone. Laozi stands silent at the Emperor's elbow. The dominant turn is the refusal. The reader turns because somebody has to answer that.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic. The Hall of Jade, a moment later. Laozi owns the page: he comes down to the chained monkey's level, quiet, no brow-dabbing, the tray of empty gourds in his hands, and explains the furnace, which burns for forty-nine days and returns whatever goes in to what it was made of. Wukong is made of Laozi's top peaches and his elixir, and Laozi wants them back. Wukong, alone and flat, answers that he is made of a rock, as if that helps. Then the throne: the Emperor, fingers to his temple, eyes shut, while Laozi looks up at him and says they will get a rock, and he will get his pills back. The Emperor says do it, and this time it works. The dominant turn is the furnace sentence. Erlang and the court stay subordinate. The reader turns because they are going to burn him for forty-nine days.

## PAGE 41

Illustrated prose. Dominant turn: forty-nine days; the corner where the wind is; waiting. The page belongs to the dark. Inside the furnace there is nothing but black and one thin thread of gray light coming under the lid, and a monkey's silhouette curled around that thread in a corner, eyes shut, tail over his nose. Outside, the bronze furnace glows at the seams, the lid shut, Laozi feeding the fire with his sleeve over his face. The prose carries the time: he hit the walls, changed into a fish and a bird, found nothing, and on the second day stopped and felt along the bottom of the dark for the one place the fire did not reach. He had never waited for anything. He waited. He counted the days by Laozi's footsteps, and on the forty-eighth he stood up. Laozi stays subordinate. The reader turns because the lid is next.

## PAGE 42

Spectacle. Dominant turn: the lid kicked off; golden eyes; Heaven breaks. Two locations, because the turn is the transition. The furnace lid blows straight up, the bronze splits, fire and smoke pour out, and a monkey rises in scorched gold armor with the chains falling off him in pieces. His eyes are gold now, with a thin red ring of fire around each. Below, the Hall of Jade seen from its doorway: pillars cracking, robed immortals scattering, the Emperor's beads swinging, and the staff leaping up off the throne steps by itself into the monkey's open hand. One sound and one caption; the caption names what went in and what came out, and that the fire in his eyes stayed. Everything else is subordinate to the rising figure and the new eyes. The reader turns because Heaven's own furnace just made him stronger, and Heaven is out of ideas.

## PAGE 43

Dramatic. Dominant turn: the Emperor at his own throne: "send for the Buddha." The Emperor is pressed back against his throne at the top of the steps; Wukong stands three steps below with the staff, armor scorched, plumes singed, eyes gold; Laozi is on the floor among the empty gourds with nothing in his hands; the pillars lean. Wukong lists every idea they had, stable boy, empty house, no invitation, a furnace, says he is still here, and tells the Emperor to come down so he can sit in the throne a while. The Emperor looks to Laozi, who for the first time has no answer. Then the Emperor, eyes closed, like a man giving up a secret, says send west to the Buddha. Wukong asks who that is. Laozi says: the one who owes Heaven nothing, whom Heaven has never asked for anything, until now. The reader turns because the Emperor just sent for that being.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. Dominant turn: the wager. The Buddha is here, seated, filling the far end of the hall so that the throne sits beside his knee: saffron over one shoulder, a bare curled head, a small smile, one hand open on his knee. Wukong is tiny on the floor before that hand with his staff up. Everyone else has gone to the walls. The Buddha asks what he wants the throne for; Wukong says so they can never say he is nobody, it goes to the strongest, and that is him. The Buddha's hand opens flat, palm up, the size of a courtyard, and he offers a small bet: jump out of the hand and the throne is his; fail, and he goes back to Earth and stays. Wukong climbs onto the palm laughing, looking back at the Emperor: big, but not that big. The Buddha says then go. The reader turns because he is about to leap.

## PAGE 45

Spectacle. Dominant turn: the five pillars at the edge of the world; the name; the mark. White. A flat white nothing with no horizon, and five pink pillars rising out of it into a white sky, tapering, taller than sight. At the foot of the middle one, a tiny gold figure scratches red letters into it with the needle from behind his ear. Then the middle pillar close: the red letters, the monkey with his back to the reader, one hand at his belt, a small splash at the pillar's foot, his face over his shoulder, delighted. The pillar carries his title and that he was here. A caption says this is the end of everything; he signed the middle pillar, and then, to be sure, he marked it. The white is the point. The reader turns because he has signed the end of the world and is going back to collect.

## PAGE 46

Dramatic. Dominant turn: the hand turns over; the writing on the finger; he runs. Wukong lands back on the open palm, bowing to the walls, and tells them to send someone to look. The Buddha says he does not need to; look down. The page belongs to the hand turning over, slowly, enormous: on the middle finger at the second joint, the same red letters, a faint wet patch beneath them, and Wukong's golden eyes on it. He says that is his writing. The Buddha says that is the rest of it; he never left the hand. Wukong bolts off the palm toward the door, staff and plumes flat out behind him, shouting that it is a trick; the hand rises above him, open, coming down, and the Emperor watches from behind the beads. The Buddha tells him to hold still, and wait. The reader turns because he has never held still in his life.

## PAGE 47

Spectacle. Dominant turn: the hand comes down: a mountain with a face in it. One image. The hand comes down out of the sky over the coast beside Flower-Fruit Mountain and becomes, as it lands, a mountain of five bare ridges like fingers pressed into the earth. Across the water the green peak is small, the split stone and the red banner tiny on top of it. At the base of the new mountain there is a cleft, and in the cleft a small gold-furred face with its eyes open. A gold paper seal flutters down to rest on the summit. The single caption says the hand came down beside his own mountain and became one, a paper seal followed to hold it shut, and he fit under it with his head out. The reader turns because he is under a mountain, his eyes are open, and the book has one page left.

## PAGE 48

Illustrated prose. Dominant turn: five hundred years; snow; Old Ma's peach; someone will come. Winter, the five ridges under snow, the monkey's face in the cleft, fur frosted, eyes open and still gold. Climbing the last of the slope on her stick, a small gray monkey in a red shawl with a peach in her free hand. Across the water, the green peak, the split stone, and the banner gone pale pink. The prose carries the years: the mountain did not move and neither could he; he could see his own peak and the banner fading; he could not reach the needle behind his ear. Every year an old gray monkey climbed up with a peach and said the Buddha's words back to him, for as many years as she had, and after her a younger monkey who had been told to. Someone will come. Wait. The reader closes the book on a monkey waiting, for the first time, for someone.
