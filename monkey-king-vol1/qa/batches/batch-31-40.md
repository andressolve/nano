# Sequence gate — canonical pages 31–40

## Blind uninterrupted sequence

Wukong finds the peach stones and learns that Heaven is preparing the Emperor's peach banquet. His teasing questions become a direct demand to know whether he was invited; the answer is no. The exclusion wounds his pride, and he enters the empty banquet hall resentful that every ranked immortal has been included while the food sits unused. He eats and drinks the feast himself, then leaves looking for more.

He slips into Laozi's guarded rooms, mistakes the contents of five gourds for sweets, eats them all, and realizes too late that they were Laozi's elixir. Back in the ruined hall, Laozi reports the loss to the Emperor and explains that Wukong consumed five hundred years of work. The Emperor abandons further offers and sends his nephew Erlang, with Erlang's third eye, hound, and forces, to bring Wukong in.

Erlang meets Wukong openly on the mountain. Their taunts establish mutual nerve and even a flash of respect: Erlang is pleased that Wukong does not simply flee, and Wukong is pleased to be challenged directly. They clash, then match each other through a sustained series of animal transformations. Erlang finally spots Wukong disguised as a small temple because the temple's odd rear flagpole and living eye betray him. When Erlang threatens the door, Wukong breaks disguise and runs; Erlang and the hound pursue, and Wukong jokes that the dog changes the terms of his earlier refusal to run.

Laozi then throws the last possession that is truly his, a metal bracelet, and strikes Wukong from behind. The hound brings the stunned Wukong down and the surrounding force chains him. Wukong objects to the underhanded capture, but Erlang plainly credits Laozi, the army, the hound, and the bracelet rather than claiming a solo victory. Wukong asks him to repeat that truth in the hall.

Erlang does so before the Emperor. Ordered to kneel, Wukong refuses because the publicly granted title “Great Sage Equal to Heaven” makes kneeling a contradiction. Laozi proposes putting him in the furnace for forty-nine days: if Wukong is made of the stolen top peaches and elixir, the furnace should return those materials. Wukong insists that he is made of rock. Laozi accepts the wager—then he will recover his pills and Heaven will recover a rock—and the exasperated Emperor authorizes it, warning Laozi that this attempt must work.

## Script comparison and sequence judgment

The visible sequence preserves the material story without a causal break. The exclusion on page 31 directly motivates the banquet violation on page 32; drunken wandering causes the elixir accident on page 33; the combined losses cause Laozi and the Emperor to send Erlang on page 34; the challenge develops through combat, matched transformations, and Wukong's failed disguise on pages 35–37; Laozi's bracelet and the hound make the capture possible on page 38; and Erlang's promised truthful accounting leads directly into Wukong's refusal to kneel and Laozi's furnace proposal on pages 39–40.

The scripted text is present with the intended speakers and remains intelligible across panel and page transitions. Wukong, Laozi, the Jade Emperor, and Erlang retain distinct identities and motives. Erlang's third eye, red plume, lance, and hound make him readable through the fight, while Wukong's gold/red form language makes the transformation montage legible against Erlang's gray/red forms.

Consequential objects and settings carry through: the top peaches lead to the banquet and return in Laozi's furnace argument; the five elixir gourds move from the laboratory to the ruined hall and then the throne; Wukong's staff passes from the duel to the capture and lies at the throne; Laozi's bracelet causes the fall and remains visible at court; and the chains applied at capture persist through judgment. Flower-Fruit Mountain's split peak, Wukong's banner, the Hall of Jade, and Laozi's laboratory remain sufficiently differentiated and continuous.

The focal line stays with Wukong's injured pride, comic excess, excitement at meeting an equal, evasive wit, and chained defiance. Erlang's enjoyment of the contest and insistence on honest credit form a coherent secondary line rather than displacing that focus. Laozi's change from accommodation to decisive action is earned by the losses shown.

The batch does not read as compressed summary. Page 36's spectacle montage gives the sustained confrontation a visual and rhythmic expansion, while the surrounding dramatic pages vary among discovery, comedy, institutional fallout, challenge, pursuit, capture, and judgment. The run is not a wall of equivalent dramatic scenes. Consequential beats receive aftermaths: the exclusion produces retaliation, the feast and elixir losses produce mobilization, the duel produces a multi-party capture, and the capture produces both public accounting and the furnace decision. The page turns consistently advance or answer the prior page's pressure.
