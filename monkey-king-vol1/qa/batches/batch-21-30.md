## Blind sequence record

Wukong reaches the open South Gate with Laozi and takes the lack of resistance
as proof that Heaven wants him. In the Hall of Jade, the Jade Emperor assigns
him to keep the Heavenly Horses; Wukong hears “Heavenly” and assumes the job
must carry status. He spends a month making the neglected horses healthy and
happy, discovers that he is genuinely good at the work, and enjoys it enough
to postpone asking about rank.

When Laozi finally tells him the post is below rank, Wukong's pride turns that
contentment into humiliation. He smashes the stable desk, abandons the job,
and returns to Flower-Fruit Mountain. There he admits to Old Ma that Heaven's
quiet laughter wounded him, then converts the wound into defiance by naming
himself Great Sage Equal to Heaven and raising the title on a banner. Heaven
sends its army; Wukong defeats it before lunch.

The defeated court answers force with appeasement. Laozi persuades the Jade
Emperor to grant Wukong the exact title he invented, plus a house in Heaven and
no duties. Laozi carries that decree to Flower-Fruit Mountain. Old Ma points
out that Wukong already has a mountain and a name, but official recognition
matters more to him than the victory itself, so he returns to Heaven after
promising that he will come back.

The granted title proves hollow: Wukong sits alone on his new roof, neither
visited nor needed. Heaven eventually gives him a duty guarding the peach
garden. Treating his title and assignment as ownership, he begins with the
rarest peaches and escalates from one a day to one an hour, leaving the stones
where the gardeners will find them and feeling no need to hide what he has
done.

## Script comparison and sequence judgment

- The material story, exact text, and speaker attribution match the supplied
  script across the batch.
- The causal and emotional chain remains legible at every turn: mistaken
  welcome leads to mistaken status; real fulfillment is broken by humiliation;
  humiliation produces the self-made title; victory forces official
  recognition; recognition draws Wukong back; the empty reward creates the
  peach-garden assignment; and entitlement produces the theft.
- Sun Wukong, Laozi, the Jade Emperor, and Old Ma remain visually and verbally
  distinct. The staff, stable desk and ledger, title banner, decree, heavenly
  residence, and peach garden retain their consequential functions across page
  turns. Movement between Heaven and Flower-Fruit Mountain is clear.
- Focal integrity holds. The brief court-only aftermath on page 27 supplies
  the consequence of Wukong's victory, then returns directly to his decision
  on page 28.
- Page turns pay off their preceding setups and generate the next question.
  No consequential beat lacks aftermath: the rank revelation is followed by
  Wukong's confession and counterclaim; the army's defeat is followed by the
  court's response; the decree is followed by his choice to return; and the
  hollow title is followed by the duty that creates the next transgression.
- The batch varies spectacle, dramatic scenes, and illustrated prose. The
  month in the stables, the battle, the hollow residence, and the accelerating
  peach theft use compression deliberately, while adjacent pages supply the
  emotional or political aftermath. No stretch reads as materially compressed
  summary, and the linked dramatic pairs do not become a wall of scenes.
