## Cold read

A stone on the peak of an empty mountain cracks open and a gold monkey climbs out. The other monkeys take him in; the eldest, Old Ma, puts her hand on his head and says "You're here." He grows up faster and louder than the rest. One hot day at the waterfall, Old Ma says whoever goes through the water and comes back gets made king, and that nobody has ever done it. He goes through, finds a dry stone house with room for everyone, and is crowned. That night, drunk on being king, he asks how old Old Ma is and she tells him she has seen three kings and they are all dead; only immortals don't die; they live far to the west. He says he'll go find one and make him teach.

He rafts west, spends three years among men learning to walk upright and wear a coat, and is pointed to a Master who has had students for forty years and never taught any of them the thing the monkey wants. The Master turns him away at the gate, hears "out of a rock," and gives him a name: Sun Wukong. He sweeps for seven years and asks once a year. Then the Master taps his head three times with his hands behind his back; everyone laughs; the monkey alone reads it as "midnight, back door." At midnight he is taught two things, the shape and the cloud, and warned: use them to be admired and you're out. The next morning he turns into a pine for the students, and the Master throws him out with an order never to say whose student he was. Home, Old Ma reads it on his face. He wants a weapon so he'll be a king and not a loud monkey; she says the Dragon King has a treasury and nobody has ever gone and asked.

He walks into the sea. The Dragon King wants him gone and, to be rid of him, points him at the pillar that anchors the sea. It hums for him; he tells it "smaller" until it is a needle he tucks behind his ear. He demands armor, gets it, tells the Dragon King his name and that he came out of a stone and can't be killed. The Dragon King notices the sea is still up and writes to Heaven. In Heaven, the Jade Emperor wants never to hear the name again; Laozi, his counselor, says a title is cheaper than soldiers and is sent to fetch him.

Laozi arrives on a cloud. Wukong says yes before hearing the job; Old Ma says he promised to teach them not to die and hasn't, and asks him to come back. Heaven's gate is open, so he decides they want him. The Emperor makes him keeper of the horses without looking at him. He asks whether it's a high post; Laozi doesn't answer. For a month he's happy with the horses, then asks Laozi the rank and Laozi tells the truth: below rank, the post for someone who isn't anyone yet. He breaks the desk and goes home, paints GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN on a banner, and beats the whole army by lunch. Laozi tells the Emperor to give him the words; the Emperor does, on the condition he stays out of the hall. Laozi reads it to the troop. Old Ma asks why go back up; he says because Heaven said his name and he wants to hear them say it up there. He gets a gold house and nothing to do, and it feels like the stable with a better roof. The Emperor gives him the peach garden to guard.

He eats the top peaches, the ones nobody has ever been allowed. Laozi comes for middle peaches for his elixir and "for tonight," and Wukong learns there is a banquet for every immortal of rank and asks plainly if he's invited: no. He eats the banquet alone, gets drunk, wanders down a red corridor into Laozi's lab, mistakes five gourds of elixir for sweets and eats them all, and sobers up glowing. Laozi, for the first time with no offer, says send everything and send Erlang, the Emperor's nephew with three eyes and a hound. Erlang comes alone in front of the army and asks if the banner's true; Wukong asks which eye to hit first; they fight shape for shape, then Wukong hides as a temple with his tail as a flagpole and runs from the dog. Laozi drops an iron ring on him from behind. Erlang wants it understood that wasn't him; Wukong, laughing in chains, says it took all of them and to say so in the hall.

In the hall Erlang says it. Wukong refuses to kneel to an equal. Laozi wants his peaches and elixir back and will burn him forty-nine days to get them. Inside, Wukong hits the walls for a day, then finds a hair of cold air under the lid and, for the first time in his life, waits. On the forty-eighth day he stands; the lid blows; the staff leaps to his hand; his eyes are gold with a ring of fire. He tells the Emperor to come down off the throne. Laozi has nothing left. The Emperor sends west, to the Buddha, who owes Heaven nothing. The Buddha arrives, asks what he wants the throne for ("so they can't ever say I'm nobody"), and bets: jump out of my hand and it's yours; fail and you go back to Earth and stay. Wukong somersaults to the edge of the world, signs the middle of five pillars, and marks it. Back on the palm, the Buddha turns his hand over: the writing is on his finger. Wukong bolts; the hand comes down beside his own mountain and becomes one, with a seal on top and his head sticking out. Five hundred years. Every year an old gray monkey climbs up with a peach and says the Buddha's words back to him, for as many years as she has; then a younger monkey who was told to. Someone will come. Wait.

**Where I stopped.** I never had to reread or guess. Four half-beats, none of which cost me the thread: p8 panel 1, where the Master says "what you came for" before anyone has said it on the page (p7's last sentence had already told me he refuses everyone, so it read as the doorman's refusal, and the real question follows two balloons later); p33→p35, where he is in Laozi's lab and then on his own peak (he had said "Which way is home" and "I'll take her some," and the army goes to his mountain, so I supplied it without effort); p39, "You said it in front of everyone," where I registered that Laozi, not the Emperor, read the scroll; and p30, "so that the stones fell where the gardeners would find them, and he did not care," where "so that" briefly read as purpose. All are listed below as polish, not findings.

## Is it alive?

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** Yes. The engine is in the room from p3: Old Ma sets a prize nobody has claimed, and the monkey takes it before anyone can stop him. The heat of p6 is real and specific: she is deciding whether to tell a child who has just been crowned that he ends, and she tells him. "How old is very?" into "Where everything goes" is the best exchange of the opening, and p7's "she had been the one who told him about immortals, and she knew it" gives the prose page an actual wound instead of a travel bridge. Want-from is clean on every page: she wants someone to go through; he wants the crown; then he wants the answer and she wants not to lie to him. Voice: Old Ma is already unmistakable ("Bow your head, or whatever it is kings do").

**Movement II (pp 8–14).** Alive, and the register is different from Movement I, which is a credit. Subhuti speaks in tests and the attribution strip passes without effort; nobody else in the book could say "A thing that wants to live forever needs one." The three-taps riddle is exactly the kind of puzzle a ten-year-old will solve half a beat before the monkey does and feel clever about. The subtext test is carried by Subhuti's dodge on immortality ("That is all immortality is. Don't tell the others"), which the book then honors all the way to the furnace: he is never killed, only caught. The fall on p13 is fast but earned, because p11 said the exact rule and p12 shows him breaking it privately before he breaks it in public. p14 is quietly moving: Old Ma reads the expulsion off his face and says "Never mind. You came back," and his appetite immediately reroutes into a weapon. The want is never off the page.

**Movement III (pp 15–19).** A pleasure. Ao Guang's want-from is the funniest in the book (he wants this conversation to end and gives away the sea's anchor to end it), and his voice never slips: "Tell me what you want, little neighbor, and then tell me you are leaving." The shrinking pillar is a proper set piece for these readers, and "Yes. I've got a staff." is the protagonist's voice in five words. The aftermath is there (the sea holding itself, "nobody told me"), and the letter is the right size. p19 introduces two antagonists with distinct wants in one page: the Emperor wants quiet; Laozi wants permission to manage. The subtext of "Soldiers cost more than titles" is that Laozi is proposing an insult and calling it a kindness, and the book knows it.

**Movement IV (pp 20–29).** This is the heart of the book and it is the strongest movement. Every page has a want-from and a lie by omission: "It is a post in Heaven" answers a question about height by naming a place; "Everyone says so" is what triggers "Everyone? Then tell me." p24 is the wound stated once, exactly, in Laozi's mouth, and the desk breaks. The banner page is pure appetite, and "It took him until lunch" is the correct allocation: the army was never a contest, and showing it would have made Erlang a repeat. p28, the choice to go back up, is what makes the movement a story rather than a sequence: "Heaven said my name, the way I wrote it. I want to hear them say it up there" is a want a seven-year-old will recognize in his own body. p29's "It felt like the stable, with a better roof" is the line I would quote to sell the book.

**Movement V (pp 30–34).** Alive; the appetite goes literal. "He started at the top." p31 is the cruelest page in the script and it is dramatized, not narrated: Laozi's evasion ("The title was so that you would not need one") is followed by the reader's own question in Wukong's mouth ("Say it plainly. Am I invited?") and the one-word answer. p32 talking to a peach, p33 "Sweets! Old Ma would love these" (he never stops thinking of her, and he eats all five anyway) — both are voice. p34 turns Laozi from manager to executioner the moment it costs him personally, and the Emperor's "Another title? A bigger house?" is the first time he is funny, which is the right time.

**Movement VI (pp 35–38).** The boys' favorite movement, I would bet. Erlang is the only person in Heaven who talks to Wukong the way Wukong talks to everyone, and the script knows that is the heat: for two pages the monkey is happier than he has been since the stables. "Which one do I hit first?" / "Nobody has ever asked me that." The temple with the flagpole round the back is a gag that will survive a hundred rereads. The ring from behind is the book's real moral turn, and it is enacted: Erlang refuses the credit, Wukong laughs in the chains and demands the truth be said in the hall, and on p39 it is. Nothing here is narrated that should have been shown.

**Movement VII (pp 39–47).** Yes. p40 is the emotional center of the volume and it is prose because it should be: "He had never waited for anything. He waited." The blast, the staff leaping to his hand, "That's every idea you had, and I'm still here," and the Emperor "giving up a secret" when he sends west. p43 is the one place the protagonist says the theme out loud ("So they can't ever say I'm nobody"), and I weighed it against the aphorism test: it passes, because it is an answer to a direct question from the only being who ever asks him one, and because this character has no subtext gear at all; it is in his voice, not the writer's. The pillars and the marking will make two boys shriek and then the finger will make them go quiet. p47 pays every debt the book took on: "come back" is inverted (she comes to him), the Buddha's words get their source, and "for as many years as she had" says her death without saying it. I felt it. I would read it again.

Whole-book: the protagonist is never serene for a page; there is no logistics dialogue anywhere (p31's peach count is the trigger for "For tonight?" and is two lines); no aphorism survives the attribution strip; every one of the eight characters changes what Wukong wants, knows, or chooses; every crowd is scenery; every consequential beat has its aftermath, including the protagonist's. The thirty-odd short balloons each answer a complete question or statement in the same panel or echo the line immediately before; none floats, none carries plot logic its neighbor does not supply. Architecture: one mode, one dominant turn, one dominant panel, three panels or fewer, at most two locations (p7 and p41 are two-location pages whose turn is the cut), and every page-turn line is a question, a threat, or an appetite. Balance 29/8/10 is close enough to the target and the extra spectacle pages are all scale beats these readers will want.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p18, the letter.** As rendered, the letter names the monkey, the stone, and the anchor, but not the mountain; Laozi arrives at Flower-Fruit Mountain on p20. The reader has "up top" from p16 and supplies the rest, and no one will stop. Three words would close it: "A monkey called Sun Wukong, of Flower-Fruit Mountain, has taken the anchor of the Eastern Sea."
2. **p23, prose.** "He had never been good at anything that was not a fight." He has not fought anyone on the page yet; a ten-year-old may notice. "anything that was not a dare" or "anything that did not end with somebody off a throne" would be truer to what the reader has seen.
3. **p30, prose.** "He ate them in the tree, so that the stones fell where the gardeners would find them, and he did not care." "So that" reads as intent for half a beat and then collides with "he did not care." "and the stones fell where the gardeners would find them, and he did not care" removes the wobble.
4. **p33→p35, transition.** His return home from Laozi's lab is inferred, cleanly, from "Which way is home" (p32), "I'll take her some" (p33), and the army going to his mountain. If the owner wants it closed on the page, one extra Erlang line on p35 does it without a caption: "You went home. Good. I would have hated to look for you."
5. **p39.** "You said it in front of everyone." It was Laozi who read the scroll to the troop (p28). Wukong's logic still lands, but "You had it read out in front of everyone" is both truer and more like him.
6. **p44, art note for the prompt, not the script.** The letters are "scratched" with a needle and are red; the builder will need a reason the scratch shows red (the pillar's flesh beneath a pale skin, or the needle drawing color). Not a story matter, but worth deciding before the packet.
7. **p26, considered and passed.** I weighed whether the army's defeat is an inverted page (the fight elided, then discussed on p27). It is not: the descending and retreating army is the shown event, the caption is the joke, and the real fight is correctly saved for the one opponent who can keep up. Dramatizing the army would have created the repeated-episode problem the mandate warns against.
8. **p47, considered and passed.** Old Ma is not named in the prose ("an old gray monkey"). The shawl, stick, and peach in the image identify her, and the anonymity is doing work in the sentence that says she died. Leave it.
9. **Balance for the record.** Ten spectacle pages against a target near six. Each is a scale beat (the stone, the cave, the cloud, the sea, the gate, the army, the shape-fight, the furnace, the pillars, the mountain) and each is earned by clarity on the page before it. Not a concern for this audience.
