## Cold read

A stone on an empty mountain cracks and a gold monkey climbs out. The brown monkeys take him in; the eldest, Old Ma, puts her hand on his head. He is faster and louder than everyone and nobody minds. On a hot day the troop dares itself to go through the waterfall — whoever goes and comes back is king — and only he goes. Behind the water is a dry stone house with room for all of them. Old Ma crowns him Handsome Monkey King. That night, by the fire, he asks how old she is and she tells him: three kings before him, all dead. Kings die. Only immortals don't. He decides on the spot to go find one and make him teach.

He rafts west for a season, walks among men for three years, and an old woman points him to the Master's mountain, warning that the Master has never taught anyone the thing he wants. The Master refuses him at the gate, then names him — Sun Wukong — and hands him a broom. Seven years of sweeping; seven "not yet"s. At a lesson the Master taps his head three times and walks off with his hands behind his back; the students laugh; Wukong alone reads it as *midnight, third watch, back door*. At midnight he gets the two tricks — the shape and the cloud — and a warning: use them to be admired and I will send you away alone. The next morning he turns into a pine for the students. The Master throws him out and tells him never to say whose student he was.

He comes home on a cloud. Old Ma counts ten years and a season, and sees at once that he was thrown out. He decides to make a name of his own; a king needs a weapon; the old ones said the Dragon King has a treasury. He walks into the sea. The Dragon King, weary and polite, offers him the one thing nobody can lift to be rid of him. Wukong talks the pillar down to a needle, tucks it behind his ear, demands armor, tells the Dragon King his name and his boast, and leaves. The Dragon King writes to Heaven.

In Heaven the Jade Emperor wants never to hear the name again. Laozi, his round counselor, proposes a cheaper answer than soldiers: a small title. He fetches Wukong personally. Wukong says yes before asking what the post is; Old Ma asks the question he didn't, and asks for one thing — come back. In Heaven he is made Keeper of the Heavenly Horses. He is happy for a month; then he asks Laozi what rank it is, and Laozi does not lie: it is below rank. Wukong breaks the desk and goes home. He paints a banner — GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN — and Heaven's whole army comes and goes home by lunch. Laozi tells the Emperor to give him the words. Laozi reads the scroll to the troop. Old Ma asks why go back up when he has already won; he says, because they said my name up there and I want to hear it. She says come back. He always does.

The gold house is empty. Nobody visits. The Emperor makes him guard the peach garden — a monkey, in an orchard. Wukong eats from the top trees, the ones nobody was ever allowed to eat. Laozi comes for banquet peaches and Wukong learns there is a banquet, every immortal of rank, tonight, and he is not invited. Laozi says the title was so that he would not need an invitation. Wukong eats the banquet alone, drunk, wanders into Laozi's red laboratory, eats five gourds of elixir thinking they are sweets for Old Ma, and comes out sober and glowing. Laozi, for the first time not smiling, tells the Emperor no more offers: send Erlang.

Erlang, the Emperor's three-eyed nephew, arrives alone with a hound and asks whether the banner is true. Wukong asks which eye to hit first. They fight shape for shape; nobody has ever kept up with him. Wukong hides as a temple with his tail as the flagpole; Erlang is not fooled; Wukong runs from the dog. From above, Laozi drops an iron ring on his head — the last thing he has that is his own. Wukong, face down and laughing, tells Erlang to say in the hall that it took all of them.

In the hall he refuses to kneel: equals don't. Laozi will burn him forty-nine days in the furnace to get his peaches and elixir back; Wukong says he is made of a rock. In the furnace he rages, then finds one thread of cold air and waits — the first time he has ever waited. On day forty-eight he stands; on forty-nine the lid blows and he comes out with fire in his eyes. He backs the Emperor against his own throne and tells him to get off it. Laozi has nothing left. The Emperor sends west, to the Buddha, who owes Heaven nothing.

The Buddha fills the hall and asks what Wukong wants the throne for. So they can never say I'm nobody. A bet: jump out of my hand and the throne is yours. Wukong somersaults to five pillars at the end of the world, signs the middle one, marks it, and comes back. The Buddha turns over his hand: the writing is on his middle finger. Wukong bolts; the hand comes down; five hundred years, then someone will come, wait for him. The hand becomes a mountain beside his own. Every year Old Ma climbs it with a peach and says the Buddha's words back to him until she runs out of years, and after her a younger monkey who was told to. Someone will come. Wait.

**Where I stopped, in order of first reading:**

- **p17→p19.** "The sea is anchored on that!" — then a letter saying the anchor is taken, then Heaven ordering the pillar brought back. I waited for the sea to do something. It never does, and nobody mentions it again. (Finding 1.)
- **p30→p31.** p30 told me nobody remembers what the top peaches do because nobody has ever been allowed to eat one, and then "He started at the top." p31 Laozi walks up a path covered in stones, looks straight up at the tree where the feet are hanging, and says nothing about it. I reread p31 looking for the line. It isn't there. (Finding 2.)
- **p13→p14.** Small: the Master said "never say that you were my student"; the next page Wukong says "He said never to say his name." I checked back. (Nonblocking.)
- **p11.** "The first in forty years" — the first forty I had heard of. Didn't stop me long. (Nonblocking.)
- **p40.** I wondered, as a ten-year-old would, why a monkey who can be a snake or a sparrow does not go out through the gap the wind comes in. (Nonblocking.)

## Is it alive?

Yes. I want to say that plainly before the findings, because the findings are two dropped threads in a script whose people are otherwise alive on every page. I read it in one sitting and did not once feel I was being briefed.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** Alive. The opening is one sentence and one figure looking at you, and it works. p3 is the book's first want-from — Old Ma wants someone to go, Wukong wants to be the one, the troop wants to not be the one — and the page is built on nobody moving. p5 is pure joy and the "why you turn" line is honest: it *is* the happiest anyone will be. p6 is the best early page: "How old is very?" is a seven-year-old's question, and Old Ma's answer is the book's engine, spoken once and not repeated, exactly as her ledger says. He is standing on a stool with wet eyes and fists shut. Heat: a child finding out he ends, from the person who loves him. p7's prose earns its keep with one clause — Old Ma "did not tell him to stay, because she had been the one who told him about immortals, and she knew it." That is the sentence her whole arc hangs from and it is placed early.

**Movement II (pp 8–14).** Alive. Subhuti passes the attribution-strip in every line: nothing he says could be in anyone else's mouth. The three-taps page is a real gag that a ten-year-old solves with the monkey, and the students laughing behind their hands gives the reader someone to be smarter than. p11 is quick but has heat — "Don't tell the others" makes the teaching a secret between two people, and the threat in panel 3 is spoken in the voice that means it while the monkey is already grinning. p13 hurt. "I know what you wanted. Go." p14 is Old Ma at her best: "You came back on a cloud and your face was wrong. Never mind. You came back." Subtext is present and doing work: she says "never mind" and means the opposite. The weapon pivot is bridged in his mouth and I accepted it on first read.

**Movement III (pp 15–19).** Fun, with a good new voice. Ao Guang's "little neighbor" and "I am too old to find out" are exactly the weary-polite-to-the-edge-of-panic of his ledger. p17 — talking the pillar down like a dog — is the kind of page Sebastian will want read twice. p19 is the one page in the movement I'd call competent rather than felt, but it is short, it introduces two voices distinctly (the Emperor's fingers at his temple, Laozi already dabbing), and Laozi's "Soldiers cost more than titles" is a manager's line, not the writer's. Passes.

**Movement IV (pp 20–29).** The strongest run in the book. p20: "Who cares!" against "What is the post?" is the whole relationship in four words each, and "Come back. That's the whole thing I want from you" is the want-from stated by the character without being an aphorism. p22: Laozi does not answer the question, and the script trusts the reader to hear it. p24: Laozi's "It is the post one gives to someone who is not anyone yet" is the cruellest honest line in the book and it is right that he does not lie. p25 the banner; p26 "It took him until lunch" is a perfect spectacle caption. p28 is the page that makes the whole back half work — "Because they said it. Heaven said my name, the way I wrote it. I want to hear them say it up there" — a choice, dramatized, in his voice, with Old Ma asking the question a reader would ask. This is the appetite test passed in one balloon.

**Movement V (pp 30–34).** The thinnest movement, and it is where both blockers live. Two prose pages back to back (29, 30) slow the entry, though each is good prose and p29's last sentence is the best joke in the book. p31 is excellent from "For tonight?" onward — the stillness "the way he was still under the three taps, but with no smile" is a real callback, and "No." is the founding-case short line done right. But p31's first panel ignores what the reader was just told mattered most (Finding 2). p32–33 are comic and earn the elixir; "Old Ma would love these. I'll take her some" is heartbreaking in a drunk balloon, which is the right place for it. p34 turns Laozi and the Emperor together and the hand on the shoulder is a good enacted beat.

**Movement VI (pp 35–38).** Alive and funny. Erlang is the only person who talks to Wukong the way Wukong talks to everyone, and the script knows it: "Which one do I hit first?" / "Nobody has ever asked me that." The temple gag lands because Erlang narrates it dryly instead of being fooled. "That was before the dog!" is the single best short balloon in the script. p38: "It took all of you. Say that in the hall when they ask" — the want is intact face-down in chains. Laozi's "the last thing I have that is mine" makes the ring a cost, not a gadget.

**Movement VII (pp 39–47).** Alive, and it lands. "Equals don't kneel. You said it in front of everyone." p40 is the right use of prose — waiting is interior and cannot be dramatized in balloons — and "He had never waited for anything. He waited." is the volume's turn stated in the only register that can carry it. p42's list — "Stable boy. Empty house. No invitation. A furnace." — is the whole book in nine words, spoken by the person it happened to. The Buddha asks questions and never sermonizes; the bet is legible to a seven-year-old; the marked pillar is exactly the joke this audience deserves; "That's my writing." / "And that, I think, is the rest of it." is perfect. p47 is the payoff of every hand-on-head in the book and it made me feel something. "After that a younger monkey did it, who had been told to" is the sentence I will remember.

**Would I read it?** Yes. I would hand it to a ten-year-old tonight if the two threads below were tied.

## Mandatory findings (blockers only, page order)

### 1. pp 17–19 — the sea's anchor is a stated stake with no aftermath

**Failure.** p17, Ao Guang: *"It moved. It has never moved. The sea is anchored on that!"* p18, the letter: *"A monkey called Sun Wukong has taken the anchor of the Eastern Sea."* p19, Laozi: *"A monkey has taken the pillar that holds the sea down,"* and the Emperor: *"Send soldiers. Bring the pillar back."* Three pages, three characters, one order. The pillar is then Wukong's staff for the rest of the book, is never returned, and the sea is never mentioned again by anyone.

**What a first-time reader must repair.** Whether the sea fell, whether it is going to, and why every person who said it mattered stopped caring by p20. Francisco tracks legendary objects; he was told in three voices that this one holds the sea down, and the book then asks him to forget it. The Emperor's "Bring the pillar back" is a plan the reader watches Heaven abandon without a word. This is a §3f consequential beat without its aftermath and a §8.6 causal chain with a dangling link.

**Prescription.** Close it in text, in one of two ways; either is a one-page change.

*(a) Keep the anchor and pay it.* On p18 panel 3, give Ao Guang a balloon before the letter, looking up through the water: *"And the sea is still up. It seems it was holding itself all along, and nobody told me."* Then the letter can keep "has taken the anchor of the Eastern Sea," and on p19 the Emperor's "Bring the pillar back" reads as a lord wanting a vassal's property returned, which is what it is. The reader has the state of the sea before the plot moves.

*(b) Remove the function.* On p16 panel 3, Ao Guang: *"There is one thing. It was in this sea before the sea was. Nobody has lifted it. Nobody ever will. Go and try."* On p17 panel 2: *"It moved. It has never moved. Nothing in my sea has ever moved that!"* On p18 the letter says *"has taken the pillar from the floor of the Eastern Sea,"* and on p19 Laozi says *"the pillar from the bottom of the Eastern Sea."* Nothing else changes.

I prefer (a): it costs one balloon and it is in Ao Guang's voice.

### 2. pp 30–31, with payoff on p39 or p41 — the top peaches are promised and never answered; Laozi sees the evidence and does not react

**Failure.** p30 prose: *"The ones at the top ripened every nine thousand years, and nobody remembered what they did, because nobody had ever been allowed to eat one. He started at the top."* The script makes the top peaches the biggest mystery in the garden, in its own voice, and sends him straight to them. p31 panel 1 staging: Laozi "coming up the terrace with a basket… peach stones on the path. His eyes go up the terraces to the biggest tree at the top, where two gold feet hang down." His balloon: *"Great Sage. I need forty of the middle peaches for my elixir and forty more for tonight. Where are the—"* — and the scene pivots entirely to the invitation. On p34 Laozi reports only the elixir. On p39 he says *"You are made of my peaches and my elixir"* — the word "peaches" appears, but never "the top ones," and never what they did. p41's caption gives him fire in his eyes without attributing it to anything.

**What a first-time reader must repair.** Two things. First, on p31: *did Laozi notice?* He is staged looking at the evidence and the dialogue behaves as if he saw nothing. The reader has to decide whether Laozi is blind, indifferent, or the script forgot — and every one of those answers damages the want-from of the page, because Laozi's want in this room should include the forbidden peaches and instead is only "fill my basket." Second, across the book: *what did the top peaches do?* The script asked the question on p30 in the reader's ear and never answers it. "Recoverable" via p39's generic "my peaches" is a revision flag, not a pass.

**Prescription.** Two lines, one at the sighting and one at the payoff.

*Sighting, p31 panel 1.* Add a Laozi balloon before the basket line, bending to the stones: *"Great Sage. These stones are from the top trees. Nobody in Heaven has ever been allowed one."* Then, from the tree, a Wukong balloon: *"They're good. Bit dry."* Then Laozi's existing line and the existing "For tonight?" cut. That is fourteen words of Laozi and four of Wukong; p31 currently sits near 70 words and holds it. It also sharpens the pivot: Laozi *chooses* to let the peaches go and move on to his basket, which is the manager his ledger describes.

*Payoff, choose one (both are better).* On p39 panel 2, Laozi: *"You are made of my top peaches, that nobody was ever allowed, and my elixir. I want them back."* And/or on p41, the caption: *"Forty-nine days. Nine-thousand-year peaches, five gourds of elixir, and a rock. He came out with the fire in his eyes, and it stayed."* Either one tells the reader, in text, what eating the top of the garden did: cooked with the elixir in the furnace, it made him this. The mystery p30 opened gets its answer on the page where the answer is visible.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p3 / p6.** Old Ma on p3: *"That has always been the rule."* On p6: three kings *"None of them went through the water. They just shouted the loudest."* The p6 line answers the contradiction on its own page, so no reader is left holding it, but *"That was always the rule. Nobody has ever done it."* on p3 would make p6 land harder rather than merely reconcile.
- **p11.** *"You are the first in forty years to understand a simple thing."* The forty is floating; nothing in rendered text has set it. It is characterful and harmless, but if the owner wants it checkable, p7's old woman could say *"He has had students for forty years, and he has never once taught any of them what you are asking for."*
- **p13→p14.** Subhuti: *"never say that you were my student."* Wukong, next page: *"He said never to say his name."* The reader never heard the Master's name either, which is a nice touch, but the paraphrase is one page from the original. *"He said never to say I was his. Fine. I'll make a name of my own."* keeps the beat and matches.
- **p29–p30.** Two illustrated-prose pages back to back at the top of Movement V. Both are good and both carry information that has to be prose (time, the three tiers). But the empty-house loneliness of p29 is the one beat in Wukong's list on p42 that the reader never *saw* — it is narrated. Consider whether p29 wants a single silent dramatic beat (him on the roof, a servant passing without looking up) or whether p30 could be a spectacle page with its three-tier information moved into a shorter caption. Not required; the movement works as written.
- **p33→p35.** After the laboratory he is next seen on his peak. The intent is seeded (*"Which way is home"*, *"I'll take her some"*), and the reader supplies "he went home" without effort. If the owner wants it airtight, one clause in p34's caption-free page is not the place; leave it.
- **p40.** *"a thread of cold no wider than his tail."* A ten-year-old who watched him be a snake and a sparrow may ask why he does not go out the way the wind comes in. *"a thread of cold no wider than a hair"* closes it without changing the beat.
- **p20 / p47.** *"You said you'd teach us not to die. You haven't yet."* He never does, and she dies bringing him peaches. This is not a missing aftermath; it is the book's ache, and it is paid exactly right by *"for as many years as she had."* Noting it so nobody "fixes" it.
- **p44.** The mark on the pillar is a judgment for the owner, not the critic; for Francisco and Sebastian it is precisely right, and p45's *"a faint wet patch"* is the correct amount of it.
- **Five-word audit.** Every balloon of five words or fewer was checked (pp 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 14, 17, 19, 20, 25, 28, 31, 35, 37, 39, 42, 43, 45). Each is either a complete thought or a direct answer to a complete question in the same panel with one visible referent. None fails. None should be lengthened.
- **Budget.** pp 6, 11, and 34 sit in the upper part of the dramatic budget; the audit passed them and no line should be cut, but the page contract should reserve balloon lanes on those three before faces are placed.
- **Balance.** 29 / 8 / 10 is 62 / 17 / 21 against 60 / 20 / 13 — inside "roughly." The spectacle pages are all doing work (1, 4, 12, 15, 21, 26, 36, 41, 44, 46); none is filler.
- **Cast.** Eight named, none earns less than its keep, no functional crowd. Subhuti never enters Heaven and the script keeps it that way; the ledger's re-entry collision (Laozi six pages after Subhuti) is a reference problem, not a script one, and p20's *"I am Laozi, from the Jade Emperor, who rules Heaven"* gives the reader the name before it matters.
