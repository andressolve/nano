## Cold read

A gold monkey climbs out of a cracked stone on a mountain by the sea. The brown monkeys take him in; the eldest, Old Ma, lays her hand on his head. On a hot day at the waterfall she says whoever goes through the water and comes back gets to be king, and he goes before anyone can stop him. Behind the water is a dry stone house with room for all of them; they move in and crown him. That night by the fire he asks how old she is, and she tells him kings die too, and only immortals don't, and they live far to the west. He builds a raft and leaves. Years later, in a man's coat, an old woman points him at a mountain where "the Master" lives and takes no students.

The Master asks who his parents are, hears "a rock," and names him Sun Wukong. He sweeps for seven years. At a lesson the Master taps his head three times and walks off with his hands behind his back; the monkey alone decodes it (midnight, back door) and is taught the shape and the cloud in one night, with the warning never to use them to be admired. He shows the other students a pine trick and is expelled and told never to say whose student he was. He flies home. Old Ma reads his face. He decides a king needs a weapon and she sends him to the Dragon King's treasury under the sea.

He walks into the sea, rejects the Dragon King's sword and halberd, and talks the sea's iron anchor pillar down to a needle behind his ear. He demands armor, gets it, leaves. The Dragon King writes to the Jade Emperor. In Heaven, a round old counselor with a gourd talks the Emperor out of soldiers and into giving the monkey a job, and is sent to fetch him. The monkey says yes before asking what the job is; Old Ma asks him to come back. The job is keeper of the horses; he is good at it and happy for a month, until he asks the counselor what rank it is and hears "below rank." He smashes the desk and goes home. He paints GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN on a banner. Heaven's army comes and goes home by lunch. The counselor tells the Emperor to give him the title he wrote, and a house, and nothing to do. The monkey ends up on the roof of a gold mansion, then in charge of the peach garden, where he eats the top peaches. The counselor comes for peaches for a banquet "tonight" and, pressed, admits the monkey is not invited. The monkey eats the banquet alone, wanders drunk into the counselor's laboratory, eats five gourds of gold pills thinking they are sweets, and sobers up glowing. The counselor says no more offers, send Erlang.

Erlang, three-eyed, with a hound, meets him on the peak, fights him shape for shape, catches him as a temple with the flagpole round the back; the monkey runs from the dog and is dropped by the counselor's iron ring from above. In chains before the throne he refuses to kneel; the counselor proposes forty-nine days in his furnace to get the pills back. In the dark the monkey finds the one cold thread of air, waits — for the first time — and bursts out on the forty-ninth day with fire in his eyes, wrecks the hall, and tells the Emperor to come down off the throne. The Emperor sends west, to the Buddha. A giant in saffron fills the hall and offers a bet: jump out of my hand and the throne is yours. The monkey somersaults to five pillars at the end of the world, signs the middle one, pees on it, comes back, and is shown the writing on the Buddha's middle finger. He bolts; the hand comes down beside his own mountain and becomes one, with his head sticking out. Winters pass. Old Ma climbs up with a peach every year until she can't, and then younger monkeys do. "Someone will come, she had said. Wait."

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed, in reading order:**

- p6: "three kings before you" — but p3 said the rule "has always been" go-through-the-water, and nobody had. How were the other three made?
- p7→p8: told the Master "does not take students," then watched him take one in two questions. Where was the no?
- p10: "the third watch" — Sebastian will not know what a watch is.
- p14: "Seven years and a season" — but p7 said the world of men took him "for years" on top of the seven.
- p14: he lands, grieves, says he'll teach them all not to die, and in the next panel wants a weapon. Why a weapon, now? And the teaching promise never comes up again.
- p19: the counselor knows the monkey's name, that he came from a stone, changes shape, rides a cloud, can't be killed. The Dragon King was told none of that.
- p19–p23: nobody says the counselor's name. Wukong calls him "Laozi" on p24 as if we knew.
- p27→p28: he beat their army; next page he lives in their house. When did he say yes?
- p32: "Five hundred years of it" — how does he know the number?
- p41→p42: the Emperor says "send west," and the Buddha is simply there. What did the monkey do while they sent?
- p44/p46: "because that was the Buddha's number" — the Buddha never said a number. He said "go back to Earth."
- p45: a gold paper seal floats onto the summit and nothing ever says what it is.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This script has a pulse, and most of the time it has a grin. The protagonist never goes serene; the appetite runs from the first balloon ("So somebody should go and look") to the last one he speaks ("I felt the wind—"). The voices survive the attribution-strip test on every dramatic page: cover the names and Old Ma is still the dry one who says the hard thing once, Laozi is the one who answers a different question than the one asked, the Emperor is the one who ends sentences, Erlang is the one who is enjoying himself, the Buddha is the one asking. I found no logistics dialogue and no aphorism dialogue. Subtext is present on most dramatic pages, and the best pages are built on it (p22, p24, p30). Movement by movement:

**I — The Stone and the Waterfall (pp 1–7).** Alive. "Well. You're here." is the right first line for Old Ma, and p6 is a real scene: the question about her age turns into the fact of his death in three exchanges, and his answer is to leave. Heat: she is the one who told him, and the p7 prose knows it. Only the crowning on p5 is thinner than it should be — "Kneel, or whatever it is kings do" reads aloud as a joke that the crowd delivers for her. Fine.

**II — The Master (pp 8–14).** Alive, but this is the movement where I felt least. The naming on p8 lands because nobody has given him anything before; the expulsion on p13 lands hard ("Master, I only wanted them to—" / "I know what you wanted. Go."). Between them the riddle and the midnight lesson are competent rather than felt; Subhuti's warning on p11 does its structural work and the three balloons of instruction are the closest the book comes to a briefing, though they stay in his voice. The real soft spot is p8: the page-turn promise on p7 is a refusal, and the refusal never happens (finding 2). p14 is the emotional spine of the movement — "Your face was wrong. Never mind. You came back." — and then the weapon pivot in the last panel arrives from nowhere (finding 5).

**III — The Staff (pp 15–19).** Fun. Ao Guang's "little neighbor" is a voice. "Yes. I've got a staff." is the right five-word answer to a question meant to frighten him. The heat is exact: the Dragon King wants him gone and gives away the sea's anchor to get it. p19 is the best introduction of a schemer in the book — Laozi's "Soldiers cost more than titles" tells you the whole man — except that the reader never hears his name (finding 7).

**IV — The Stable Boy (pp 20–28).** The core of the book, and it works. "Who cares!" on p20 with Old Ma's "What is the post?" is the want-from test passing in four words. p22's "Is that a high post?" / "It is a post in Heaven" is a real dodge a ten-year-old will hear. The prose on p23 ("The horses were fat and shining, and that was the ledger") is the warmest writing in the volume, and it makes p24 hurt — Laozi does not lie, and the desk goes. The banner page is earned. The one dead patch is p28, where the protagonist's decision to go back up to Heaven — after beating its army — is narrated in a subordinate clause, and it is the only place in the book where the monkey's choice is reported instead of made (finding 8).

**V — The Peaches (pp 29–33).** The best movement. p30 is the best page: "Say it plainly. Am I invited?" / "No." I read it aloud and it stopped me. The escalation stable → house → invitation makes three different points (rank, words, presence), so it is not a repeated episode; p41's list proves the script knows the sequence. The drunk banquet and the sweets are exactly right for the audience — talking to a peach, "Red's fine" — and the reason he eats the pills is Old Ma ("I'll take her some"), which is the book remembering its own heart. p33 turns Laozi in one panel: no dabbing, no smile.

**VI — Erlang (pp 34–37).** Alive and sporting. "Three eyes. Which one do I hit first?" / "Nobody has ever asked me that." is the first time anyone speaks to him as an equal, and the script knows it is the turn. The temple with the flagpole round the back is the right gag at the right length. "That was before the dog!" is Sebastian's favorite line; I would bet on it. Laozi's "This is the last thing I have that is mine" is the quietest good line in the book, and Erlang's "That wasn't me. I want that understood." pays his want exactly.

**VII — The Furnace and the Hand (pp 38–46).** The furnace prose on p39 ("He had never waited for anything. He waited.") is the best writing in the volume and the reason the ending works. p41's "Stable boy. Empty house. No invitation. A furnace." is the speech the whole book has been building. The Buddha is written correctly: questions, a bet, no sermon, and the joke at the end of the world is pitched perfectly for a seven-year-old and a ten-year-old at the same time. p46 is quietly devastating — Old Ma climbing with a peach "for as many years as she had" — and it lands the volume's real subject, which the script never says aloud: he wanted none of them to die, and he is the only one who didn't. Two seams show: the Buddha's arrival with no time in it, and the "five hundred years" that nobody said on the page.

**Would I read it?** Yes, and I would read it to the boys. It is not merely correct. The findings below are seams, not a dead register — but several of them are exactly the "solve the adaptation" gaps the mandate exists to catch, and one (p28) is the protagonist's most important choice in the middle of the book going unmade on the page.

## Mandatory findings (blockers)

**1. p3 / p6 — the kingship rule contradicts the three prior kings.**
p3, OLD MA: "Whoever goes through and comes back alive, we make king. That has always been the rule." p3 also establishes nobody has ever gone through ("Nobody knows where the water comes from"). p6, OLD MA: "Old enough that I have seen three kings before you."
*Reader repair:* how were three kings made under a rule nobody had ever satisfied? Francisco will ask this out loud.
*Prescription:* keep both lines and let Old Ma resolve it in her own voice on p6 panel 1, e.g. "Old enough that I have seen three kings before you. None of them went through the water. They just shouted the loudest." This also seeds Wukong's own "A king with no weapon is just a loud monkey" on p14.

**2. p7 → p8 — the promised refusal never happens.**
p7 prose: "the Master lives there, and he does not take students." Page-turn line: "the monkey has never in his life taken no." On p8 Subhuti asks two questions and names him; there is no no.
*Reader repair:* the reader was promised a door that would not open and watches it open on its own; the naming reads as routine intake instead of a reversal, and Subhuti's want ("the student he has been waiting for") is invisible.
*Prescription:* give Subhuti the refusal in p8 panel 1 and let the answer overturn it. E.g. SUBHUTI: "I do not take students. Go home to your parents." WUKONG: "I don't have any. I came out of a rock, over the sea. It's still there if you want to see it." Then panel 2 as written. Alternatively, sharpen p7 to "he has not taken a student in forty years," which p11's "the first in forty years" then pays — but do the p8 refusal regardless; it is the scene's engine. (p8 is ~77 words; the addition fits within the dramatic budget.)

**3. p14 — the time count contradicts the script's own clock.**
p7 prose: "The sea took him west for a season. The world of men took him for years." p9: "He swept for seven years." p14, OLD MA: "Seven years and a season. I counted."
*Reader repair:* the reader who was told "years" in the world of men has to decide Old Ma miscounted, or the script did.
*Prescription:* put a number in p7 ("The world of men took him for three years") and have Old Ma count the total on p14 ("Ten years and a season. I counted."). Her counting is the point of the line; make it checkable.

**4. p16 / p18 / p19 — Heaven knows what nobody told it.**
p16, WUKONG to Ao Guang: "I'm the Monkey King of Flower-Fruit Mountain, up top." That is all the Dragon King learns. p18 letter: "A monkey has taken the anchor of the Eastern Sea." p19, LAOZI: "A monkey that came out of a stone, learned to change shape and ride a cloud, and cannot be killed. He's called Wukong."
*Reader repair:* the reader must invent a source for the name, the stone, the shape, the cloud, and the immortality. The rule is explicit: a character acting on information the reader never saw them acquire.
*Prescription:* let Wukong boast it and Ao Guang report it. On p18 panel 1, add AO GUANG: "And who do I say took it?" / WUKONG: "Sun Wukong. Say I came out of a stone, and I walked in dry, and I can't be killed." Then p18 panel 3 letter: "A monkey called Sun Wukong has taken the anchor of the Eastern Sea. He came out of a stone, he says, and cannot be killed, he says. I am too old to find out." Then p19 LAOZI reports only what the letter holds: "A monkey that came out of a stone, walked into the sea dry, and says he cannot be killed. He's called Wukong." (Drop "change shape and ride a cloud" from Laozi; Heaven learns the cloud when Laozi sees him on p20.)

**5. p14 — the weapon has no motive, and the promise to teach the troop is never paid.**
Panel 1, WUKONG: "I can't die. Nobody can kill me. I'm going to teach every one of you." Panel 3, WUKONG: "A king with no weapon is just a loud monkey. Where does a king get one?" Nothing bridges the wound in panel 2 to the weapon in panel 3, and "teach every one of you" — the book's founding want from p6, "none of us has to die" — is never mentioned again by anyone, including on p46 where Old Ma dies bringing him peaches.
*Reader repair:* why does a monkey who just said he will teach his family immortality instead go shopping for a staff? And what happened to the teaching? The ending's irony (he is the only one who doesn't die) depends on the reader remembering a promise the script itself forgets.
*Prescription, two parts.* (a) Bridge the pivot in Wukong's own voice on p14 panel 3: "He said never to say his name. Fine. I'll make one of my own. A king with no weapon is just a loud monkey. Where does a king get one?" (b) Pay the promise once, on p20, in Old Ma's mouth, where it sharpens "Come back": OLD MA: "You said you'd teach us not to die. You haven't yet." / WUKONG: "When I'm back. When I'm a lord." / OLD MA: "Come back. That's the whole thing I want from you. Come back." Do not add it to p46, which is at 138 prose words, the exceptional ceiling; the reader will carry the unkept promise to the last page without help.

**6. p19–p24 — Laozi is never named in rendered text until Wukong uses the name.**
Speaker labels do not render. On p19, p20, p22, and p23, no spoken or printed line contains "Laozi." p24, WUKONG: "Then tell me, Laozi." p32: "Laozi's place."
*Reader repair:* the reader must deduce that the round old man with the gourd has been called Laozi all along, at the moment Wukong first says it in anger.
*Prescription:* name him where the Emperor dispatches him, p19 panel 3: JADE EMPEROR: "Fine, Laozi. Go and fetch him yourself." And let him introduce himself on p20 panel 1: "Sun Wukong. I am Laozi. I come from the Jade Emperor, who rules Heaven…" Two words each; no budget concern.

**7. p27 → p28 — the protagonist's decision to return to Heaven is not on the page.**
p24: "I don't begin. I'm going home." p26: the army goes home. p27: Heaven decides to give him the words. p28 prose: "They gave him the words, and a house made of gold, and nothing to do in it." At no point does Wukong choose to go back up.
*Reader repair:* he beat their whole army by lunch and then moved into their house; the reader has to supply why (the title is what he wanted; hearing Heaven say his own words back is the prize). This is the monkey's most consequential choice in the middle of the book, and it is narrated in a subordinate clause on a page that is already at 128 prose words.
*Prescription:* add a dramatic page between 27 and 28 (renumber after): Laozi alone on his cloud at the peak with the scroll, reading GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN aloud under the banner. OLD MA: "You beat them. Why go back up?" WUKONG: "Because they said it. Heaven said my name, the way I wrote it. I want to hear them say it up there." OLD MA: "Come back." (If the owner will not add a page, the minimum is a first sentence on p28 — "Laozi came down alone, read the title out under the banner in front of the whole troop, and the monkey went up that afternoon." — but that pushes p28 to ~150 words and requires cutting nothing causal; the added page is the right fix.)

**8. p32 — Wukong states a number he was never given.**
WUKONG: "Those weren't sweets. That was the thing he makes. Five hundred years of it." The only prior mention of the elixir is p30, LAOZI: "forty of the top peaches for my elixir." No one has said five hundred years.
*Reader repair:* the reader wonders how a drunk monkey knows the age of another man's medicine.
*Prescription:* strip the number from Wukong and leave it to Laozi, who already says it on p33. p32 panel 3: "Those weren't sweets. That was the thing he makes. His elixir. All five gourds. And I feel wonderful." Or, if the number is wanted early, give it to Laozi on p30: "forty of the top peaches for my elixir — five hundred years I've been at it — and forty more for tonight."

**9. p41 → p42 — the Buddha arrives with no time in it.**
p41, JADE EMPEROR: "Then send west. To the Buddha. Tell him Heaven is asking." p42 opens with the Buddha filling the hall and Wukong on the floor.
*Reader repair:* what did the monkey — three steps from a throne he has just demanded — do while a message went west and a giant came east? The reader must either assume he waited politely (he has never waited; that is p39's whole point) or that Heaven's messages are instantaneous.
*Prescription:* let the Buddha close the gap himself in p42 panel 1: BUDDHA: "Heaven asked, so I came. Sit down a moment. What is it you want the throne for?" That is his voice (slow, plain, asks questions), it makes his arrival part of his nature, and it leaves Wukong no time to have waited.

**10. p42 / p44 / p46 — the sentence is never spoken; the bet's terms and its penalty do not match.**
p42, BUDDHA: "If you can't, you go back to Earth." p44, BUDDHA: "Yes. Now hold still." p46 prose: "how many there were in five hundred, because that was the Buddha's number."
*Reader repair:* the reader must accept "five hundred" as something the Buddha said when he never said it, and reconcile "go back to Earth" with five hundred years under a mountain. For a seven-year-old, the bet's fairness matters; the Buddha is the one being who is straight with the monkey, and the script has him understate the stakes without anyone noticing.
*Prescription:* put the number in the Buddha's mouth on p44 panel 3: BUDDHA: "Yes. Now hold still. Five hundred years should do it." Six added words; p46's "the Buddha's number" then refers to something the reader heard. Optionally sharpen p42's terms to "If you can't, you go back to Earth, and you stay there."

## Nonblocking observations

- **p10 — "the third watch of the night."** Sebastian will not know what a watch is; the p11 candle and the location line ("midnight," which does not render) carry it. Consider "the third watch of the night — midnight — and come in by the back door."
- **p5 — "Kneel, or whatever it is kings do."** Reads aloud as Old Ma not knowing the ceremony, which is the joke, but a reader may briefly ask why a king kneels. Fine as is; if revised, "Bow your head, or whatever it is kings do" removes the wobble.
- **p29 — the top peaches.** "nobody remembered what they did, because nobody had ever been allowed to eat one" is a raised question that the book never answers; the fire in his eyes is credited to the furnace. Either let Laozi's furnace speech on p38 fold them in ("You are made of my elixir and my peaches") or cut the mystery.
- **p37 — "I know. I saw."** Panel 1 stages Wukong "not up." Consider "I know. I saw him on the way down."
- **p37 → p38 — Erlang vanishes.** Wukong's "Say that in the hall when they ask" is never paid; Erlang is absent from p38. One Erlang line in p38 panel 1 ("It took all of us, uncle. He wanted that said.") would pay it and give the Emperor's "Kneel" more sting. Not a blocker because Erlang's own aftermath is complete on p37.
- **p45 — the gold paper seal.** It floats onto the summit and no text names it. A reader will ask what it is. Either drop it from the staging or give the caption one clause ("and a paper seal came down to hold it shut").
- **p19 / p22 — the pillar.** The Emperor orders "Bring the pillar back," Wukong volunteers "It's behind my ear," and nobody ever asks for it. Deliberate contempt, and it reads as such; noted only so the owner confirms it is intended.
- **p46 word count.** The prose field is at 138 words, the exceptional ceiling. Any revision that touches p46 must split rather than add; that is why finding 5 pays the teaching promise on p20.
- **p46 page-turn line** says "he is, for the first time, waiting"; p39 already gave him his first wait ("He had never waited for anything. He waited."). The note does not render; correct it to "waiting, and this time for someone."
- **Mode balance.** 28 / 8 / 10 = 61% / 17% / 22% against a rough 60 / 20 / 13. Spectacle runs heavy and prose light. Every spectacle page is earned for this audience, and the audit did not warn; but if the owner wants the ratio closer, p21 (the South Gate) is the candidate for conversion to illustrated prose. Finding 7's added dramatic page will nudge the ratio anyway.
- **Ledger check.** Voices match the ledger on every page. Subhuti's p11 instruction is the wordiest he gets and is the one place "few words, every one a test" bends; it is still his. The ledger's costume state (a) "kilt of leaves" is never mentioned in the script, which is correct — it belongs to the reference plan, not the page.
- **Five-word balloons.** All 36 audited. Every one is either a complete thought or a direct answer to a complete question in the same panel with one visible referent. None fails. "All of it" (p20) admits two readings (all of Heaven / heard all of it); both are true, so it passes.
