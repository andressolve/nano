## Cold read

A stone on the peak of an empty mountain cracks and a gold monkey climbs out, looking at me. The brown monkeys take him in; the eldest, Old Ma, puts her hand on his head and says "You're here." He grows up loud and fast and does not know what he is. On a hot day Old Ma names the prize — whoever goes through the waterfall and comes back is king — and he is in the water before she finishes. Behind it is a dry stone house big enough for everyone. She crowns him ("Bow your head, or whatever it is kings do") and it is the happiest page in the book, which the script says out loud in its page-turn line and is right about. That night he asks how old "very" is and learns that kings die and only immortals don't. His answer is to leave.

A raft, a season at sea, three years walking on two legs in a coat, one old woman who points at a mountain: the Master there has never taught anyone the one thing he came for. The Master refuses him at the gate before he speaks, then hears "I came out of a rock," and gives him a name instead of an answer. Seven years of sweeping and "not yet." Three taps on the head in front of the laughing students, and he is the only one who reads it: midnight, back door. He gets two things — the shape and the cloud — and a warning: use them to be admired and you go alone. He is a pine tree in the courtyard by morning. Thrown out, and told never to say whose student he was. Home, Old Ma has counted ten years and a season and can tell from his face that he was thrown out. He wants a name of his own, and a king needs a weapon, and nobody has ever gone and asked the Dragon King.

He walks into the sea. The Dragon King, exquisitely polite, wants him gone and offers him the one thing nobody can lift to be rid of him. The pillar is warm, and humming, and shrinks when told to, and goes behind his ear as a needle. Armor, plumes, "Now that is a king." The Dragon King, alone, notes that the sea is still up and nobody told him, and writes to Heaven. In Heaven, the Emperor wants never to hear the name again; Laozi proposes a job because a monkey with a title holds still; the Emperor tells him to go fetch the monkey himself.

Wukong says yes before asking what the post is, and Old Ma notices, and says "Come back." The gate is open, so he decides they want him. Keeper of the Heavenly Horses — "Is that a high post?" "It is a post in Heaven." A month of being good at something. Then he asks Laozi straight and Laozi tells him straight: below rank. The desk in two pieces. Home, he paints GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN on a banner. The army comes; it takes him until lunch. The Emperor, standing for the first time, hears Laozi say give him the words, a house, no duties, and he'll sit in it. Old Ma asks why go back up; because Heaven said his name the way he wrote it and he wants to hear it up there. "Come back." "I always do." The mansion feels like the stable with a better roof. They put a monkey in charge of the peaches, and Laozi knew.

He starts at the top, the ones nobody has ever been allowed. Laozi comes with a basket for the banquet — "For tonight?" — every immortal of rank, and "The title was so that you would not need one." "Am I invited?" "No." He eats the party alone before it starts, apologizes to a peach, walks drunk toward the red door, finds Laozi's gourds, eats five gourds of "sweets" thinking of Old Ma, and sits up sober and glowing: that was the elixir, and he feels wonderful. Laozi, for the first time not dabbing his brow: no more offers, send Erlang.

Erlang introduces himself and asks whether the banner is true. "Three eyes. Which one do I hit first?" — nobody has ever asked him that. They fight shape for shape, down to the water; a temple appears on the slope with a furred flagpole round the back; "It's a new style"; "That was before the dog!" Laozi, from above, drops an iron ring on the back of his head, apologizing. Face-down under his own banner, laughing: it took all of you, say that in the hall.

In the hall, Erlang says it. Kneel. Equals don't kneel. Laozi wants his peaches and pills back and has a furnace for that; "I'm made of a rock." Forty-nine days. He fights the dark for a day, finds the one thread of cold, curls around it, and waits — he has never waited for anything. The lid goes up, the staff leaps to his hand, his eyes are fire now. "Stable boy. Empty house. No invitation. A furnace. That's every idea you had." He wants the throne. Laozi has nothing. The Emperor, eyes closed, sends west to the one who owes Heaven nothing. The Buddha arrives seated, asks what he wants the throne for — so they can't say I'm nobody — and offers a bet: jump out of my hand. Five pillars at the end of the world; he signs the middle one and pees on it. Back on the palm, told to look down: his writing on the second joint of the middle finger, and a wet patch. "Yes. Now hold still. Five hundred years should do it. Then someone will come for you." The hand becomes a mountain beside his own mountain. Winters. He can see his banner going pink across the water. Every year an old gray monkey in a red shawl climbs up with a peach and says the Buddha's words back to him, for as many years as she has; then a younger one who was told to. Someone will come. Wait.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed on the cold read:** p40→p41, "at forty-eight he stood up" then "Forty-nine days" — a half-second of arithmetic, resolved as stood up on 48, lid on 49; not a repair of story. p47, "an old gray monkey" — I knew it was Old Ma from the shawl, the stick, the peach, and the sitting; I wanted the name and did not need it. Nothing else. I never guessed at a motive, a relationship, a location, or a step.

## Is it alive?

Yes. I am supposed to say whether I would read it; I would, and I would read it to a seven-year-old, and the seven-year-old would make me read the temple page twice.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** Appetite from the first line of dialogue: "So somebody should go and look." Heat on p6 is real and specific — an old mother telling a child that kings die, and then having to watch what that sentence does to him, and p7's prose knows she knows ("because she had been the one who told him about immortals, and she knew it"). Old Ma's voice is fully hers — "Bow your head, or whatever it is kings do"; "May you never get tired of it." The softest page is p3 (the rule stated), and even that has "King. Of all of you?" carrying want. Not merely competent; I felt the crowning.

**Movement II (pp 8–14).** The Master's voice is distinct enough to strip names from: level, brief, every line a test. The three taps are a puzzle the reader gets to solve alongside the monkey, and the page-turn line ("the monkey was the only one who got the joke") is true of what is on the page. Subtext is the engine here: "Don't tell the others"; "Master, I'd never" with the grin already on him; "I know what you wanted. Go." The best line in the movement is Old Ma's "You came back on a cloud and your face was wrong." The weakest join is the weapon pivot on p14, and it is bridged in his voice with a callback to "shouted the loudest," so it holds.

**Movement III (pp 15–19).** Ao Guang is a joy — polite to the edge of panic, "little neighbor," "I am too old to find out." What he wants from Wukong (to leave) and what Wukong wants from him (something with weight in it) are opposed on every line. "Do you know what you have just done?" / "Yes. I've got a staff." is exactly the protagonist. The letter is a letter with a want, not logistics. p19 has a real quarrel between a man who wants quiet and a man who wants permission, and the Emperor's "Go and fetch him yourself" is punishment disguised as assent.

**Movement IV (pp 20–29).** The strongest movement. Every dramatic page has someone saying something other than what they mean: "It is a post in Heaven. Come along, Keeper"; "Who cares!"; "He'll never need to come in it, Majesty." The heat between Wukong and Old Ma on p20 and p28 is the book's spine and it is written in two words she repeats. p24 is the best kind of scene: the honest man tells the truth and it detonates. "They laughed at me up there, Old Ma. Quietly. Which is worse." p29's "It felt like the stable, with a better roof" is the whole book in a sentence.

**Movement V (pp 30–34).** "Am I invited?" / "No." earns its one-word answer. Two solo pages (32, 33) stay alive because he talks to a peach and thinks of Old Ma while eating the elixir, which is the tenderest and funniest thing in the book and sets up p47 without saying so. Laozi's turn on p34 — no dabbing, "No more offers" — is enacted in a face and a dropped tray, not narrated.

**Movement VI (pp 35–38).** The funniest pages, and the ones with the most heat between equals. Erlang is the only person in the book who talks to Wukong the way Wukong talks to everyone, and the script knows it ("Nobody has ever asked me that"). The flagpole gag is enacted, not explained. Laozi's ring from behind is the correct betrayal for this Laozi, and Erlang's "That wasn't me. I want that understood" keeps his voice intact in defeat.

**Movement VII (pp 39–47).** The furnace prose on p40 is the best writing in the script — "He had never waited for anything. He waited." The Buddha asks questions and never sermonizes; "Yes. Now hold still." is perfect. The pee on the pillar is exactly the right register for the readers, and the wet patch on the finger pays it. p47 lands: the banner going pink, the peach on the rock, "for as many years as she had," and the last four words with a source the reader saw spoken on p45. I felt this ending.

**Want-from, page by page:** every dramatic page has a speaker who wants something from the other person in the room, and none of them are "to inform." **Attribution-strip:** eight speaking characters, eight recognizable voices; I could cover the labels on any page and assign the lines. **Logistics:** none found; the only arrangement-shaped lines (Ao Guang's letter, Laozi's forty peaches) each carry a want and trigger the next turn. **Aphorism:** none; the lines that sound like maxims ("A monkey with a title holds still"; "Whatever goes in comes out as what it was made of") are the speaker's actual plan, tested and failed on the page. **Serene protagonist:** never, including under the mountain. **Inverted pages:** none; the rout on p26 and the furnace on p41 are shown, and their meetings come after.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p40 / p41 — 48 and 49.** "At forty-eight he stood up" then the caption "Forty-nine days." A careful ten-year-old does one beat of arithmetic. Either make the early exit the point (he stood up on forty-eight because he would not wait to be let out; caption "Forty-eight days. One short.") or let the caption count the sentence and the prose the standing. The first is the better beat; both are polish.

2. **p47 — "an old gray monkey."** The shawl, the stick, the peach, and the sitting identify her without the name, so this is not a gap. But this is the page where the reader most wants to hear "Old Ma" once more, and the withheld name reads as elegy to an adult and as a small distance to a seven-year-old. "Old Ma climbed up" would cost nothing and lose nothing of "after that a younger monkey did it."

3. **The promise to teach the troop** (p14 "I'm going to teach every one of you"; p20 "You haven't yet" / "When I'm back. When I'm a lord") is never spoken of again and is paid silently on p47 by Old Ma dying mortal while he watches from under a mountain. The silence works. If the owner wants it to land harder, one touch on p28 in Old Ma's mouth ("You have a mountain and a name, and a troop you promised something") would do it; if not, leave it.

4. **p7 and p47 both sit at 138 prose words**, two under the exceptional ceiling. Both are the right length for what they do and both fields are over plain ground (flat sea, snow). Flag for the packet: compose the prose field first on both, and if either ever grows by a sentence, split rather than tighten.

5. **p29 — "the Emperor, who wanted him busy, found him a duty"** after "no duties" on p27. The motive is stated, so no repair is required; it could be one clause sharper ("who did not like a monkey with nothing to do") so the reversal reads as the Emperor's character rather than a change of plan.

6. **p26.** If any page in the book ever earns one more, it is the rout of Heaven's army, which the readers this is for would happily watch for two pages. I would not add it: "It took him until lunch" is the joke, and a longer rout would take the real fight away from Erlang. Noted so nobody later mistakes the brevity for a defect.

7. **p9 "the monkey did not write" / p25 painting characters / p44 scratching letters.** "Did not write" plainly means did not take notes; no reader will stop. Recorded only so it is a known choice.

8. **p46 — the paper seal.** The caption says "A paper seal followed, to hold it shut" without saying whose. The hand is the Buddha's and the seal is gold and falls from where the hand came from; the picture answers it. Fine as is.
