VERDICT: REVISE

*(Saved verbatim by the orchestrating session from the fresh round-2 script-critic agent's reply; the critic's toolset is read-only.)*

## Cold read

What I read, as someone who has never met this story:

A stone on a mountain peak cracks and a gold monkey climbs out. The other monkeys are brown; the eldest, Old Ma, puts her hand on his head and lets him stay. Years later, at the waterfall, Old Ma says whoever goes through the water and comes back gets made king; he goes, finds a dry stone house behind the falls big enough for everyone, and is crowned. That night he asks how old she is, learns that kings die and only immortals do not, and decides to go find one. Raft, sea, three years walking among men, an old woman points him at a gray mountain with one pine. The Master there refuses him, then names him Sun Wukong and hands him a broom. Seven years of sweeping and "not yet." Then three taps on the head and hands behind the back, which Wukong alone decodes as midnight, back door; at midnight the Master gives him the shape and the cloud and warns him never to use them to be admired. He immediately turns into a pine tree for applause and is thrown out with an order never to say whose student he was. Home: Old Ma reads it on his face. Needing a weapon to make a name of his own, he walks into the sea, refuses the Dragon King's sword and halberd, talks the sea's anchor down to a needle, demands armor, and tells the Dragon King his name and boast. The Dragon King writes to Heaven. Laozi talks the Jade Emperor out of soldiers and into a title, then fetches Wukong himself; Old Ma asks what the post is, he says who cares, she says come back. The post is stable keeper; he is good at it and happy for a month, then asks the rank and is told it is below rank. He smashes the desk, goes home, paints GREAT SAGE EQUAL TO HEAVEN on a banner, beats Heaven's army by lunch, and Laozi returns to read the banner back to him as an imperial decree. He goes up again, because they said his name. Gold house, nothing to do, then a peach garden to guard. He eats the top peaches, learns from Laozi that there is a banquet tonight and he is not invited, eats the banquet alone, wanders drunk into Laozi's laboratory, eats five gourds of elixir thinking they are sweets, and sobers up feeling wonderful. Laozi: no more offers, send Erlang. Erlang, the Emperor's three-eyed nephew, is the first person who talks to him as an equal; they fight shape for shape, Wukong hides as a temple with a flagpole tail, runs from the dog, and Laozi's iron ring takes him from behind. In the hall he refuses to kneel; Laozi puts him in the furnace for forty-nine days to get his pills back; Wukong finds the one cold corner and, for the first time in his life, waits. He blows the lid, backs the Emperor against his own throne, and the Emperor sends west to the Buddha. The Buddha bets him he cannot jump out of his hand; he somersaults to the five pillars at the end of the world, signs the middle one, pees on it, comes back, and the pillars were the Buddha's fingers. The hand comes down beside his own mountain and becomes a mountain; he is under it with his head out. Old Ma climbs up every year with a peach until she runs out of years; then a younger monkey does. Someone will come.

Where I stopped on the first read:

- **p7 → p8.** The old woman says the Master "has not taken a student in forty years." The next page opens on a courtyard of students with brooms, and the Master says "I do not take students" in front of them. I went back to check whether I had misread.
- **p30 → p31.** p30 says nobody has ever been allowed to eat a top peach. p31, Laozi needs forty top peaches for his elixir and forty more for tonight's banquet. So they are eaten, by Laozi and at banquets. I stopped.
- **p42 → p43.** Wukong asks "Who's the Buddha?" The next page does not answer, and nobody ever does.
- **p47, last line.** "Someone will come, she had said. Wait." I did not remember Old Ma saying it, went back through her every line, and she never does. Nor could she know it.
- Smaller hitches I noted and moved past: p11 "you will leave here without me"; p33 how he knows the room is Laozi's; p34 "Five hundred years" of what; p38 "the last thing I have that is mine."

## Is it alive?

Yes. This script is alive, and I want to say that clearly before the findings, because the findings are gaps, not a dead register.

**Movement I (pp 1–7).** The engine is set in seven pages and it is the right engine: not "can he beat Heaven" but "will anyone ever say he belongs." Old Ma's hand on the head is the book's recurring gesture and it is planted on p2 and paid on p5, p14, p20, p28, and by absence on p47. p6 is the best early page: Wukong asks how old "very" is, and the answer is that kings die; his response is not grief but a plan ("I'll make him teach me, and none of us has to die"). That is appetite, and it is the appetite that runs to p45. Heat is real: Old Ma knows she set him going ("because she had been the one who told him about immortals, and she knew it").

**Movement II (pp 8–14).** Subhuti passes the attribution-strip cold; nobody else in the book speaks in tests. The three-taps page (p10) is a delight: the students laugh, the monkey alone gets the joke, and the reader is let in on it in his voice. p11 has the one line in the book that could tip into aphorism ("That is all immortality is") and it is saved by "Don't tell the others," which makes it a secret between two people rather than a theme. p13's expulsion hurts because p8's gift was a name and the punishment is silence about it. p14 is the best-written Old Ma page: "You came back on a cloud and your face was wrong. Never mind. You came back." I felt that.

**Movement III (pp 15–19).** Fun, fast, and the Dragon King is a distinct voice ("little neighbor," "I am too old to find out"). The staff wanting to be found ("Like it's been waiting") gives an object a want, which is exactly the right move for this reader. p18 is the first time Wukong writes his own legend into someone else's mouth, and the script knows it. Lowest heat of the seven, and correctly the shortest.

**Movement IV (pp 20–29).** This is where the book becomes a book. The want-from is perfect on p20: Laozi wants him on the cloud, Old Ma wants the name of the post, Wukong wants to be sent for. "Nobody has ever sent for me" is the whole novel in five words. p22's unanswered question ("Is that a high post?" / "It is a post in Heaven") is the best subtext in the script. p23 is genuinely lovely prose: "The horses were fat and shining, and that was the ledger." p24 turns on Laozi refusing to lie, which makes him more dangerous, not less. p28 earns its place: "Because they said it" is a reason no other character could give. p29's "It felt like the stable, with a better roof" is the aftermath the mandate demands.

**Movement V (pp 30–34).** p31 is the sharpest dramatic page in the book. "The title was so that you would not need one" / "Say it plainly. Am I invited?" / "No." Laozi's evasion, Wukong's refusal to accept evasion, the one-word answer that lands as a blow. The drunk banquet (p32) is the funniest page for a seven-year-old and it is also a clear turn. p33's "Oh, no. Those weren't sweets... And I feel wonderful" is the voice working. p34 gives Laozi his turn: "No more offers."

**Movement VI (pp 35–38).** Erlang is the character I would most want more of, which is the right amount. "Three eyes. Which one do I hit first?" / "Nobody has ever asked me that." The temple with the flagpole out the back is exactly the joke this audience wants, and "That was before the dog!" is the best short line in the script. p38 gives the loss to Laozi, not to Erlang, and Erlang says so, which protects both their distinct responsibilities.

**Movement VII (pp 39–47).** p39 is compact and hard: "Equals don't kneel. You said it in front of everyone." / "You are made of my peaches and my elixir. I want them back." / "I'm made of a rock." p40 is the heart of the book and the writer knows it: "He had never waited for anything. He waited." p42's list ("Stable boy. Empty house. No invitation. A furnace.") is the protagonist naming his own book without an aphorism in it. The Buddha is slow and plain and asks questions, as the ledger promises. The pillars and the wet patch will make two boys shout. p47 is a true ending: the promise to teach the troop, made on p14 and called on p20, is never kept, and Old Ma climbs the mountain with a peach for as many years as she has. I did not expect to be moved by a script gate, and I was.

Would I read it? Yes. Would I hand it to a ten-year-old and a seven-year-old? Yes, after the four gaps below are closed, because each of them is the kind that a ten-year-old catches and asks about, and one of them is the last line of the book.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p7 / p8 / p11 — the Master "has not taken a student in forty years," and the next page is full of students.**

Quote, p7: "the Master lives there, and he has not taken a student in forty years." Quote, p8, staged in a courtyard where "Students with shaved heads and brown robes have stopped sweeping to stare": "I do not take students. Go home to your parents." Quote, p11: "You are the first in forty years to understand a simple thing."

What the reader repairs: a direct contradiction across a page turn. Either the students are not students, or the old woman was wrong, or "take" means something a seven-year-old cannot supply. The reader also has to decide whether the Master is lying on p8, which colours everything he says afterward. The ledger locks the students as young, so "they were admitted more than forty years ago" is not available.

Prescription: make the refusal about what Wukong asks for, not about admission, and let p11's "forty years" carry the real claim (nobody has passed his test). Concretely, p7: "the Master lives there. He has students, and he has never once taught any of them what you are asking for." p8, Subhuti: "I do not teach what you came for. Nobody learns it here. Go home to your parents." p11 stays as written and now lands as the payoff. If the writer prefers to keep the old woman's "forty years," it must be "forty years since anyone got past his door" and p8's refusal must be reworded to match.

**2. p30 / p31 — nobody has ever eaten a top peach, and Laozi harvests them by the forty.**

Quote, p30: "The ones at the top ripened every nine thousand years, and nobody remembered what they did, because nobody had ever been allowed to eat one." Quote, p31, next page: "I need forty of the top peaches for my elixir and forty more for tonight."

What the reader repairs: whether the top peaches are forbidden or routine. p33 and p39 then depend on Laozi's elixir being made of these peaches, so the contradiction propagates into the mechanism of the ending.

Prescription: keep p30 intact (it is the better sentence and it makes Wukong's transgression bigger) and move Laozi's need to the middle orchard: "I need forty of the middle peaches for my elixir and forty more for tonight. Where are the—" with his eyes going up to the top tree where the feet are hanging. p39's "You are made of my peaches and my elixir" still holds. Alternatively, if the top peaches must be Laozi's, add one clause on p31 that this is the first time they have ever ripened ("They're ripe. The first time. Forty for my elixir and forty for tonight"); that is weaker because p30 then reads as a setup the text immediately undoes.

**3. p42 / p43 — "Who's the Buddha?" is asked in rendered text and never answered.**

Quote, p42: "Then send west. To the Buddha. Tell him Heaven is asking." / "Who's the Buddha?" Quote, p43: "Heaven asked, so I came. Sit down a moment."

What the reader repairs: who this being is, why the ruler of Heaven sends to him "like a man giving up a secret," why his word can settle the throne ("I'll say so myself"), and why the Emperor, who has never deferred to anyone, stands against the wall. The script closes the arrival gap (good) but not the placement gap. The climax and the ending are decided by the only major character the book never places, and the script puts the reader's question in Wukong's mouth and then walks past it. A seven-year-old will ask it out loud at the page turn.

Prescription: answer it in one line from the person who has most reason to fear the answer, on p42 panel 3, where the budget has room (52 words): LAOZI: "The one who lives west of everything and owes Heaven nothing. Heaven has never asked him for anything. Until now." Or give the Buddha his own placement on p43 panel 1: "I am the Buddha. I live west of everything, and Heaven does not usually ask me for things. It asked. So I came. Sit down a moment." One of these, not both. Whichever is chosen, the "why you turn" line on p42 should stop relying on "the only thing that scared Laozi," which is not on the page.

**4. p47 — the last line of the book is a line from a scene the reader never saw, from a character who could not know it.**

Quote: "Someone will come, she had said. Wait."

What the reader repairs: when Old Ma said this, and how she could. She is never on any page after p28's "Come back" / "I always do," and nobody on the mountain hears the Buddha's terms. The reader has to invent a scene at the cleft in which Old Ma makes a promise she has no basis for, and then has to decide whether it is a comfort or a truth. That is the sentence the whole book ends on, and a ten-year-old will say "who's going to come? how does she know?" The Buddha's "you go back to Earth, and you stay there" on p43 points the other way.

Prescription: put the promise in a mouth that can know it, in rendered text, before p47. The Buddha on p45, after "Now hold still. Five hundred years should do it," adds: "Then someone will come for you. Wait for him." That fits the ledger (his want is for the monkey to learn to wait) and closes the loop with p40. p47's last line becomes "Someone will come, he had said. Wait." If the owner wants the line to remain Old Ma's, she must say it on the page, with her honesty intact: p47 prose gives her a spoken sentence at the cleft ("Someone will come. I don't know who. Wait for them, the way I waited for you.") and the last line changes to "she said." Note p47 is at 138 prose words, the exceptional ceiling; if words are added there, take them from "He learned what a year was, and how many there were in five hundred, because that was the Buddha's number," which the page can survive without, rather than shrinking anything.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p8, Subhuti's turn from "Go home" to a name.** What changed his mind is inferable (a child from a rock, with no parents and no name) but not shown as a choice. One beat before the naming — even Subhuti repeating "Out of a rock" while the whisk stays still — would make it chosen rather than reported. Not blocking because the broom and the name make the acceptance itself unambiguous.
- **p11, "Use them to be admired, and you will leave here without me."** A seven-year-old may hear "I won't come with you." Consider "and I will send you away, and you will go alone." p13's "I said what would happen" then has a plainer antecedent.
- **p33, "Laozi's place. Ha. The peach man."** He identifies the room by sight; the reader identifies it by the shelves of gourds matching the gourd on Laozi's hip, which is a visual tag only. Cheap insurance: "Gourds. Laozi's gourds. This is Laozi's place."
- **p34, "He ate the elixir. All five gourds. Five hundred years."** Five hundred years of making, or of life granted? Either reading works for the plot, but "Five hundred years of my work" removes the wobble and makes the ring on p38 read as personal.
- **p38, "This is the last thing I have that is mine."** Beautiful for an adult; opaque for the younger reader, who has not been told that the ring is Laozi's own rather than Heaven's. Budget allows a preceding balloon: "I offered you everything Heaven had. This is the last thing I have that is mine."
- **p43, "So they can't ever say I'm nobody."** This is the one place the book's theme is spoken aloud. It works because it is a blunt answer to a direct question from the only being who asked, and because it is in his voice. Do not let any revision add a second such line anywhere; one is the limit.
- **Balance.** 29/8/10 is 62/17/21 against 60/20/13. Every spectacle page is earned (arrival, wonder, the shape-battle, the lid, the pillars, the hand) and I would not cut one; noting only that the book leans on spectacle and could take one more prose page if a future round adds one.
- **p26, "It took him until lunch."** The defeat of Heaven's army is a strip and a joke rather than a dramatized fight. For this audience it is the right call, and p27 supplies the aftermath. Recording it so nobody mistakes it for an inverted page: the dominant image is the army coming down and the monkey with the tree-trunk staff, which is the event.
- **Five-word audit.** All thirty-three pass. The ones I checked hardest: "Then you have learned nothing." (p10, intentionally misread by the students and correctly read by Wukong on the same page); "Good." (p35, one visible referent, the strike, and in character); "I always do." (p28, answer to "Come back" in the same panel); "No." (p31, answer to a complete question in the same panel); "I'm made of a rock." (p39, complete thought, and the setup for the furnace).
- **Continuity checked and clean:** the clock ("a season" + three years + seven years = "ten years and a season," p14); the staff as needle behind the ear from p17 through p44 and its unreachability on p47; the staff left on the throne step p39 and retrieved p41; the five gourds from p33 to p34 to p39 to p42; the ring off by p39; the promise to teach the troop made p14, called p20, and left unkept on p47, which is the point.
