# Cold read: pages 1–40

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands on its own. I could follow the plot, distinguish the recurring characters, understand why Wukong made each major choice, and read the dialogue at pace without consulting anything else.

## Retell of the story so far

A stone cracks open on the uninhabited peak of Flower-Fruit Mountain and produces a golden young monkey. The mountain's brown monkeys take him in, and their elder, Old Ma, treats him like one of their newborns. As he grows, he becomes faster, louder, and bolder than the rest. When the monkeys wonder what lies behind their waterfall, he accepts the old rule that whoever goes through and returns alive will be king. He finds an empty stone home behind the water, brings everyone inside, and is acclaimed the Handsome Monkey King.

Old Ma eventually tells him that three kings before him have died and that only immortals escape death. Wukong resolves to find one, learn the secret, and save all the monkeys from dying. He crosses the sea, passes through the human world, and reaches a master's mountain. The Master first turns him away because he has no parents or name, but the stone-born monkey's persistence interests him. The Master names him Sun Wukong and keeps him as a student.

Wukong spends seven years doing chores and listening to lessons. When the Master asks what he has learned, Wukong understands three taps of the Master's staff as a private summons at the third watch. In secret, the Master teaches him two powers: changing shape and traveling vast distances by cloud-somersault. He explains that being able to become anything and go anywhere makes a being extremely hard to kill, which is the practical meaning of immortality. He warns Wukong not to show off and says that if Wukong does, he will send him away and deny having taught him. Wukong immediately delights the other students by turning into a pine, so the Master expels him exactly as warned.

Wukong returns to Flower-Fruit Mountain after ten years and a season, announces that he cannot die, and promises to teach the other monkeys. Old Ma sees from his expression and cloud arrival that he was thrown out. When Wukong declares that a king needs a weapon, she sends him to the Dragon King beneath the eastern sea. Wukong walks underwater into the sea palace, rejects the weapons offered as too light, and lifts the enormous pillar that anchors the sea. The pillar responds to him and shrinks at his command into a staff. He also demands armor, a feathered cap, and cloud-walking boots. The alarmed Dragon King equips him, then reports to the Jade Emperor that Wukong stole the sea's supporting pillar and claims he cannot be killed.

The Jade Emperor initially wants soldiers sent after Wukong, but Laozi proposes giving him a minor job in Heaven instead. Laozi summons Wukong, who is thrilled that Heaven has heard of him and leaves despite Old Ma's reminder that he still has not taught the monkeys not to die. Heaven's open gate convinces Wukong that he is welcome. The Jade Emperor appoints him Keeper of the Heavenly Horses. Wukong mistakes the heavenly location for proof that it is a high office. He is excellent at the work and genuinely loves the horses, but when Laozi finally admits that the job has no rank and is essentially stable-boy work, Wukong smashes his desk and returns home in fury.

Feeling that Heaven laughed at and tried to order him, Wukong invents his own title: Great Sage Equal to Heaven. He raises a banner bearing it. Heaven sends its entire army, but Wukong defeats them by lunchtime. Laozi persuades the offended Jade Emperor to grant Wukong the grand title, a golden house, and no duties, reasoning that the empty honor will keep him out of the imperial hall. Wukong accepts because Heaven has publicly read his chosen words and allowed his banner to stand, though Old Ma doubts the point of returning to a place he has just defeated.

Wukong soon grows bored in his idle heavenly house, so the Emperor makes him guardian of the peach garden. The highest peaches grant immortality and have never been available to him, but nobody explicitly tells him not to eat them. He begins devouring them. Laozi arrives to collect middle-grade peaches for the Emperor's banquet and reveals that every immortal of rank is invited while Wukong is not. Wukong takes this as proof that his title is another sham. He enters the empty banquet hall, consumes the feast and wine meant for the important guests, then drunkenly follows a red-lit passage while trying to go home. It leads to Laozi's guarded store, where he mistakes Laozi's elixir pills for sweets and eats all five gourds.

With the banquet wrecked and five hundred years of Laozi's work gone, the Jade Emperor orders a final military response. Laozi recommends Erlang, the Emperor's three-eyed nephew, because Erlang will enjoy a real fight. Erlang arrives with his black hound, challenges the truth of Wukong's banner, and is pleased when Wukong attacks instead of fleeing. They match each other weapon for weapon and transformation for transformation. Wukong finally disguises himself as a temple, but Erlang notices that the supposed flagpole is a tail and threatens to kick in the door. Wukong breaks the disguise and runs, joking that his promise not to run was made before the dog joined the pursuit.

As Erlang, the dog, and Heaven's army chase Wukong, Laozi apologizes from above and strikes Wukong from behind with his own bracelet. The blow drops him, and the combined force captures and chains him. In the imperial hall, Erlang honestly reports that it took all of them, including Laozi, to bring Wukong down. The Jade Emperor orders the Great Sage Equal to Heaven to kneel, but Wukong refuses because Heaven publicly granted him a title declaring him equal. Laozi proposes putting Wukong in his forty-nine-day furnace to recover the forbidden peaches and elixir. Wukong answers that he was made from a rock. The Jade Emperor orders Laozi to proceed and insists that this attempt must work.

## Cast

- **Sun Wukong / the Handsome Monkey King / Great Sage Equal to Heaven:** A gold-furred monkey born from a split stone. He begins in a skirt of green leaves, later wearing ornate red-and-gold armor, cloud-walking boots, a small crown with long feathers, and carrying a red-and-gold size-changing staff. He is the protagonist: brave, quick, literal-minded, boastful, affectionate toward his mountain family, hungry for recognition, and furious at condescension.
- **Old Ma:** An elderly gray female ape with a deeply lined face, a red cloak, and a walking stick. She is the mountain troop's elder and Wukong's foster-maternal anchor. She explains death, sees through his claims, advises him, and repeatedly calls him back to his promise to the monkeys.
- **The Master:** A tall, slender old human with long white hair and beard, pale robes, and a white whisk. He is Wukong's austere spiritual teacher. He names Wukong, recognizes his unusual understanding, secretly teaches him transformations and cloud travel, then expels him for showing off.
- **Laozi:** A short, round elderly human with a bald crown, white side hair and beard, blue robes, and gourd vessels at his belt. He is a senior heavenly adviser, alchemist, and problem-solver. He repeatedly contains Wukong through bureaucratic schemes, then personally causes his capture with a bracelet.
- **The Jade Emperor:** A large, black-bearded man in elaborate gold robes and a square ceremonial crown, seated on Heaven's throne. He rules Heaven. He is offended by Wukong's title and destruction but repeatedly follows Laozi's less costly plans until those plans fail.
- **The Dragon King of the Eastern Sea:** A large green humanoid dragon with whiskers, coral-like antlers, claws, and richly patterned dark green robes. He rules the underwater palace, loses the sea-anchoring pillar and armor to Wukong, and reports the threat to Heaven.
- **Erlang:** A young, long-haired human warrior in silver armor with a third eye on his forehead, a red plume, and a long three-pointed spear. He is the Jade Emperor's nephew and the first opponent who can match Wukong's fighting and transformations. He values the challenge and tells the truth about how Wukong was captured.
- **Erlang's hound:** A lean black dog who accompanies Erlang and joins the pursuit. Its involvement breaks the one-against-one balance enough that Wukong starts running.
- **The monkeys of Flower-Fruit Mountain:** A troop of ordinary brown monkeys who adopt Wukong, make him king, live in the waterfall home he discovers, and cheer his defiance of Heaven.
- **The Master's students:** Shaven-headed human students in brown robes. They accept Wukong among them and applaud his forbidden pine transformation, indirectly prompting his expulsion.

## Causal chain

1. Wukong is born alone from stone, and the mountain monkeys give him a family.
2. His courage in crossing the waterfall wins him kingship and responsibility for that family.
3. Old Ma's explanation of death makes him seek immortality for himself and the troop.
4. His persistence and insight win him a name and secret powers from the Master.
5. His need to impress others makes him reveal those powers, so the Master expels him.
6. His new strength and belief that a king needs a weapon lead him to take the Dragon King's sea-anchoring pillar and armor.
7. The Dragon King's complaint brings Wukong to Heaven's attention.
8. Laozi's attempt to neutralize him with a lowly stable job works briefly because Wukong loves the horses, but concealment of the job's status turns pride into rebellion.
9. Wukong's self-bestowed title draws Heaven's army; defeating it forces Heaven to recognize the title publicly.
10. Heaven's second containment plan gives him honor without belonging or purpose; boredom leads to the peach-garden assignment.
11. Wukong eats the immortality peaches, then learns his impressive title still does not earn him an invitation to the banquet.
12. Feeling insulted again, he consumes the banquet and, while drunk, accidentally enters Laozi's store and eats the elixir.
13. The accumulated thefts and humiliation make the Jade Emperor send Erlang and the whole army.
14. Erlang can match Wukong, but the dog, army, and Laozi's surprise blow are all needed to capture him.
15. Wukong's stone birth and layers of acquired immortality now make ordinary punishment useless, so Heaven turns to Laozi's furnace.

## Backtracking, rereading, or guesses

No page required backtracking or rereading.

Pages 36–38 ask the reader to infer action visually, but the inference was immediate rather than a repair. On page 36, Wukong's forms remain gold while Erlang's remain gray, so I understood who was chasing whom. On page 37, the tail-shaped flagpole and the eye visible through the temple window identified the temple as Wukong's disguise before he fled. On page 38, Laozi's position overhead, the flying bracelet, and Erlang's denial made it clear that Laozi struck Wukong from behind. I did not misidentify any recurring character.

## Text and attribution at reading pace

No text failed at reading pace, and no speech attribution was ambiguous enough to interrupt the read. The narration pages carried the time jumps cleanly. The two recurring elderly human men remained distinct: the Master is tall and pale-robed with long hair and beard, while Laozi is short, round, bald-topped, and blue-robed.

## What I expect next

Laozi will put Wukong into the furnace, expecting to reduce him to his original stone substance and recover the immortality peaches and elixir. I expect the plan to fail because his stone birth, the Master's training, the peaches, and the elixir have made him harder to destroy than Heaven understands. The forty-nine days may transform or strengthen him instead. He will probably escape, recover his staff, and answer the humiliation of capture with a larger assault on Heaven. Erlang may remain a respected rival rather than a simple enemy because he fought fairly and publicly credited everyone involved in the capture. Old Ma and the monkeys remain the emotional reason Wukong will eventually return to Flower-Fruit Mountain.
