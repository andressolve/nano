## Retell of the story so far

A golden young monkey is born when a stone splits open on the empty peak of Flower-Fruit Mountain. The mountain's brown monkeys gather around him, and their elderly leader, Old Ma, accepts him even though he has no mother and no idea what he is. He grows up faster, louder, and more daring than the others.

The monkeys do not know what lies behind their waterfall. Their rule is that whoever can pass through it alive and return will become king. The golden monkey volunteers, dives through, and discovers a dry stone home behind the water with beds, bowls, a bridge, and space for everyone. He returns, reminds the others of their rule, and is acclaimed the Handsome Monkey King.

Later, he asks Old Ma about age and learns that three kings before him have died. She tells him that everything dies except immortals and that stories place such beings far to the west, beyond the sea. Determined to keep himself and his monkeys from dying, he builds a raft, crosses the sea, learns enough human ways to travel among people, and finds a secluded Master who has refused this question for forty years. The Master initially turns him away, but the monkey's origin and persistence interest him. He gives the nameless monkey the name Sun Wukong, “the one who wakes to emptiness.”

Wukong spends seven years sweeping, working, and listening from the back of the Master's classes. When tested in public, he says he has learned everything except the one thing he came for. The Master taps him three times and leaves; the other students laugh, but Wukong understands the taps as a secret invitation to the back door at midnight. The Master privately teaches him two powers: changing shape into anything that has a body, and traveling a hundred thousand miles in one cloud-somersault. These abilities make him extremely difficult to kill. The Master warns him not to show off or reveal their connection.

Wukong immediately tests the cloud, then displays his shape-changing to the other students by becoming a pine. As warned, the Master expels him and orders him never to claim he was his student. Wukong returns to Flower-Fruit Mountain after ten years and a season away, telling Old Ma that he cannot die and will teach the others. Old Ma instantly sees that he was thrown out. She also tells him that a king without a weapon is merely a loud monkey, so he goes beneath the eastern sea to ask its Dragon King for one.

Wukong rejects the Dragon King's ordinary weapons as too light. The king shows him an immense pillar that has anchored the sea since its creation. Wukong alone can move it, and it obeys his wish to become small enough to wield as a staff. He then demands armor, a feathered cap, and cloud-gripping boots. Dressed as a warrior-king, he declares that he came from a stone and cannot be killed. The frightened Dragon King reports the theft of the sea's pillar to the Jade Emperor in Heaven.

The Jade Emperor wants soldiers sent, but his adviser Laozi proposes containing Wukong with a minor heavenly job instead. Laozi fetches him from the mountain. Wukong is thrilled that Heaven has heard of him, despite Old Ma's concern that the offer sounds self-serving. At Heaven's open gate, he assumes he is welcome. The Emperor appoints him Keeper of the Heavenly Horses. Wukong loves the work and transforms the neglected horses, never asking what rank the job carries. After a month, Laozi tells him it has no rank at all: it is work given to someone who “is not anyone yet,” effectively a stable-boy position. Humiliated, Wukong smashes the desk, rejects the post, and returns home.

On Flower-Fruit Mountain he creates his own title, Great Sage Equal to Heaven, and raises it on a red banner. Heaven's army descends in overwhelming numbers, but Wukong defeats the whole force by lunchtime. Laozi again advises the Emperor to give Wukong the title he wants, a golden house in Heaven, and no responsibilities, keeping him away from court. Wukong accepts the formally proclaimed title and goes back to Heaven, telling Old Ma he wants to make Heaven honor the name he chose.

The empty honor quickly becomes lonely and boring. The Emperor then gives him a duty guarding Heaven's peach garden. Laozi presents this as a great honor but neglects to explain that the three thousand six hundred trees bear peaches that grant wisdom, long life, or immortality, depending on how rarely they ripen. No one has explicitly told Wukong not to eat them. He starts with the rarest peaches and escalates from one per day to one per hour, leaving the pits where the gardeners will find them.

## Cast

- **Sun Wukong / the Handsome Monkey King / Great Sage Equal to Heaven:** A small, golden-furred monkey with a long tail and expressive amber eyes. He begins in a skirt of green leaves, later adding red-and-gold armor, cloud-gripping boots, and a feathered cap. He carries a red-and-gold staff that can change size. He is the rock-born protagonist: brave, quick to learn, loving toward his monkeys and the horses, hungry for immortality and recognition, and dangerously proud when he feels mocked or diminished.

- **Old Ma:** A large, stooped, gray elderly monkey with white facial hair, a walking stick, and a worn red wrap. She is the mountain elder who first accepts Wukong, confirms his kingship, tells him about death and immortals, pushes him to acquire a proper weapon, and repeatedly sees through his boasts. She functions as his mother figure, truth-teller, and cautious anchor.

- **The Master:** A tall, severe elderly human with white hair tied up, a long white beard, pale robes, and a white whisk. He leads a remote mountain school. He names Wukong, recognizes his intelligence, and secretly teaches him shape-changing and cloud travel, then expels him after his predictable boasting.

- **The Master's students:** Shaven-headed human men in plain brown robes. They work and study at the mountain school, dismiss Wukong, then become the audience whose laughter and applause tempt him to show off.

- **The Dragon King of the Eastern Sea:** A towering, sea-green, dragon-headed ruler with whiskers, coral-like horns, claws, and an ornate dark teal robe. He first treats Wukong as a presumptuous little neighbor, then becomes alarmed when Wukong lifts the pillar that anchors the sea. He supplies the staff and royal armor under pressure and reports Wukong to Heaven.

- **The Jade Emperor:** A broad, dark-bearded human ruler in elaborate gold robes and a hanging-bead crown, seated on a celestial throne. He governs Heaven. He dislikes hearing Wukong's name and initially favors force, but accepts Laozi's cheaper strategy of using titles and jobs to contain him.

- **Laozi:** A short, round-faced elderly human in blue robes, with a bald crown, wispy white hair and beard, and a gourd hanging at his side. He is the Jade Emperor's practical adviser and messenger. He fetches Wukong, explains the humiliating stable post, and engineers the empty title and later the peach-garden assignment.

- **The mountain monkeys:** A large community of mostly brown-furred monkeys. They become Wukong's subjects and family, move into the home behind the waterfall, celebrate his titles, and are the people he originally hopes to protect from death.

- **The Heavenly Horses and Heaven's soldiers:** The horses reveal Wukong's capacity for patient, caring work. The black-armored soldiers embody Heaven's failed attempt to overpower him.

## Causal chain

The stone opens, so the nameless monkey enters the world alone. Old Ma and the troop accept him, giving him a family. His daring lets him cross the waterfall, so the troop gains a home and makes him king. Kingship leads him to ask what happened to earlier kings; learning that they died gives him the central goal of defeating death. That goal sends him across the sea to the Master. His unusual understanding earns secret training, but his need to impress others makes him reveal it and causes his expulsion. Back home, Old Ma's challenge about a weapon sends him to the Dragon King. His power lets him take the sea pillar and royal equipment, prompting the Dragon King's complaint to Heaven. Laozi's containment plan brings him to the celestial stables. Wukong succeeds because he cares about the horses, but discovering that Heaven considers him beneath rank turns pride into revolt. His self-declared title draws Heaven's army; defeating it forces the court to grant him the title without real status. The resulting idleness leads Heaven to assign him the peach garden. Laozi's omission, Wukong's appetite, and his continuing drive toward immortality lead directly to the theft of the rarest peaches.

## What I expect next

The missing peaches and discarded pits will expose Wukong almost immediately. Because the rarest fruit reinforces the immortality he originally sought, he will now be even harder for Heaven to control. I expect the Emperor's strategy of flattering him with hollow status to collapse into a more serious attempt to arrest or punish him, probably with forces or powers beyond the army he already defeated. I also expect Wukong to treat Heaven's response as another insult rather than recognize the scale of his theft, while Old Ma remains the person most able to see where his pride is taking him.
