## The cut list — named, unapologetic, famous material included

### Whole sequences cut

- **The raid on the Underworld** (ch. 3): Wukong dragged to Hell in his sleep,
  striking his name and the monkeys' names from the Book of Life and Death.
  Famous. Cut. It makes the same point as the Dragon King raid (he takes what
  he wants from a lower realm and they complain upward), and the sea gives the
  book an object, the staff, where Hell gives it only a grievance. His fear of
  death is answered by Subhuti's teaching, once, in Movement II.
- **The Demon King of Havoc** (ch. 2): the homecoming fight where Wukong
  reclaims the cave. Cut. Wukong comes home to a home. The volume's first real
  fight is in Heaven, where it counts.
- **Nezha and Heavenly King Li** (ch. 4): the first champions Heaven sends.
  Cut. The champion fight belongs to Erlang, whose duel of shapes is the
  better scene. Heaven's first response is the army, as spectacle, and it
  fails in a page and a half.
- **The six demon sworn brothers** (ch. 3–4), who suggest the title "Great
  Sage Equal to Heaven." Cut. Wukong names himself. It is his best line and
  nobody else gets to write it.
- **Guanyin's recommendation of Erlang** (ch. 6). Cut. Laozi proposes Erlang,
  because Laozi is the one who has run out of offers.
- **The three failed executions** (ch. 7: sword, fire, lightning). Cut. One
  furnace, forty-nine days, is the execution, and it fails by making him
  stronger.
- **The years wandering the human world** (ch. 1): eight or nine years of
  travel before he finds Subhuti. One illustrated-prose page.
- **The seventy-two transformations as a catalogue, the hair-clone trick, the
  staff's many names and exact weight, the armor from the three other dragon
  kings, the Star of Longevity's gifts, the seating of the peach banquet.**
  All cut. See the volume mandate: three tricks, no lists.

### Characters cut

Nezha, Heavenly King Li, the Demon King of Havoc, the six sworn brothers,
Guanyin, the Ten Kings of the Underworld, the Four Heavenly Kings, the
twenty-eight constellations, Erlang's six brothers, the Star of Longevity, the
Queen Mother's fairy maidens, the peach garden's gardeners, Marshals Ma and
Liu and Generals Beng and Ba (the named monkey officers), the ministers of the
heavenly court, the three other dragon kings.

### Characters merged

- **Gold Star of Venus + Laozi → Laozi.** One face does three jobs: proposes
  the job, proposes the empty title, and, after his own elixir is eaten, drops
  the snare and lights the furnace. Heaven's patience and Heaven's fury are one
  person, and the reader watches him turn.
- **The Queen Mother of the West + the Jade Emperor → the Jade Emperor.** The
  banquet is his. He is the one who did not invite Wukong, so the insult has a
  face the reader already knows.
- **The unnamed old monkey (ch. 1) + the monkey officers → Old Ma.** One elder,
  named, alive throughout: crowns him, warns him about death, receives him
  home, and holds the last page.

### Why the biggest cut pays for the volume

Cutting the Underworld raid and the two early fights (the Demon King, Nezha)
deletes three complete sets of production problems at the root: a fourth
realm with its own architecture and cast, two armored boy-warriors who would
collide visually with each other and with Erlang, and a second legendary
object (the Book of Life and Death) competing with the staff for the reader's
attention. It also fixes the story's shape. Without them, every escalation in
the book is between Wukong and Heaven, the reader never has to learn a new
enemy, and the seven movements climb in one direction.

## Protected claims

None required by the owner yet. **Proposed, pending owner decision:** the book
treats its source as a Chinese myth cycle and not as costume. Names are
standard pinyin (Sun Wukong, Ao Guang, Laozi, Erlang), the settings are drawn
from Chinese landscape and architecture rather than a generic "Eastern" look,
and no character speaks in pidgin, proverb, or fortune-cookie aphorism.

## Sensitive material and its treatment

- **Death.** The engine starts with the fear of it. Nobody dies on the page.
  Old Ma is old, not dying, and stays alive to the last page. Wukong's fear is
  a child's clear fear: *this ends, and I don't want it to.*
- **The furnace.** Forty-nine days in Laozi's furnace is, in the source, an
  execution by burning. Here it is staged as a forge: darkness, heat, the
  reader hears him rather than sees him burn, and he comes out tempered, with
  golden eyes that see through any shape. No injury on the page.
- **The mark on the pillar.** In the source Wukong writes his name on the
  Buddha's finger and then pees on it. This is the best joke in the book for a
  seven-year-old and it is the proof that he was there. **Owner decision:**
  keep both, keep the name only, or keep both with the second staged out of
  frame (a sound, a splash, Wukong's face). The brief recommends the third.
- **Religious figures.** Buddha, Laozi, and the Jade Emperor appear as beings
  in a myth with wants and tempers, not as objects of doctrine. No character
  preaches. The Buddha's power is stillness and scale, never a sermon.
- **Violence.** Heaven's army is spectacle: soldiers scattered, no wounds, no
  blood. Erlang's hound bites Wukong's leg; it is a humiliation, not an
  injury. Wukong never kills anyone in this volume.
