# Volume Architecture — 47 pages

Mythic China, no year. Three realms: Flower-Fruit Mountain and the sea below
it; the Master's mountain far to the west; and Heaven, above the clouds.

---

## I — THE STONE AND THE WATERFALL · pp 1–7

**Emotional entry:** wonder. **Exit:** a small raft on a huge sea, one monkey
standing on it, not looking back.

A stone on the peak of Flower-Fruit Mountain splits and a monkey climbs out.
Nobody made him. The monkeys take him in, and Old Ma, the eldest, is the first
to touch him. Years pass. On a hot day the troop dares each other to jump
through the waterfall; Old Ma names the prize: whoever goes through and comes
back is king. He goes, finds the Water Curtain Cave behind the water, a dry
stone house big enough for everyone, and leads them in. Old Ma crowns him.
There is a feast, and he is happy, until he asks her how old she is, and she
tells him that kings die too. Only immortals do not. He decides, that night,
in front of the whole troop, that he is going to find one and make them
teach him.

**The turn:** the happiest creature in the world learns that it ends, and
refuses.
**Page-turn out:** where do you find an immortal?

## II — THE MASTER · pp 8–14

**Emotional entry:** hunger. **Exit:** a monkey thrown out of the only place
that ever called him by name, somersaulting home anyway.

At the mountain of the Slanting Moon, the Master Subhuti asks him who he is
and he says he came out of a rock. Subhuti gives him a name, Sun Wukong, and a
broom. Years of sweeping. One day, in front of all the students, Subhuti hits
him three times on the head and walks off, and every student laughs at the
monkey, and only the monkey understands: three strikes means the third watch
of the night, come by the back door. At midnight Subhuti teaches him the two
things this book allows: how to change his shape and how to somersault a
hundred thousand miles on a cloud, and tells him plainly that the first is for
staying alive and the second is for leaving, and that a student who uses them
to be admired will lose his Master. The next morning Wukong turns into a pine
tree to make the students clap. Subhuti throws him out, and forbids him, for
ever, to tell anyone whose student he was. He goes home in one leap. Old Ma is
waiting. She has heard the old ones say there is a dragon king under the
eastern sea with a treasury of weapons.

**The turn:** he gets the tricks and loses the Master in the same day, and
learns nothing from it yet.
**Page-turn out:** a king needs a weapon.

## III — THE STAFF · pp 15–19

**Emotional entry:** appetite. **Exit:** a letter of complaint rising through
the water toward Heaven.

He walks into the sea. Ao Guang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, is polite the
way a large person is polite to a small one, and has his guards bring out a
sword, then a halberd, and the monkey laughs at each: too light. The Dragon
King, wanting him gone, mentions the one thing nobody can lift: the iron
pillar that has anchored the sea since the sea was made. Wukong puts his hand
on it, tells it to be smaller, and it obeys, and again, until it is a needle
he tucks behind his ear. Then he wants armor to go with it, and gets it,
because the Dragon King will give anything to end the visit. The moment he is
gone, Ao Guang writes to Heaven. In the Hall of Jade the Emperor reads the
letter with Laozi at his elbow. The Emperor wants quiet. Laozi has an idea:
give the monkey a job. A monkey with a title will hold still.

**The turn:** the sea's anchor is a needle behind his ear, and Heaven now
knows his name.
**Page-turn out:** Heaven is sending someone.

## IV — THE STABLE BOY · pp 20–29

**Emotional entry:** pride. **Exit:** a monkey in a golden mansion in Heaven
with nothing to do, and a peach garden being handed to him.

Laozi comes down to the mountain in person with the invitation, and Wukong,
who has never been invited to anything, says yes before he hears the job.
Old Ma tells him to come back. The South Gate of Heaven is bigger than the
sky. The Emperor names him Keeper of the Heavenly Horses, and he is thrilled,
and he is good at it, and the horses love him. Then he asks Laozi what rank a
Keeper of Horses holds, and Laozi, who does not lie, says it is below rank.
The wound opens. He breaks the stable desk and goes home and raises a banner
with a title he wrote himself: Great Sage Equal to Heaven. Heaven sends an
army. He meets it alone with the staff and the army goes home. In the Hall
the Emperor is furious and Laozi says: give him the title, it is only words,
and a title is cheaper than an army. Laozi brings the title down to the peak
and reads it out under the banner, and Old Ma asks why go back up, and he says:
because they said it. He goes. He gets a mansion, and nothing to do in it, and
the Emperor, wanting him busy, gives him the peach garden to guard.

**The turn:** Heaven tries to buy him with a name and finds out he wants a
real one.
**Page-turn out:** they have just put the monkey in charge of the peaches.

## V — THE PEACHES · pp 30–34

**Emotional entry:** mischief. **Exit:** Laozi, the man who made every offer,
saying "no more offers," and the sky filling with soldiers.

The peaches ripen once every three thousand years and he eats them by the
armful. Laozi comes to the garden for peaches for his elixir and lets slip
that they are also for the banquet, and Wukong asks which banquet, and Laozi
has to tell him: the Emperor's peach banquet, for immortals of rank, tonight.
He is not on the list. He goes to the hall before the guests arrive and eats
everything and drinks the wine and stumbles, drunk and hurt, into Laozi's
laboratory, where five gourds of golden pills look like candy. He eats them
all. They are five hundred years of Laozi's work, and they make him
unkillable, and they sober him up enough to understand what he has just
done. He runs. In the Hall, Laozi tells the Emperor that his elixir is gone,
and that he has no more offers to make. Send Erlang.

**The turn:** the insult was not being uninvited. It was being managed, and
he finds out on the same page he finds out about the party.
**Page-turn out:** who is Erlang?

## VI — ERLANG · pp 35–38

**Emotional entry:** thrill. **Exit:** a monkey on the ground with a dog on
his leg and a ring around his head, laughing, because it took all of them.

Erlang arrives alone in front of the army with a third eye in his forehead
and a hound at his heel and introduces himself like an equal, which nobody
has done before, and Wukong likes him at once and hits him. They fight shape
for shape: sparrow and hawk, fish and cormorant, snake and crane. Wukong
turns into a temple with his tail as a flagpole behind it, and Erlang laughs:
he has never seen a flagpole behind a temple. Wukong runs. From the clouds,
Laozi drops the iron ring on his head from behind. The hound takes his leg.
Chains.

**The turn:** the first fair fight he ever has, he loses, and not to Erlang.
**Page-turn out:** what does Heaven do with a monkey it cannot kill?

## VII — THE FURNACE AND THE HAND · pp 39–47

**Emotional entry:** dread. **Exit:** a mountain with a monkey's face in it,
snow, and a peach set on the rock by an old gray hand.

In the Hall, Laozi asks for the monkey: forty-nine days in his furnace, and
he will have his elixir back out of the ashes. The Emperor says do it. In the
dark and the heat, Wukong finds the one corner where the wind comes in, and
waits, for the first time in his life. On the forty-ninth day he kicks the lid
off with eyes gone gold and the whole of Heaven breaks around him until the
Emperor is backed against his own throne, and Laozi has nothing left, and the
Emperor says the thing nobody in Heaven says: send for the Buddha. The Buddha
arrives sitting down. He offers a bet: leap out of my hand and the throne is
yours. Wukong somersaults to the five pillars at the edge of the world and
writes his name on the middle one and leaves a second mark, and comes back,
and the Buddha turns his hand over, and there is the writing on his finger.
Wukong runs. The hand comes down and becomes a mountain. Five hundred years.
Snow, and spring, and every year an old gray monkey climbs to the rock with a
peach. He is still there. He is waiting. Somebody is going to come.

**The turn:** the only thing that can stop him is something that never fights
him, and what it teaches him is the one thing he has never done.
**Page-turn out:** (none: the book ends.) The promise is that someone will come.

Spectacle runs above the ~13% target on purpose: the brief promises a
spectacle book, and every spectacle page here is an arrival, a duel, or an
aftermath that the dramatic pages before it have earned. It relaxes no rule;
the target is a balance, not a ceiling. The script gate should still ask of
each spectacle page whether a dramatic page would do more.

**No movement runs more than five dramatic pages without a prose or spectacle
page.** Longest runs: 16–20 (five), 31–35 (five). The prose and spectacle
pages are what buy the dramatic pages their room; they cannot be cut for budget.

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
The wound (p. 24) gets its own page before the banner (p. 25). The choice to go back up to Heaven
is made on its own page (p. 28), not narrated under the mansion. The elixir
(p. 33) is not on the page where he learns about the banquet (p. 31). The
furnace gets a prose page of duration (p. 40) before the havoc (p. 41). The
hand (p. 45) and the mountain (p. 46) are separate pages, and the mountain
gets its aftermath (p. 47).

**One dominant turn per page.** Every row above names the one thing that
changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence comes back here,
not to the prompt.
