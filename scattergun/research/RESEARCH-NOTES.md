## The shell (Wikipedia: Shotgun shell)

- Components in firing order: brass-lined base with **primer** (larger than rifle/pistol primers) → **smokeless powder** → **wad** (gas seal: "prevent the shot and powder from mixing, and provide a seal that stops gas from blowing through the shot rather than propelling it") → plastic **shot cup with crumple-zone cushion** (compresses under acceleration, protects pellets) → **shot pellets** → folded crimp closure.
- Shot cup peels open after exiting the muzzle; pellets then disperse.
- **Birdshot** numbered #2–#10, smaller number = larger pellet. **#8 shot ≈ 410 pellets per ounce.** Typical trap/skeet target load = 1 to 1⅛ oz → **roughly 450–460 pellets in a 1⅛ oz #8 load** (410 × 1.125 ≈ 461).
- **Buckshot**: 00 ("double-aught") = 8–18 large pellets per 12-ga shell (typically 9 in a standard load).
- **Slug**: single solid projectile, deer-sized game, effective ~100 m (rifled-barrel sabot slugs 150+ m).
- Effective ranges: birdshot ~45 m, buckshot ~35 m; small shot loses appreciable energy by ~100 yards.
- Steel-shot 12-ga standard loads in Europe capped at **425 m/s (1,390 ft/s)** muzzle velocity — use "around 400 meters per second / about 1,200–1,400 ft/s" as the typical band.

## Gauge (Wikipedia: Shotgun shell)

- Gauge = how many lead balls of that bore diameter weigh one pound. **12-gauge: a lead ball fitting the bore weighs 1/12 lb.** Lower number = bigger bore.
- Bore diameters: 10-ga 0.775 in (19.7 mm); **12-ga 0.729 in (18.5 mm)**; 20-ga 0.615 in (15.6 mm); **.410 is a caliber, not a gauge** (would be ~67.6-gauge).

## Firing event / internal ballistics (Shotgun Life, RifleShooter, gun-digest internal-ballistics sources)

- SAAMI max average pressure 12-ga: **11,500 psi**; target loads typically ~9,000–11,000 psi. Phrase as "about 700 times the pressure of a car tire" (≈10,500/32 ≈ 330× — CHECK: car tire 32 psi → 10,500/32 ≈ 330. Use "more than 300 times the pressure in a car tire").
- Barrel time: the whole event — primer strike to pellets exiting — takes **about 3 milliseconds** (sources: ~2 ms internal event; 0.003–0.007 s range). Script uses "about three thousandths of a second."
- Pressure has dropped near zero 2–3 ms after primer strike.

## Pattern spread (TheFirearmBlog myth-busting, RifleShooter patterning)

- Old rule of thumb: cylinder bore spreads **~1 inch per yard** of travel. Modern wads pattern tighter: ~8 in at 10 yd, ~15 in at 20 yd. Script phrasing: "roughly an inch wider for every yard it flies — a little less with modern shells."
- Pattern measured as % of pellets inside a **30-inch circle at 40 yards**.

## Choke (Wikipedia: Choke (firearms))

- Choke = tapered constriction at the muzzle end. Constrictions 0.00–1.15 mm (turkey chokes to 2.5 mm).
- Grades & 40-yd pattern %: **Cylinder 40% · Improved Cylinder (0.25 mm) 50% · Modified (0.51 mm) 60% · Full (0.76 mm) 70%** (in 30-in circle).
- **W.W. Greener** credited with the first practical choke, 1870s; his choke-bored guns won London trials 1875/1877/1879.
- Modern screw-in interchangeable choke tubes standard.
- Skeet: ~75 cm pattern at 20 m with minimal constriction; trap uses tighter chokes for farther targets.

## History (Wikipedia: Shotgun)

- **Fowling pieces** for birds, 16th-c Europe; barrels up to 6 ft by early 1600s.
- Word "shotgun" first recorded **1776** in Kentucky ("Frontier Language of the West").
- Double-barreled guns dominated the 19th c.; first American **hammerless** shotgun Daniel LeFever **1878**.
- Smoothbore (no rifling) because the job is dispersing many pellets, not spinning one bullet.
- Browning shotguns: lever **Model 1887**, pump **Model 1893** and **1897**, semi-auto **Auto-5**.
- Clay sports: trap and skeet both Olympic events.

## John Moses Browning (Wikipedia: John Browning; Britannica; Utah Life; Boise Gun Club)

- Born **January 23, 1855, Ogden, Utah Territory**. Father Jonathan, a pioneer gunsmith, opened his Ogden shop **1852**. John worked in the shop **from age 7**.
- Boyhood gun: around **age 10**, with 5-year-old brother **Matt**, built a crude working gun from a broken flintlock barrel, scrap wood, and wire while their father was out (Utah Life / Boise Gun Club accounts; Wikipedia confirms first rifle built as his father's apprentice without an age). SCRIPT USES: "about ten years old."
- First patent **October 7, 1879, at age 24** (single-shot falling-block rifle); he and brother Matthew sold it to Winchester. **128 firearm patents** total.
- Worked with Winchester from 1883.

## Auto-5 (Wikipedia: Browning Auto-5)

- Designed **1898**, patented **1900**. Browning called it his **"best achievement."** First semi-automatic shotgun.
- **Long-recoil action**: (1) barrel and bolt recoil together, locked, the full length of a shell; (2) barrel returns forward while bolt stays back — spent shell ejects out the right side; (3) bolt runs forward, feeding the next shell from the magazine. Friction rings tune it to load strength.
- Winchester (T.G. Bennett) refused Browning's royalty terms → Browning took it to **Fabrique Nationale (FN) in Herstal, Belgium**; production began **1902**.
- Nicknamed the **"Humpback"** for its squared-off high rear receiver. Produced until **1998** (Remington's licensed copy = Model 11, ~850,000 made before 1947).

## Clay target sports (Wikipedia: Trap shooting)

- Origins: late-1700s live-pigeon trap shooting (often passenger pigeons, released from under hats/traps).
- **Glass balls** invented 1860s by Charles Portlock of Boston (Annie Oakley and Doc Carver shot them).
- **Clay pigeons 1880** — credit disputed between **Fred Kimble** (coal-tar targets that break cleanly) and **George Ligowsky** (baked clay). SCRIPT: name both or say "American inventors around 1880." DELIBERATE-DEPARTURE LOG: credit dispute noted, script says "around 1880."
- Modern clay disc: **~4.5 in (11 cm) across**. American trap target ~**40 mph**; **Olympic trap ~62 mph (100 km/h)**. Trap house launches away from shooter, oscillating arc (54° American; Olympic bunker = 15 machines).
- Trap in the **Olympics since 1900**; women's event added 2000. Skeet = crossing targets between two towers; sporting clays = course of varied throws.
