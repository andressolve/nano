- Left label: ONE BULLET. The target keeps moving while the bullet flies. Miss by a finger's width and you have missed completely.

Composition: ivory poster sheet. The cutaway shell painted huge across the page, tilted slightly, every layer visible; the intact red shell standing smaller beside it for scale. Six callouts on thin leader lines. Title strip top.

LETTERING:
- Title strip: INSIDE THE SHELL — A MACHINE IN A TUBE
- Callout 1 (to brass base): BRASS HEAD. The strong metal base that holds everything together and takes the strike.
- Callout 2 (to primer disk): PRIMER. The spark plug. One sharp tap and it spits a jet of flame.
- Callout 3 (to powder layer): POWDER. A pinch of smokeless powder. The primer's flame turns it into hot expanding gas — the push.

- Callout 5 (to pellet cluster): SHOT. About 450 lead pellets, each smaller than a peppercorn. This is the cloud, packed and waiting.
- Callout 6 (to folded top): CRIMP. The top is simply folded shut. The firing pressure unfolds it on the way out.

## PAGE 4 — Three Thousandths of a Second — POSTER (sequence cutaway) — T5 — ref: ref_shell

Composition: ivory poster sheet. A long horizontal cutaway of a shotgun barrel crossing the full page, shown in three painted phases left to right: (1) chamber end — firing pin denting the primer, a small flame jet; (2) mid-barrel — the wad and pellet cluster sliding forward, hot gas shaded behind them; (3) muzzle — pellets emerging, the wad's petals peeling open behind them in the open air. Phase zones subtly tinted. Title strip top, callouts below each phase.

LETTERING:
- Title strip: THREE THOUSANDTHS OF A SECOND
- Callout 1 (under phase 1): THE TAP. The firing pin dents the primer. It spits flame into the powder, and the powder becomes a violent push of hot gas — more than three hundred times the pressure inside a car tire.
- Callout 2 (under phase 2): THE PUSH. The gas cannot get past the wad's seal, so it shoves the whole stack — wad and pellets together — down the barrel. The inside of the barrel is perfectly smooth — engineers call it a SMOOTHBORE. Pellets need no spin; they only need a clean, straight launch.
- Callout 3 (under phase 3): THE LAUNCH. The pellets leave the muzzle at around four hundred meters per second. The plastic wad opens like petals, the air drags it back, and it falls away. The cloud flies on alone.
- Bottom caption: From tap to launch takes about three thousandths of a second — less time than a single beat of a fly's wing.

## PAGE 5 — The Cloud Grows — FIELD (pattern range) — T4 — ref: composite_field

Composition: the pattern range, seen from behind the shooting line at a low angle. The hero shotgun rests open and unloaded on a wooden rest in the left foreground. Down the grass lane, four white paper sheets on wooden frames at increasing distances; the nearest shows a small dense cluster of holes, each farther sheet a wider, thinner spread. Small painted distance posts. Overcast neutral light.

LETTERING:
- Top caption band: The moment the cloud leaves the barrel, it begins to spread. The old rule of thumb: about an inch wider for every yard it flies — a little less with modern shells. Engineers measure it on paper sheets like these, counting how many pellets land inside a big circle forty yards out.
- Bottom caption band: Spreading is the shotgun's gift and its curse. Up close the cloud is small and dense — easy to aim wrong, impossible to miss with. Far away it is wide but thin — a bird-sized gap can open between pellets. Past about forty-five meters, the cloud is too thin and too slow to do its job. So the next question became: can you CONTROL how fast the cloud spreads?

## PAGE 6 — The Squeeze — POSTER — T5 — ref: ref_hero_shotgun

Composition: ivory poster sheet. Center: a large painted cutaway of the last stretch of a shotgun barrel, showing the bore narrowing in a gentle taper at the muzzle; pellets shaded inside bunching closer together. Right side: four small painted muzzle circles labeled with grades, each above a small cone showing a different spread width. Lower left vignette: a Victorian gentleman gunmaker with mutton-chop whiskers examining a barrel in a London workshop. Title strip top.

LETTERING:
- Title strip: THE CHOKE — SHAPING THE CLOUD
- Callout 1 (to tapered bore): The CHOKE is a gentle squeeze at the last few centimeters of the barrel — the bore narrows by less than a millimeter. Like pinching a garden hose, the squeeze tightens the stream: the pellets leave bunched closer and the cloud spreads more slowly.
- Callout 2 (to muzzle circles): Gunmakers grade the squeeze. CYLINDER means no squeeze at all. IMPROVED CYLINDER, a quarter of a millimeter. MODIFIED, half. FULL, three quarters — at forty yards it keeps nearly twice as many pellets on target as no choke at all.
- Callout 3 (to vignette): In the 1870s, a London gunmaker named W.W. Greener turned the choke from a rumor into a science — his choke-bored guns crushed every rival in public shooting trials, year after year.
- Bottom caption: Modern barrels take screw-in choke tubes, so one gun can swap its squeeze in a minute: open for close fast targets, tight for far ones. The cloud became adjustable.

- Title strip: WHAT "12-GAUGE" ACTUALLY MEANS
- Callout 1 (to scale): The old gunmakers' ruler: take a lead ball that fits the barrel exactly. If twelve such balls weigh one pound, the gun is a 12-GAUGE. It is a medieval way of measuring — and the world still uses it.
- Callout 2 (to barrel mouths): So the rule runs backwards: the SMALLER the number, the BIGGER the barrel. A 12-gauge bore is 18.5 millimeters across; a 20-gauge is smaller at 15.6; the little .410 is smaller still — and that one is named in inches, just to confuse everyone.
- Bottom caption: The 12-gauge became the world's standard — big enough to throw a serious cloud, gentle enough for a day of sport. Nearly everything in this book is a 12-gauge.

LETTERING:
- Title strip: THE PUMP — A NEW CLOUD IN HALF A SECOND
- Callout 1 (to slide handle): The sliding wooden handle under the barrel is the engine. Browning patented the design in 1893; his improved model of 1897 ran for half a century.
- Callout 2 (to ejecting hull): PULL BACK: the bolt slides open, hooks the empty hull out of the chamber, and flicks it away — and the spring inside is cocked for the next shot.
- Callout 3 (to rising shell): PUSH FORWARD: a fresh shell pops up from the tube under the barrel, and the bolt shoves it home. Locked, loaded, ready.
- Callout 4 (to magazine tube): The tube holds the queue — shell after shell, nose to tail.
- Bottom caption: One smooth back-and-forth — the famous KA-CHUNK — and the gun is ready again in under a second. The shooter's own arms power the whole machine. Browning's next idea was stranger: what if the gun could do even that part itself?

## PAGE 10 — The Gun That Loads Itself — POSTER — T5 — ref: ref_humpback

Composition: ivory poster sheet. The humpback semi-automatic shotgun painted in full side profile across the page width. Above it, three small painted sequence panels showing the long-recoil cycle as ghosted cutaways: (1) barrel and bolt locked together sliding rearward, (2) barrel forward again while the bolt holds back and an empty red hull flips out the side, (3) bolt running forward pushing a fresh shell in. Callouts on thin leader lines. Title strip top.

LETTERING:
- Title strip: THE AUTO-5 — THE GUN THAT LOADS ITSELF
- Callout 1 (to sequence panel 1): THE TRICK IS THE KICK. Every shot shoves the gun backward — recoil. Browning refused to waste it. In his design, the barrel and bolt ride the kick backward TOGETHER, like two train cars coupled.
- Callout 2 (to sequence panel 2): They uncouple. The barrel springs forward; the bolt stays back; the empty hull flips out through the side.
- Callout 3 (to sequence panel 3): Then the bolt chases forward, lifting the next shell into place. The energy that loaded the gun came from the shot itself. Engineers call it SEMI-AUTOMATIC: one trigger pull, one shot, and the machine reloads itself.
- Callout 4 (to the humped receiver): The squared-off rear earned it a nickname: the HUMPBACK.
- Bottom caption: Browning finished the design in 1898 and called it the best achievement of his life. When America's biggest gun company refused his terms, he sailed to Belgium and had it built there instead, starting in 1902. It stayed in production for almost a century — until 1998.

The target is honest: it cannot be bribed, and it never flies the same way twice.
