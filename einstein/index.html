Test Your Understanding

1. When Albert was five and sick in bed, his father gave him a gift that made him feel something was strange and hidden about the world. What was it?
Exactly. The compass needle always pointed north, no matter how he turned it. Something invisible had to be moving it. Albert later said he never forgot that feeling — it was his first hint that the world is full of hidden forces waiting to be understood.
Not quite. It was a small brass compass. The needle always pointed north — an invisible force moving it. Albert said he never forgot that feeling. It was his first glimpse that the world is stranger and more hidden than it looks.
2. After Albert graduated from college, he couldn't find a university job. What “cobbler's job” did he take instead — the one where he secretly rewrote physics on the side?
Right. He was a Third-Class Patent Examiner in Bern, reviewing electrical switches and clocks eight hours a day. He finished the patents quickly — and the long quiet afternoons belonged to his own papers. A desk job changed physics.
Not quite. He was a patent clerk in Bern, reviewing drawings of electrical switches and clocks. He called it his “cobbler's job.” But the patents were quick, and the long quiet afternoons at his desk became the most productive hours of his life.
3. In 1905, Einstein's “miracle year,” he published four world-changing papers. Which one later won him the Nobel Prize — not relativity?
Yes! His Nobel Prize came not for the famous equation or relativity, but for showing that light travels in tiny packets — grains of energy we now call photons. This discovery started quantum physics, even though Einstein himself never fully trusted it.
Not quite. Einstein won the Nobel Prize for the photoelectric effect — showing that light is not a smooth wave but travels in tiny packets of energy. It was his first 1905 paper, and it started the quantum revolution.
4. Two years after the miracle year, Einstein had what he called “the happiest thought of my life.” What did he realize?
Exactly. Imagine someone falling from a roof — in that moment, they feel weightless. Gravity, for them, has vanished. That tiny thought led Einstein to realize that gravity isn't really a force at all. It's the shape of space and time, bent by heavy things like planets and stars.
Not quite. He imagined a person falling from a roof — and realized that the falling person feels no gravity at all. From that small thought, he worked for eight more years and finally saw that gravity is not a force. It is the curving of space and time around matter.
5. In 1919, a British expedition photographed something in the sky that proved Einstein right and made him world-famous overnight. What did they photograph?
Yes! Einstein had predicted that the sun's mass would bend starlight passing near it — proving that space itself bends around heavy objects. During a solar eclipse on Príncipe Island, Arthur Eddington photographed stars near the blackened sun. The stars were shifted exactly as Einstein said they would be. The next day's headline: “Lights All Askew in the Heavens.”
Not quite. They photographed a total solar eclipse. Einstein had predicted that starlight passing near the sun would bend — because space itself is curved around heavy things. The photographs showed stars shifted exactly as he said. Overnight, he was the most famous scientist in the world.
