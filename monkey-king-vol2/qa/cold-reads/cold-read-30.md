# Cold read — pages 1–30

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands without outside documentation. I understood who the central pair were, why they were traveling together, why they split, why Wukong returned, what the golden band does, how the original horse was replaced, and what new danger awaits at the farm.

## Retell of the story so far

Five hundred years ago, the Monkey King fought Heaven and lost. The divine hand that defeated him became a mountain, trapping him beneath it while his monkeys continued bringing him peaches as tribute.

Guanyin comes to the imprisoned monkey with an offer. A monk named Sanzang is walking from the far east to the far west to fetch holy books, and the road is dangerous. If the Monkey King guards him all the way, the mountain will open. Wukong eagerly agrees, but immediately wants to solve the journey by flying the monk west. Guanyin insists that Sanzang must travel every mile on foot and warns that other guardians have also been asked to wait for him.

Sanzang, a gentle but stubborn monk who was rescued from a river as a baby and raised by monks, has been sent west by the Tang emperor. His two human companions abandon him at the edge of the empire, but he continues alone with his bay horse. When he reaches the mountain, Wukong calls to him from inside. Sanzang climbs to the summit and removes the gold seal. The mountain explodes open, and Wukong emerges in his old armor.

Wukong introduces himself as Sanzang's bodyguard and offers to carry him west in a single leap. Sanzang refuses because the walking itself is the pilgrimage. That night Sanzang sees Wukong's ruined armor and gives him a robe, the first freely given gift Wukong remembers receiving. Wukong is touched, though he tries not to show it. He finds ordinary walking maddeningly slow, but he leaves his mountain and follows.

Robbers stop them on the road. Sanzang is terrified; Wukong is delighted to have his first fight in centuries. He turns the needle he carries into a staff and violently defeats the gang. When he is about to finish their leader, Sanzang stops him. They argue over mercy and survival: Sanzang says Wukong could have frightened the men away and refuses to travel with a robber, while Wukong says a man threatening them with a cleaver deserves what he gets. Hurt and furious after waiting five hundred years only to be rejected, Wukong abandons him.

Wukong visits Ao Guang, the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, an old acquaintance from whom he once stole the iron pillar that became his staff. Wukong complains about the monk. Ao Guang shows him a picture-story about a boy who repeatedly retrieved an old man's shoe from below a bridge; the old man then taught the patient boy, who became a great man. Wukong calls the story terrible but understands its point and returns to Sanzang.

While Wukong is gone, Guanyin visits Sanzang. She admits that she sent Wukong and gives Sanzang a plain cap with a gold band sewn inside, plus three words that will force Wukong to stop if he is about to do something terrible. When Wukong returns, both companions make awkward peace. Sanzang gives him the cap as though it were another present. The gold band fastens permanently around Wukong's head. During another argument about Wukong using violence, Sanzang recites the words, causing the band to contract in terrible pain. Wukong nearly attacks him, and Sanzang repeats the spell until he drops his staff.

Wukong confronts Guanyin about replacing one prison with another. She says he was about to break Sanzang's head and that the band is the only way the monk can stop him. She promises it will come off when they reach the west, but tells Wukong he must remove it himself then. Wukong returns to camp. Sanzang apologizes for using the spell out of fear and for disguising the cap as a gift. Wukong admits that fear is what people usually feel around him. Sanzang promises to try not to fear him and to use the words only when necessary; Wukong dryly predicts that will be often. Their reconciliation is real but uneasy.

Soon a white river dragon bursts from the water and swallows Sanzang's horse, saddle and all. Wukong saves Sanzang, then fights the creature. When it hides in a hole, Sanzang's simple suggestion to lure it out helps: Wukong insults its parents until it attacks. After pinning it, Wukong learns that it too was told by a lady in white to wait for a westbound pilgrim. The dragon is a son of Ao Guang who accidentally burned his father's best pearl and has waited in the stream for years. Realizing it has eaten the very pilgrim's horse it was meant to meet, it apologizes and turns itself into a white horse to carry Sanzang west.

The three continue into autumn. The transformed dragon-horse carries Sanzang faithfully and never speaks again. Sanzang and Wukong now share some concern for it, and Wukong hides his feelings behind a joke about telling Ao Guang what happened the next time he visits for tea.

At nightfall they arrive at Gao's farm. An old man initially refuses them entry because the family is in danger. He explains that a supernaturally strong young man married his youngest daughter, then revealed himself as a pig-snouted monster. The creature eats enormous amounts and locks the daughter in a back room every night. Wukong is pleased to have a demon to catch and announces that he will find the creature that night.

## Cast

- **Sun Wukong / the Monkey King:** A small, wiry humanoid monkey with golden-brown fur, a long tail, bright amber eyes, and an expressive, sharp-featured face. He first appears trapped in rock, then bursts free in battered red-and-green armor. Afterward he wears the patched pale robe Sanzang gives him, a ragged tiger-patterned lower garment, and eventually a plain cloth cap over a permanent gold circlet. His red-and-gold staff can shrink to a needle. He is immensely strong, fast, funny, boastful, emotionally raw, and dangerously quick to violence. His role is Sanzang's reluctant bodyguard and difficult companion.

- **Sanzang:** A young, slender, bald monk in yellow robes with a red checked outer wrap. He carries a ringed religious staff, a woven pack, and initially travels with a brown bay horse. He is gentle, earnest, stubborn, frightened by danger, and committed to walking every mile of the pilgrimage. He is the moral center of the pair, but his use of Guanyin's coercive band also gives him frightening power over Wukong.

- **Guanyin:** A serene woman in flowing white robes who travels on a cloud, carrying a willow branch and a small vase. She recruits both Wukong and the river dragon to protect the pilgrim. She is a divine organizer and authority figure, but also the person who secretly equips Sanzang with the painful gold band.

- **The white dragon / dragon-horse:** A long white aquatic dragon with pale blue mane and scales, green eyes, small gold horns, claws, and a horse-like head. It is Ao Guang's son, punished after accidentally burning a precious pearl. After eating Sanzang's bay horse by mistake, it takes the form of a graceful white horse and becomes Sanzang's mount and another member of the pilgrimage.

- **Ao Guang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea:** A huge blue-green, antlered dragon in ornate robes, living in an underwater palace. He knows Wukong from an earlier theft of his iron pillar. He serves as an old acquaintance and indirect adviser whose deliberately pointed shoe story nudges Wukong back toward Sanzang.

- **Sanzang's bay horse:** A sturdy brown packhorse that accompanies him from the east and is swallowed by the white river dragon. Its loss leads directly to the dragon joining the party in replacement form.

- **The robbers:** A gang of rough human men with clubs and a large cleaver. They trigger the central moral break between Sanzang and Wukong.

- **The Gao farm elder and family:** The elderly farmer who admits the travelers and asks for help. His youngest daughter is being confined by her monstrous husband.

- **The pig demon:** Not yet seen directly. By the elder's account, he first appeared as an exceptionally strong young man, married the youngest daughter, then developed a pig snout, enormous appetite, and abusive behavior. He is the immediate next threat.

- **Minor figures:** A smaller monkey outside the mountain brings Wukong a peach; monks rescue and raise the infant Sanzang; two unnamed human companions turn back before the journey properly begins; the Tang emperor commissions the pilgrimage.

## Causal chain

1. Wukong's defeat by Heaven leaves him imprisoned for five hundred years.
2. Guanyin needs protection for Sanzang's westward pilgrimage, so she offers Wukong freedom in exchange for service.
3. Sanzang removes the mountain's seal, freeing Wukong and creating their initial obligation to one another.
4. Sanzang's insistence on walking turns Wukong's effortless power into a test of patience rather than a shortcut.
5. Sanzang gives Wukong a robe with no price attached, creating the first genuine emotional bond between them.
6. The robbers expose their incompatible views of violence: Wukong attacks to eliminate danger, while Sanzang demands restraint. Their argument makes Wukong leave.
7. Ao Guang's story reminds Wukong that endurance and humility can lead somewhere worthwhile, so he returns.
8. Guanyin anticipates Wukong's dangerous temper and gives Sanzang the cap and stopping spell.
9. Sanzang tricks Wukong into wearing it, then activates it from fear. This prevents an attack but nearly destroys their trust.
10. Wukong's confrontation with Guanyin establishes that the band will remain until the pilgrimage succeeds, tying his freedom directly to reaching the west.
11. Sanzang's honest apology and Wukong's admission about being feared allow them to continue together despite the coercion.
12. The river dragon eats the horse because it has been waiting for the pilgrim without knowing what he looks like.
13. Sanzang's suggestion helps Wukong lure the dragon out; conversation reveals that Guanyin assigned it to the same pilgrimage.
14. The dragon makes restitution by becoming Sanzang's new horse, expanding the traveling party.
15. The need for shelter takes them to Gao's farm, where Wukong learns of the pig demon and volunteers to confront it.

## Backtracking, rereading, guesses, or misidentifications

No page required me to backtrack or reread in order to follow the story.

On page 1, I initially had to distinguish the smaller monkey carrying a peach from the Monkey King whose face is inside the rock. The scale, location, narration, and the next page resolved that immediately at reading pace; I did not mistake the smaller monkey for Wukong.

On page 12, I could not tell solely from the impact image whether Wukong had killed any of the robbers or only knocked them away. Page 13 made the immediate situation clear: at least the leader under Wukong's raised staff was still alive, and Sanzang was stopping a threatened killing. The uncertainty did not impair the conflict or its consequences.

On page 15, I inferred before it was stated outright that Ao Guang's shoe story was meant to teach Wukong patience and send him back to Sanzang. Wukong's departure and return on page 17 confirmed the inference. This felt like intended reader participation, not missing causality.

I did not misidentify any recurring character. Wukong's switch from armor to the monk's robe and later cap was clear because it happened on-page. The white dragon's transformation into the new horse was also explicit.

## Text and attribution at reading pace

All narration, dialogue, and sound effects were readable at normal pace. I did not encounter a line whose speaker was materially ambiguous. Speech-balloon tails, facing, panel staging, and the speakers' distinct voices kept attribution clear, including the crowded exchanges among Wukong, Sanzang, and the river dragon.

The plain prose pages slowed the rhythm slightly compared with the action pages, but the text remained concise and clear. No wording failure blocked or altered my understanding.

## What I expect next

I expect Wukong to confront the pig demon that night and discover that this apparent villain is another complicated prospective companion arranged, directly or indirectly, by Guanyin. The repeated pattern suggests that the demon will first fight Wukong, then prove connected to the westward pilgrimage.

I also expect the central long-term tension to remain the bond between Sanzang and Wukong: Sanzang needs Wukong's violence to survive but fears it, while Wukong wants belonging and recognition but resents being controlled. The gold band will likely be invoked again, and each use will test whether they are becoming true companions or merely prisoner and keeper.
