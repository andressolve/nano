## Retell

Five hundred years ago, the Monkey King fought all of Heaven, lost, and was pinned beneath the mountain made by the hand that defeated him. Guanyin visits him there and offers him a path out: a monk named Sanzang is coming from the east to fetch holy books in the west. If the Monkey King guards him on the journey, Sanzang can remove the gold seal that holds the mountain shut. The Monkey King agrees, although his first instinct is to solve the journey by carrying the monk through the air. Guanyin insists that Sanzang must walk and that the journey must remain his own.

Sanzang was rescued from a river as a baby and raised by monks. Gentle, stubborn, and afraid of nearly everything, he accepts the Tang emperor's mission to bring back the holy books. Two human companions abandon him at the edge of the empire, but he continues with only his horse.

At the mountain, the trapped Monkey King calls to Sanzang and explains the bargain. Sanzang is afraid that he may be a monk-eating demon, but climbs anyway. He prays that the seal should stay if the creature is evil and open if he is fit to accompany him. The seal flies free, the mountain bursts apart, and the Monkey King emerges. He introduces himself as Sun Wukong, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, and accepts Sanzang as his master and charge.

Their temperaments immediately clash. Wukong is fast, impatient, proud, and practical; Sanzang insists on walking every step. At camp Sanzang gives him a plain robe, an act of uncalculated kindness that affects Wukong because he is used to taking everything or receiving tribute, never receiving a gift.

When robbers stop them, Wukong eagerly steps into his first fight as bodyguard. A tiny red needle becomes his full staff, and he sends the gang flying. He is about to kill their leader when Sanzang intervenes. Sanzang believes the robbers could have been frightened away and refuses to travel with a killer; Wukong believes lethal force is simply what keeps them alive. Hurt and angry after waiting five hundred years for Sanzang, Wukong abandons him.

Wukong goes to the underwater palace of Ao Guang, the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, from whom he once stole the iron pillar that became his size-changing staff. He complains that Sanzang called him a robber. Ao Guang points out that Wukong did, in fact, rob him, then tells a story about a boy who repeatedly retrieved an old man's thrown shoe and was rewarded by being taught everything the old man knew. The intended lesson is patience, humility, and returning to one's teacher. Wukong calls it a terrible story but goes back to Sanzang anyway.

While Wukong is gone, Guanyin visits Sanzang, identifies herself as the one who sent Wukong, and gives him a cap with a thin gold band sewn inside. She teaches him three words that will make Wukong stop if he is about to do something terrible.

Wukong returns while pretending the Dragon King's story had nothing to do with it. Sanzang welcomes him and gives him the cap as another apparent present. Wukong happily puts it on and boasts that no one ever gave him his feathered cap in Heaven either; he took that one. Later, Sanzang asks Wukong to promise never to strike a man again except to save him. Wukong refuses, arguing that force, not promises, keeps Sanzang alive. Sanzang says the three words. The hidden band tightens around Wukong's head, causing extreme pain, and Wukong begs him to stop.

## Cast

- **Sun Wukong / the Monkey King / Great Sage Equal to Heaven:** A golden-brown monkey with bright amber eyes, a long tail, and a crest of spiky hair. He first appears trapped in rock, emerges in battered leaf-like armor, and later wears the plain patched robe and cap Sanzang gives him. He carries a red-and-gold staff that can shrink to needle size. He is Sanzang's immensely powerful but volatile new bodyguard: comic, proud, impatient, starved for kindness, and willing to kill threats.
- **Sanzang:** A young, slender, clean-shaven monk with a shaved head, layered ochre robes, a red checked outer robe, sandals, a wicker pack, and a ringed pilgrim's staff. He travels with a brown bay horse. He is gentle, fearful, morally uncompromising, and quietly stubborn. His mission is to walk west, retrieve the holy books, and bring them home.
- **Guanyin:** A serene woman in flowing white robes and veil, with an ornate gold crown, jewelry, a willow branch, and a small white vessel, usually riding a cloud. She arranges Wukong's release and partnership with Sanzang, then secretly gives Sanzang the means to restrain him.
- **Ao Guang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea:** A huge blue-green dragon-man with antler-like coral horns, whiskers, a pearl on his forehead, and elaborate dark robes. He rules an underwater palace. He is an old acquaintance and former victim of Wukong who gives him the lesson he needs to return.
- **The robbers:** A band of rough human men in drab, ragged clothes carrying clubs and a heavy cleaver. They threaten Sanzang for his horse, belongings, and robe. Their attack exposes the central moral conflict between monk and bodyguard.
- **Wukong's monkeys:** Smaller brown monkeys living around the mountain and apparently bringing peaches while he is imprisoned. They are the community he looks back toward after leaving.
- **Sanzang's horse:** A saddled brown bay carrying his supplies and accompanying him west.
- **The emperor, Sanzang's two former companions, Heaven, and the old man and boy in Ao Guang's story:** Mentioned or shown briefly as background to the two leads' histories and choices.

## Causal chain

Wukong's rebellion against Heaven leads to five hundred years of imprisonment. Guanyin needs a protector for the vulnerable pilgrim Sanzang, so she offers Wukong freedom in exchange for service. Sanzang's faith and mercy lead him to remove the seal, freeing Wukong. Their forced partnership exposes opposing ideas of what protection means: Sanzang insists on moral restraint, while Wukong believes overwhelming violence is necessary. Sanzang's kindness makes Wukong care about him, but Sanzang's condemnation after the robber fight wounds Wukong enough to leave. Ao Guang's parable nudges Wukong back. In Wukong's absence, Guanyin gives Sanzang a supernatural restraint. Sanzang disguises it as a gift, then uses it when Wukong refuses his moral limit, turning their disagreement into a direct struggle over freedom, authority, and trust.
