# Cold read — canonical pages 1–40

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands without outside documentation. I could follow the identities, motives, action, and changes in the traveling party on one normal-paced read. I did not encounter a page with material reader harm.

## Retell of the story so far

Five hundred years ago, the Monkey King fought Heaven, lost, and was pinned beneath a mountain formed by the hand that defeated him. His monkeys have kept bringing him one peach every spring. Guanyin comes to tell him that a monk named Sanzang is traveling west to retrieve holy books. If the Monkey King protects him for the entire journey, Sanzang's arrival will give him a chance to be freed. The Monkey King immediately wants to fly Sanzang to the destination, but Guanyin insists that the monk must make the journey himself, on foot and on the ground.

Sanzang is a gentle, frightened monk who was found as a baby in a basket and raised at a monastery. The Emperor of Tang chose him to bring the holy books back from the west. His original two companions abandon him at the edge of the empire, but he continues with his bay horse. At the mountain he meets the imprisoned Monkey King, climbs to its peak despite his fear, and removes a gold seal. The mountain explodes and frees the monkey, who introduces himself as Sun Wukong, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. Sanzang refuses Wukong's offer to carry him west because walking the whole road is the pilgrimage. At camp, Sanzang gives Wukong his spare robe. Wukong, who has always taken what he wanted and has never been given anything, is quietly moved.

When robbers stop them, Wukong enlarges his needle-sized staff and effortlessly defeats the gang. He is about to kill their leader when Sanzang stops him. Sanzang condemns his violence and says he cannot travel with a robber; Wukong, furious that his protection has been rejected, flies away. He visits his old acquaintance Ao Guang, the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea. Ao Guang reminds him that Wukong stole the staff from him, then tells him a story about a student who endured repeated humiliations from an old teacher and was rewarded with instruction. Wukong hates the story but understands the point and returns to Sanzang.

While Wukong is gone, Guanyin gives Sanzang a plain cap with a hidden gold band and teaches him three words that can force Wukong to stop if he is about to do something terrible. When Wukong returns, Sanzang presents the cap as another gift. Wukong happily puts it on, only to discover that the band has fused to his head. During an argument, Sanzang speaks the words and causes him excruciating pain. Wukong realizes he was trapped, nearly attacks Sanzang, and is stopped by the words again. After learning Guanyin devised the band, he confronts her. She admits it was the only way a single monk could restrain him and tells him he can remove it himself when they reach the west. Wukong returns. Sanzang apologizes sincerely for using both the cap and the incantation out of fear rather than necessity. They reach an uneasy but real understanding: Sanzang will try not to fear him and will use the words only when he must, while Wukong warns that this may still be often.

At a gorge, a white dragon erupts from the water and swallows Sanzang's horse, tack and all. Wukong fights it, taunts it out of hiding, and discovers it was sent by the same lady in white to wait for a westbound pilgrim. The dragon is a son of Ao Guang who was confined to the stream after accidentally setting fire to his father's best pearl. Realizing it ate the pilgrim's horse, the dragon apologizes and transforms into a white horse to carry Sanzang, as Guanyin intended. Sanzang thanks Wukong for returning to him. Wukong hides his emotion when Sanzang asks if the dragon's father knows where his son is.

The travelers next arrive at the Gao family farm. Old Gao explains that a tremendously strong young man married his youngest daughter, then revealed a pig's face, ate prodigiously, and locked the daughter away. Wukong frees the young woman and disguises himself as her to confront the husband. The pig-man boasts that he once served as a marshal in Heaven before being expelled and transformed. Wukong reveals himself and chases him into the mountains. During their fight, the pig-man complains that he worked the farm's fields and ate what he grew. He then learns Wukong is escorting the westbound monk and admits that Guanyin told him to wait for that same pilgrim, but he became distracted by marriage.

The pig-man begs to become Sanzang's disciple, promising to carry luggage, guard the camp, and barely eat. Wukong objects because he imprisoned the Gao daughter, but Sanzang offers him the same chance he offered Wukong. The pig-man apologizes to the young woman for locking her door because he feared she would flee when she saw his true face. Sanzang accepts him and names him Bajie, for the eight rules he must follow. Bajie joins reluctantly enough to keep asking whether he can return if the arrangement fails. On the road he complains constantly, overeats, and needles Wukong by calling him "big brother." Wukong unexpectedly finds himself acting as the responsible older member of the group.

The four travelers reach the Flowing Sand River, said to be eight hundred miles wide and so unnatural that not even a feather floats on it. There is no boat or bridge. Sanzang asks Wukong to fly him over just once, but Wukong says he cannot: this is Sanzang's road, and if he is carried over it, he has not crossed it. Bajie points out that the white horse used to be a dragon and proposes riding it across, while Wukong observes that the horse itself will still have to put a hoof into the deadly water. The party is left facing its first apparently impassable obstacle.

## Cast

- **Sun Wukong / the Monkey King / Great Sage Equal to Heaven:** A compact, golden-brown monkey with orange-gold eyes, a long tail, ragged pale robe, striped fur skirt, red-and-gold size-changing staff, and—after Sanzang's gift—a thin gold band around his head. He is a former rebel against Heaven, immensely powerful, impatient, funny, vain, violent, and unused to kindness. He is now Sanzang's bodyguard and increasingly the party's exasperated big brother.
- **Sanzang:** A young, bald human monk in yellow robes and a red check-patterned outer robe, carrying a ringed pilgrim's staff and woven pack. He is the Tang emperor's chosen pilgrim, traveling west for the holy books. He is fearful but persistent, morally opposed to killing, capable of deception under pressure, and capable of recognizing and apologizing for his wrongs.
- **Guanyin / the lady in white:** A serene woman in flowing white robes and veil, elaborate crown and jewelry, holding a willow branch and small white vessel and traveling on a cloud. She has arranged the pilgrimage's supernatural protectors. She offers freedom and purpose, but also imposes the painful gold band that allows Sanzang to control Wukong.
- **Bajie / the pig-man:** A huge, broad-bellied man with a pig's snout, tusks, enormous ears, black ragged clothes, and a nine-tined rake. Formerly a marshal in Heaven, he became pig-faced after being thrown out. He is a prodigious eater and capable farmworker who married and confined Old Gao's daughter, then joins the pilgrimage as Sanzang's second disciple. He is lazy, self-serving, comic, and at least somewhat ashamed of what he did.
- **The white dragon / white horse:** A long white-and-pale-blue, green-eyed dragon with horns and a flowing mane, later a handsome white horse with a blue-white mane. He is Ao Guang's son, punished after burning his father's pearl. Sent to await the pilgrim, he mistakenly eats Sanzang's original horse and becomes the replacement mount.
- **Ao Guang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea:** A massive teal sea dragon dressed as a regal host amid an underwater court. He is Wukong's old acquaintance and the father of the white dragon. He uses an irritating moral story to push Wukong back toward Sanzang.
- **Old Gao:** An elderly, thin farmer with gray hair and beard. He asks Wukong to save his youngest daughter from her pig-faced husband and is alarmed when Bajie suggests he might return.
- **Old Gao's youngest daughter:** A young human woman with dark hair and pale green clothing. Bajie locked her in the back house. She cooperates with Wukong's plan, receives Bajie's apology, and watches him leave with a subdued, conflicted expression.
- **Sanzang's original horse:** A sturdy brown bay horse carrying the monk's baggage. The white dragon swallows it along with its tack.
- **The robbers:** A road gang of roughly dressed human men armed with clubs and a cleaver. Their attack produces the first fundamental conflict between Wukong's lethal idea of protection and Sanzang's refusal to kill.
- **The mountain monkeys:** Smaller brown monkeys who maintain their king's old tribute by bringing him a peach each spring during his five-hundred-year imprisonment.
- **The Emperor of Tang and Sanzang's first two companions:** Seen or described only as part of Sanzang's background. The emperor commissions the pilgrimage; the companions abandon it at the edge of the empire.

## Causal chain

1. Wukong's defeat by Heaven leaves him trapped for five hundred years.
2. Guanyin needs a protector for Sanzang and offers Wukong freedom in exchange for guarding him on the whole westward journey.
3. Sanzang removes the seal, freeing Wukong; his insistence on walking establishes that the road itself, not merely the destination, matters.
4. Sanzang's robe is the first freely given object Wukong remembers receiving, beginning an emotional bond.
5. The robbers expose the pair's incompatible ethics. Sanzang rejects Wukong's lethal protection, so Wukong abandons him.
6. Ao Guang's story shames Wukong into returning.
7. Knowing Wukong will return but cannot be physically controlled by Sanzang, Guanyin supplies the gold band and incantation.
8. Sanzang uses the incantation from fear, provoking Wukong's sense of betrayal. Their later confrontation and apology define the dangerous compromise under which they continue together.
9. The white dragon eats the bay horse, then recognizes the pilgrim he was meant to serve and becomes Sanzang's new mount.
10. At Gao farm, Wukong's intervention uncovers another powerful being assigned by Guanyin to await the pilgrim.
11. Sanzang extends to the pig-man the same conditional mercy he extended to Wukong, creating Bajie and enlarging the party.
12. The new party reaches an uncrossable river. Sanzang's duty to cross his own road rules out Wukong's easy shortcut, and the water's danger rules out the apparent dragon-horse solution.

## Backtracking, rereading, guesses, and misidentifications

None required. I did not need to return to an earlier page or reread a line to understand the action or relationships.

On page 24, I initially understood the emerging white creature as a dragon even though Wukong jokingly calls it an eel; the following pages confirm that identification, so this caused no confusion. On page 31, the transition from the freed daughter to Wukong wearing her appearance was immediately legible from his plan, the transformation smoke, and the visible monkey traits in the disguise. I did not mistake disguised Wukong for the daughter.

## Text and attribution at reading pace

No text failed. All narration and dialogue were readable at normal pace, and every speech balloon could be attributed from its tail, placement, voice, or the immediate exchange. The shifts among spoken dialogue, narrated prose, and inset story imagery were clear.

## What I expect next

I expect the Flowing Sand River to contain another dangerous supernatural figure connected to Guanyin's recruitment plan, probably the final companion implied when she told Wukong that others were also waiting for Sanzang. The party will have to earn passage rather than bypass the river: Wukong and Bajie will likely fight or provoke its inhabitant, while Sanzang's insistence on making the journey himself will remain the condition that prevents an easy magical crossing. If that figure joins them, the group will gain someone whose abilities make the river traversable and the pilgrimage will continue west with a fuller, even more quarrelsome party.
