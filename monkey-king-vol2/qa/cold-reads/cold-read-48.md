# Cold read — canonical pages 1–48

## Verdict

APPROVED

The story lands without documentation. The identities, changing party membership, emotional turns, and reasons for each new companion joining were all clear at normal reading pace.

## Retell of the story so far

Five hundred years ago, the Monkey King fought Heaven, lost, and was trapped beneath the mountain made by the hand that defeated him. Guanyin comes to him there and offers freedom: a monk named Sanzang is walking west to bring back holy books, and if the Monkey King guards him, the mountain will open. The Monkey King eagerly agrees, though he immediately tries to bargain for a faster, easier journey.

Sanzang, an orphan raised by monks and sent west by the Tang emperor, reaches the mountain alone after his original companions abandon him. He climbs to the gold seal and peels it off. The mountain bursts apart and frees the Monkey King, who introduces himself as Sun Wukong and declares himself Sanzang's bodyguard. Sanzang insists that pilgrimage means walking the whole road rather than flying over it. That night he gives Wukong a spare robe—the first gift Wukong can remember receiving.

When robbers attack, Wukong turns a sewing needle back into his magic staff and beats them brutally. Sanzang stops him from killing the last robber and rebukes him for treating a human life as disposable. Hurt and angry, Wukong leaves. He visits Ao Guang, the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, who reminds him of a story about a boy who kept returning to fetch an old man's discarded shoe and was eventually rewarded with great teaching. Wukong understands the lesson and returns to Sanzang.

While Wukong is away, Guanyin gives Sanzang a plain cap with a hidden gold band and teaches him three words that will make Wukong stop if he becomes dangerous. Sanzang presents the cap as a gift. Later, when Wukong threatens him during an argument about violence, Sanzang says the words. The band tightens painfully around Wukong's head. Wukong is furious at the deception and confronts Guanyin, who admits arranging it because only Sanzang can restrain him. She promises the band will come off when they reach the west. Back at camp, Sanzang apologizes both for using the spell out of fear and for disguising the cap as a gift. They reach an uneasy but honest understanding: Sanzang will try not to fear Wukong and will use the words only when necessary; Wukong warns that necessity may come often.

At a gorge, a white dragon erupts from the water and swallows Sanzang's horse. Wukong fights it, discovers it was also sent by Guanyin to wait for a westbound pilgrim, and learns that it is Ao Guang's son. The dragon apologizes and transforms into a white horse to replace the one it ate. The new horse carries Sanzang onward. Its grief and shame remain visible, especially when it pauses over streams, and Wukong quietly avoids letting Sanzang see that the dragon's story affects him.

The travellers reach Gao's farm, whose owner says a powerful pig-faced monster married his youngest daughter and now locks her away each night. Wukong disguises himself as the daughter. The monster boasts that he was once a marshal in Heaven before being cast out and given his pig form; the disguised Wukong reveals that the Monkey King is coming. When the monster says the Monkey King is trapped beneath a mountain, Wukong answers, "Not anymore," and attacks. During their fight, Wukong learns that the pig too was told by Guanyin to wait for the pilgrim but forgot his duty after getting married. The pig submits and offers himself to Sanzang. Though Wukong objects that the pig imprisoned a girl, Sanzang accepts him just as he accepted Wukong and names him Bajie, for the eight rules he must follow. The daughter declines to call him back, so he joins the pilgrimage. Bajie is lazy, hungry, talkative, and comic, and he calls Wukong "Big brother."

The enlarged party reaches the Flowing Sand River, eight hundred miles across, where not even a feather floats. Wukong refuses to fly Sanzang over because the road must be crossed by the pilgrim himself. A fearsome blue-gray, red-haired river fighter attacks Bajie with a crescent-bladed staff. Bajie cannot drag him onto land, and the creature repeatedly dives away. The group reasons that he may be another guardian sent to await the pilgrim. When they call out, he emerges and reveals that he has already eaten nine monks, whose skulls alone would not sink, and believed Sanzang to be the tenth. Wukong asks Sanzang to control the attacker with the gold-band spell, but Sanzang instead accepts the monster after hearing his worst truth, names him Wujing, and promises not to fear him. Wujing removes the nine skulls and, as Guanyin foretold, they become a raft that carries Sanzang and the entire party safely across before vanishing.

That night Sanzang asks why Wukong returned after the robbers. Wukong finally explains the Dragon King's shoe story: if a boy could go back for a shoe, Wukong could go back for a monk. In the morning, the white horse carries Sanzang, Wujing and Bajie share the luggage, and Wukong runs far ahead. He could cross the remaining distance in one leap, but looks back at his slow monk climbing the road and chooses to wait.

## Cast

- **Sanzang:** A young, slim, clean-shaven monk with a shaved head, ochre robes, a red patterned outer robe, sandals, a wicker travelling pack, and a tall ringed pilgrim's staff. He is the Tang emperor's westbound pilgrim and the party's moral center. He is openly fearful but keeps walking, rejects shortcuts, sees the person inside each monster, and gradually becomes brave enough to offer trust where violence would be easier.

- **Sun Wukong / the Monkey King / Great Sage Equal to Heaven:** A small, athletic golden-brown monkey with bright amber eyes and a long tail. After his release he wears a patched pale robe and tiger-patterned lower garment; from page 18 onward he also wears the plain cloth cap with its visible gold band. He carries a red-and-gold staff that can shrink to a needle. He is Sanzang's immensely powerful, impatient, funny, boastful bodyguard. His central struggle is learning restraint, trust, and the difference between guarding someone and controlling their road.

- **The white dragon / white horse:** First a long white serpentine dragon with pale blue mane, green eyes, and gold horns; afterward an elegant white horse with an icy blue mane and tail. He is Ao Guang's son and was sent to wait for the pilgrim. After eating Sanzang's original horse, he becomes its remorseful replacement and carries Sanzang west.

- **Bajie / Pig:** A huge, round-bellied pig-man with pink-gray skin, tusks, floppy ears, black hair, a ragged black robe, blue sash, and a large nine-toothed rake. Formerly a marshal in Heaven, he was expelled and transformed after drunken misconduct. He marries Gao's daughter, imprisons her from fear of rejection, then leaves to join Sanzang. He carries baggage and supplies comic appetite, complaint, vanity, and a less lethal kind of unruliness for Wukong to manage.

- **Wujing:** A towering, muscular blue-gray river being with long wild red hair and beard, red eyes, ragged brown clothing, a crescent-bladed staff, and initially a necklace of nine human skulls. He has eaten nine earlier pilgrims while waiting unknowingly for the tenth. Sanzang accepts and names him; he becomes the quiet, formidable fourth disciple and carries one end of the luggage pole.

- **Guanyin / the lady in white:** A serene woman in flowing white robes and veil, with an ornate crown, jewelry, willow branch, small vase, and cloud. She engineers the assembly of the pilgrimage party: she promises Wukong freedom, directs the dragon, Bajie, and Wujing to wait for Sanzang, and supplies Sanzang with the coercive gold band and its three-word spell.

- **Ao Guang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea:** A massive teal-green dragon sovereign with antlers, whiskers, jeweled robes, and an undersea crystal-and-coral court. Wukong's old acquaintance and the father of the white dragon, he gives Wukong the story that persuades him to return to Sanzang.

- **Gao and his daughter:** Gao is an elderly, thin farmer with a gray beard. His unnamed youngest daughter is a slight young woman with dark pinned hair and pale green clothing. Their household introduces Bajie: Gao wants the pig-man driven away, while the daughter admits his imprisonment was wrong but also recognizes that he feared her rejection. Her silent decision not to call him back confirms his departure.

- **The robbers:** A roadside gang in rough dark clothes armed with clubs and a large cleaver. They trigger the first serious moral rupture between Sanzang and Wukong.

- **Sanzang's original horse:** A sturdy brown packhorse that accompanies him to the mountain and is swallowed by the white dragon, prompting the dragon's transformation and service.

## Causal chain

1. Wukong's defeat leaves him imprisoned for five hundred years.
2. Guanyin offers freedom in exchange for guarding Sanzang, giving Wukong a reason to accept service.
3. Sanzang removes the mountain's seal, so Wukong is freed and joins him.
4. Sanzang's insistence on walking establishes that Wukong cannot simply bypass the pilgrimage's hardships.
5. Sanzang's gift of a robe gives Wukong his first experience of generosity without tribute or transaction.
6. Wukong's brutality toward the robbers collides with Sanzang's refusal to kill, causing Wukong to abandon him.
7. Ao Guang's shoe story makes Wukong reconsider pride and return.
8. Guanyin's hidden gold band gives the physically vulnerable Sanzang a way to stop Wukong, but Sanzang's deceptive presentation and fearful use of it create a breach of trust.
9. Their mutual admissions and limited promises repair that breach enough for the journey to continue.
10. The white dragon eats the horse, but recognition of Guanyin's instructions turns a fight into recruitment; remorse makes the dragon take the horse's place.
11. Trouble at Gao's farm lets Wukong expose Bajie and discover that he too was assigned to the pilgrimage.
12. Sanzang accepts Bajie on the same principle by which he accepted Wukong, adding a third disciple and testing Wukong's tolerance.
13. The impossible Flowing Sand River forces the party to engage its resident monster rather than evade the road.
14. Recognition that Wujing was also waiting for the pilgrim turns combat into confession.
15. Sanzang accepts Wujing despite his nine killings; Wujing's skull necklace then becomes the only means of crossing the river.
16. Wukong finally tells Sanzang why he returned, completing the emotional meaning of Ao Guang's story.
17. Wukong's decision to wait on page 48 shows that the once-impatient immortal now chooses companionship over speed.

## Backtracking, rereading, guesses, and misidentifications

None. I did not need to backtrack or reread any page to follow the action, identities, transformations, party membership, or emotional logic. I made no character misidentifications.

The disguises and transformations were readable on first encounter: page 31 clearly framed the young woman as Wukong's disguise before page 34 revealed him; pages 27–29 clearly established that the water creature became the new white horse. Wujing's move from attacker to intended disciple on pages 43–45 was also explicit before the crossing.

## Text and attribution at reading pace

No text failed, and no balloon required a guess about its speaker. The alternating speakers remained clear even in dense exchanges such as pages 19–22, 25–28, 33–37, and 42–45. The narration pages were comfortably legible at the supplied proof size. Sound effects were immediate and did not compete with story text.

## What I expect next

The four companions and the dragon-horse will continue west through a chain of dangers that cannot simply be flown over or beaten into submission. Each obstacle will likely test a different weakness inside the party: Wukong's violence and impatience, Sanzang's fear and moral rigidity, Bajie's appetite and unreliability, Wujing's shame and capacity for loyalty, and the horse's remorse. I expect Wukong to keep becoming a leader and older-brother figure despite resisting that role, while the gold band remains a source of tension until Sanzang can trust Wukong without needing to use it. The repeated pattern of apparent monsters becoming companions also makes me expect future encounters to ask first who someone is and why they are waiting, not merely whether Wukong can defeat them.
