# Sequence gate — canonical pages 41–48

## Blind sequence read

At the Flowing Sand River, a blue-gray, red-bearded monster erupts from the sand-coloured water and swings for Sanzang; Bajie intercepts the blow while Wukong closes in. Bajie fights the monster under the opaque sandy water, but cannot force him onto the bank because he dives whenever he gets close. Sanzang realizes that something so powerful is hiding because it is frightened.

Bajie then connects the monster to the lady in white who separately told him, Wukong, and the horse to wait for the pilgrim. He calls out that the pilgrim is present, and the monster rises at once. The monster asks whether Sanzang is the tenth pilgrim and confesses that he ate the previous nine monks, whose unsinkable skulls he now wears. He explains that the lady in white told him to await the tenth, and that he attacked before he could identify whom he heard above the sand. Wukong leaves the monster's fate to Sanzang.

Sanzang sees that the monster volunteered the worst truth about himself and accepts him knowingly. He names him Wujing, places him at his own back, and promises not to fear him. Wujing removes the nine skulls and reveals the lady's last promise: what he carries will one day carry the pilgrim. The skulls become a raft, and the newly formed company crosses the enormous river together.

That night, safely on the far bank, Sanzang asks why Wukong returned after leaving him on the day of the robbers. Wukong admits that the dragon's image of a boy repeatedly retrieving an old man's shoe taught him that he, too, could go back for his monk; he immediately hides the tenderness behind a joke. In the morning the four companions and the white horse travel west as one company. Wukong races ahead, looks back at the slow monk he chose to return to, and waits.

## Comparison and judgment

The rendered sequence matches the script's material story and causal order. All scripted text is present in order and attributed to the intended speakers. Wukong, Sanzang, Bajie, Wujing, and the white horse remain unmistakable across page turns; the hostile ogre's transition into the named companion Wujing is clear.

The Flowing Sand River remains consistently sand-coloured through the battle, confession, and crossing. Wujing's consequential skull cord persists through the confrontation, is removed during his acceptance, and becomes the single raft that carries the entire company across. The bank-to-river-to-far-bank geography is legible, and the night camp and following morning establish the elapsed time cleanly.

Each page keeps one focal turn and earns the next: attack, stalemate, recognition, confession, acceptance, crossing, reconciliation, then departure. The run does not read as compressed summary. Its concentration of dramatic pages is broken by spectacle on pages 41 and 46 and by illustrated prose on page 48, while the dramatic stretch itself changes register from combat to deduction to moral choice. Wujing's confession and acceptance receive the crossing as consequence; Wukong's confession receives the final image of him waiting as aftermath. No consequential beat is abandoned.
