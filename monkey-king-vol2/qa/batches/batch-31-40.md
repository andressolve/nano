# Sequence gate — pages 31–40

## Blind sequence read

Wukong frees Old Gao's daughter, sends her back to her father, and takes her place as the waiting bride. Sanzang worries that he will hurt the husband; Wukong promises only to scare him, with a comic qualification. The pig-spirit arrives affectionately, tries to charm the supposed bride, and defends his appearance by explaining his fall from a heavenly marshalship. The disguised Wukong reveals that the Monkey King has been hired to catch him, then reveals himself. The terrified pig bursts through the roof and flees.

Wukong chases him to his cave. Their argument establishes both the pig's work for Old Gao and his ruinous appetite. When Wukong mentions the westbound pilgrim, the pig remembers that a lady in white had ordered him to wait for that monk; he forgot the charge and married instead. At dawn he submits to Sanzang and volunteers to serve. Wukong objects because he imprisoned the daughter, but Sanzang answers by recalling the mercy he extended to Wukong. The pig apologizes directly to the daughter. Sanzang accepts him, names him Bajie for the eight rules he must keep, and the daughter answers his attempted farewell by shutting the gate. Bajie leaves with Sanzang and Wukong, chastened but still comic.

On the road, Bajie's complaining, appetite, and teasing establish the new group's daily rhythm, while Wukong unexpectedly grows into the responsible elder-brother role. The travelers then reach the immense Flowing Sand River, whose tablet declares that no feather floats. With no boat or bridge, Sanzang asks Wukong to fly him across. Wukong refuses because carrying him past the obstacle would mean Sanzang had not traveled his own road. Sanzang accepts that he must cross by his own effort but admits he cannot swim; Bajie proposes the former dragon-horse, which visibly refuses the water.

## Script comparison

The visible story matches the script's material sequence. All scripted text is present with the intended speakers and attribution. Wukong, Sanzang, Old Gao, the daughter, the pig/Bajie, and the dragon-horse remain legible across transitions, including the pig's named-identity change after Sanzang accepts him. The pale-green bride disguise, gold headband, rake, luggage, patched robe, white horse, farm gate, westward road, and Flowing Sand River carry their consequential functions across page turns without confusion.

The focal line remains coherent: Wukong's trap leads to the pig's recognition of his forgotten charge; recognition leads to submission, accountability, naming, and departure; the travel bridge establishes the changed group dynamic before the next obstacle tests the pilgrimage's governing principle. Page turns consistently deliver their setups, especially the pig's entrance, Wukong's reveal, the chase, Bajie's recognition, the daughter's answer, the river reveal, and Wukong's refusal.

Pacing is sound. Pages 31–37 alternate intimate comedy, spectacle, action, reckoning, and emotional aftermath rather than reading as one undifferentiated dramatic block. Page 38 compresses only routine travel and uses that compression to establish relationships; it does not summarize away a consequential event. Pages 39–40 then slow for a landscape reveal and a focused moral obstacle. The daughter's response and Bajie's departure provide the needed aftermath to his apology and acceptance.

No material story, text, attribution, identity, continuity, focal, pacing, aftermath, or page-turn failure justifies a complete-page redraw.
