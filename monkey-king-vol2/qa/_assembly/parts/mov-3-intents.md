## PAGE 11

A dramatic page on the border road among boulders. Six rough men jump out at the monk: one seizes the bay horse's bridle, two tug at his bundle and his robe, and none of them says a word. Sanzang, terrified, begs that he is only a monk with nothing to steal. The dominant turn is "let me": Wukong steps out in front of his master grinning, hands on hips, delighted that this is his first fight as a bodyguard, and sends the monk behind the horse. The page ends close on two fingers holding up a tiny needle at the leader's chopper, so the reader turns to see what the needle becomes. The robbers stay a silent threat; Wukong's appetite for the fight owns the page.

## PAGE 12

A spectacle page, one image, on the border road among boulders. The needle has become the red-and-gold iron staff, swung in one enormous arc: robbers fly off in every direction, cudgels spin into the sky, and the leader lies flat on his back in the dust with his chopper gone. The dominant turn is the staff raised high in both of Wukong's hands over a man who cannot get up. The only word is the sound of the blow. Small behind the horse, the monk's mouth hangs open, silent. The reader turns because the staff is about to come down, and the joy of the fight has tipped into something the monk cannot bear to watch.

## PAGE 13

A dramatic page on the border road. Sanzang throws himself across the fallen robber under the raised staff, and Wukong shouts at him to move. The dominant turn is the quarrel: the monkey lowers the staff, furious, while the robbers run; the monk, kneeling and shaking, tells him he was going to break a man's head, hears that the road is eat or be eaten, and answers that he cannot walk with a robber. Close on Wukong's face, the hurt shows under the anger: he waited five hundred years for this man. Then the road is empty, a gold streak shoots into the sky, and the monk stands alone by the horse. The reader turns because the monk is now alone on a road that eats monks.

## PAGE 14

A dramatic page on the floor of the Eastern Sea, a world of azurite and crystal. Wukong plunges into Ao Guang's palace, scattering shrimp and crab guards, and hails the Dragon King, who freezes on his throne: not you again, the thief of his iron pillar. The dominant turn is the horrified host: the vast dragon-lord pouring tea from a tiny pot with great care, his eyes straying for one wistful moment to a small empty coral seat beside him, while Wukong sprawls with his feet up and says he quit his monk this morning because the monk called him a robber. Ao Guang says he is one. Wukong grins that robbing Ao Guang was different. The reader turns because the one he robbed is the only one he came to tell.

## PAGE 15

A dramatic page in Ao Guang's crystal palace. The Dragon King points to an ink painting on the screen behind his throne, and Wukong asks why an old man is throwing his shoe at a boy. The dominant turn is the painting itself, large and calm: an old man on a stone bridge, a shoe falling, a boy reaching up, and the story told over it, three times dropped and three times fetched, until the old man taught the boy everything. Ao Guang, hopeful, suggests going back is admirable, and far away. Wukong, on his feet with his tea slopping, calls it a terrible story and says he is going back to his monk. The painting owns the page; the old dragon's sly hope stays the joke underneath.

## PAGE 16

An illustrated-prose page on the border road, Wukong absent. The monk sits on a stone by the bay horse, his face wet, not knowing which way to go. Guanyin comes down low on her white cloud, names herself, and puts a plain cloth cap into his hands, a thin band of gold glinting inside it, and teaches him three words to say if the monkey is ever about to do a terrible thing. The dominant turn is the gift passing from her hands into his: a trap the monk now holds. The prose carries what the picture cannot, ending on her answer to what the words will do: make him stop. The reader turns because the monkey is coming back, and the monk has the cap.

## PAGE 17

A dramatic page on the border road by a plain white milestone. Wukong lands in a puff of dust and tells the monk not to make a speech; Sanzang, startled on his stone, hides the cap behind his back and says he was not going to. The dominant turn is the two of them face to face, Wukong with arms folded and not quite looking at him, explaining his return only as a dragon and a picture of a shoe he will not talk about, and the monk saying simply that he is glad. Then they walk west again, Wukong a step ahead, boasting he hit nobody the whole time and had tea. The reader turns because he came back on his own, and the monk is still hiding the cap.
