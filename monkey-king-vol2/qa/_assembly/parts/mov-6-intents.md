## PAGE 30

Dramatic page. At dusk the travellers reach a village where every door is barred, and Old Gao, peering through the crack of his gate with a lantern, tries to send them away until Wukong says he catches demons. Inside, in the lamplit front room over a feast of noodles nobody is eating, Old Gao owns the page: the strong young ploughman who married his youngest daughter turned into a pig with a rake, eats everything, and has locked her in the back house. The one dominant turn is the old man's story of the pig son-in-law, told in the front room. The monk is present and quiet; the pig is only told about, never seen. Wukong's last line turns complaint into a plan, and the reader turns because the pig is coming tonight and Wukong means to be waiting for him.

## PAGE 31

Dramatic page. Wukong snaps the lock off the back house and the daughter runs out past him into her father's arms. Inside, Wukong is caught halfway through changing, half monkey and half the young woman, while the worried monk in the doorway asks him not to hurt the pig; Wukong answers that he is going to marry him. Then the bride sits primly on the curtained bed, hands folded, perfect except for the thin gold band at her hairline. The one dominant turn is the girl let out and Wukong becoming the bride, carried by the half-changed figure facing the monk. The real daughter stays small and silent; the old man is a background figure. The joke is the gold band. The reader turns because the bride is now waiting in the dark for a pig.

## PAGE 32

Spectacle page, one image. The back house is indigo dark. A gust flings the window open, and filling it, lit only by the lantern in his own hand, the pig squeezes in: huge, floppy-eared, a rake on his shoulder and a red bridegroom's sash across his belly, calling to his darling. On the bed below, the bride sits with her back to him, still and prim. The one dominant turn is the dark back house and the pig arriving with his lantern. The pig owns the page by size and light; the bride is small, turned away, and silent, and her stillness is the joke the reader is in on. Nothing else in the room competes. The reader turns because the pig is in the room, and his bride is not his bride.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic page. The pig sits on the sagging bed courting the bride, who keeps her back to him and tells him her father says he is ugly. Wounded, hand on his chest, the pig explains he was a marshal in Heaven once, thrown out for being drunk at a party. Then the bride turns, sly, and tells him her father has hired the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. The pig freezes, ears straight up: the Monkey King is under a mountain. The bride says, not anymore. The one dominant turn is the courtship ending at the name, owned by the bride's sly turn; the pig's comedy builds toward it and stays subordinate. The reader turns because the pig is terrified of a name, and the name is sitting next to him.

## PAGE 34

Spectacle page, one image. The bride's head turns fully round, and above the pale green jacket is the grinning monkey face, gold eyes and the gold band, saying hello. The pig shrieks and bursts straight up through the roof in a storm of broken tiles, ears flying, stars above the hole. The one dominant turn is the bride's face becoming the monkey's and the pig going through the roof; both halves are one moment, the reveal first and the flight as its consequence. The monkey face owns the eye; the pig's explosion is the punchline. The room stays indigo dark, now with starlight pouring in. The reader turns because the pig is through the roof, and the monkey is going after him.

## PAGE 35

Dramatic page. Under the moon the pig flees with his rake, a broken roof tile in his hair, protesting that he worked hard; Wukong, behind him on a cloud, swings the staff and says he ate everything too, and staff and rake clash. At the crooked stone door of his cave the pig turns at bay and asks who sent him; Wukong says Old Gao asked, but he is here for a monk going west. Then the pig drops the rake, ears lifting, face lighting up in wonder: the pilgrim. A lady in white told him to wait, and he forgot and got married. The one dominant turn is the pig's wonder at the pilgrim. The fight is busy and stays small. The reader turns because the pig was waiting for the monk too, and the monk is back at the farm.

## PAGE 36

Dramatic page. At dawn in Old Gao's courtyard the pig kneels before the monk, forehead in the dust, begging to be taken on. Wukong steps between them, jabbing a finger: this pig locked a girl in a shed. The monk answers calmly that he took Wukong too, and gave him a robe. The one dominant turn is the pig kneeling and Wukong's objection met by the monk's reply, in that exchange. Then Wukong goes quiet, touching the mended patch on his robe's shoulder, and covers it with a joke. Last, the pig turns on his knees to the daughter at the gate and apologizes for locking the door, afraid she would run from his real face. Old Gao and the daughter are silent witnesses. The reader turns because he has said sorry, and she has not answered yet.

## PAGE 37

Dramatic page. At the farmhouse gate the monk asks the kneeling pig whether he will behave, hears that he will try very hard, and names him Bajie, eight rules. On his feet with the luggage on a carrying pole, Bajie haggles for two rules and asks his father-in-law to keep his bed made; Old Gao says please don't. Then the daughter looks at Bajie for a long moment and swings the gate shut on him herself. Bajie's ears droop; Wukong grins beside him, and the monk on the white horse waits beyond. The one dominant turn is the name Bajie, the bed made, and her answer: the gate shutting, owned by her silent face. The reader turns because there are three of them now and a horse, and the road goes on.
