# Page intents

Reader-facing goals, not image-generation specifications. Shown to both
builder and critic. Exact text and staging stay in the script and the prompt.

## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The volume opens on the Mountain of Five Elements in spring: five bare ridges like the fingers of a huge hand pressed into the earth, moss over their slopes, a small gold paper seal on the highest ridge. At the base, in a cleft of rock, the Monkey King's face, matted with moss, gold eyes with their thin red ring wide open. A small brown young monkey climbs away down the slope, looking back; under the face's chin sits one fresh peach. Far across a strip of sea, small and green, his home. One caption tells the reader why he is there. The dominant turn: five hundred years under a mountain, a face still awake, and a young monkey's peach. The reader turns because his eyes are open. The far mountain and the sea stay small; the face, the peach and the seal carry the page.

## PAGE 2

Dramatic. At the mountain, a white cloud comes down out of a blue sky with a tall woman in white on it, and the monkey's face in the rock rolls its eyes up at her, a peach stone in his teeth, and tells her to get in line. Close and level with his face, calm on her cloud, she names herself, Guanyin, and says she has come to ask him for something; he scowls that she is asking a rock. Then she bends a little closer and tells him about a monk walking west for the holy books, on a road that eats monks, and the price: guard him the whole way, and the mountain opens. His eyes go narrow and interested. The dominant turn: Guanyin arrives with the offer. The reader turns because the way out has a price and he has not heard all of it. The sky and ridges stay subordinate to the two faces.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. At the mountain, Wukong's face pushes out of the rock as far as it can go: yes, he will do it, after five hundred years of peaches from his monkeys. Close on both, he grins and promises to put the monk in the west by lunchtime with one somersault; Guanyin, still, tells him the monk must peel off the seal himself, and then gives the rule: the monk goes on the ground, every mile, because flying him there would make it Wukong's journey, not his. Wukong's face falls. Rising away on her cloud, she adds that he will not guard the monk alone; others are waiting too. The dominant turn: his yes meets the rule of the road. The reader turns because a monk is coming and somebody else is waiting for him. The landscape stays subordinate to the two faces and the falling grin.

## PAGE 4

Illustrated prose. The border road at dawn: a dirt road running west into dry mountains, a lone white milestone beside it, and a young shaven-headed monk in a saffron robe and red kasaya, ringed staff in hand, leading a plain bay horse west. Far behind him, small, two figures walk back east. The prose tells who he is: a baby in a basket on a river, raised by monks and named Sanzang, gentle and stubborn, who said he would walk to the far west for the holy books, and whose two companions turned back at the edge of the empire. He is frightened of almost everything, and he keeps walking. The dominant turn: the monk who is coming is alone. The reader turns because he is afraid and still walking. The two turning figures stay small and far; the road and the lone monk own the page.

## PAGE 5

A dramatic page. The lone monk, leading his bay horse past the foot of five bare ridges, is startled out of his skin by a voice from a crack in the rock: the monkey, buried to the face, has been waiting for him. The page belongs to the meeting of two faces, the frightened young monk bending to peer in and the moss-matted monkey grinning out of the stone. Its one dominant turn is the voice and the face, and the request: climb up and peel off the seal. The monk asks the sensible questions, including whether this is a demon who eats monks; the monkey has no reassurance to give except "climb anyway". What stays subordinate: the horse, the far sea, the tiny gold seal at the very top, which must be seen as high. The reader turns because this terrified man is going to climb.

## PAGE 6

A dramatic page. The climb, told in three steps: the monk stuck halfway up a ridge with the horse tiny below and the monkey shouting directions from the bottom; the monk alone at the windy summit, on his knees, eyes shut, his hand flat on the small gold seal, praying that it stay if the monkey is a demon and lift if he is not; and the seal peeling away in his fingers and lifting on the wind while the voice from below tells him to run. The page belongs to the monk's fear and his choice; its one dominant turn is the climb, the fear, and his hand on the seal. The monkey is only a voice from far below and is not seen. The reader turns because the seal is off and the mountain is about to let go.

## PAGE 7

A spectacle page, near-silent. The five ridges burst apart with a roar, rock and dust thrown into the sky, and out of the middle, riding the blast, the small gold monkey in the rags of his old armor, arms flung wide, tail out, laughing at the sky. Far down the slope, tiny, the monk lies flat on his back beside his horse, looking up; the gold seal flutters away high on the wind. The one dominant turn is the mountain cracking, the monkey rising, and the monk falling. The page belongs to the monkey's freedom; the monk is small and subordinate but must be there, because he did this. One sound and nothing else. The reader turns because the monkey is out and the monk is on his back looking at him.

## PAGE 8

A dramatic page. The monkey lands in the dust in front of the sprawled monk, who begs not to be eaten; the monkey is offended: he is the bodyguard. Then the quiet heart of the page: the Monkey King kneeling low in the dust before the astonished monk, naming himself with all his grand titles and thanking him for climbing, and the monk giving his own plain name and his errand. Last, the monkey springs up to look west and offers to somersault the monk there by supper; the monk refuses, because every mile must be his own, on foot, and the monkey grumbles that the lady said so too. The dominant turn is the kneeling, "Master", and "every step". The bow is the page's largest image. The reader turns because the fastest creature in the world has to walk.

## PAGE 9

A dramatic page at a small campfire by the road at dusk. The monkey's old armor is falling off him in pieces, and the monk gives him his spare hemp robe. The page belongs to the sewing: the monk on one side stitching a tear at the robe's shoulder while the monkey stands in it with his arms stiffly out, not knowing what to do with his hands, asking what he owes, and discovering that nobody has ever given him anything; everything he ever had, he took. The monk tells him to hold still. The one dominant turn is the spare robe mended on him, and "nobody ever gave me anything". Later, alone by the fire with the monk asleep, the monkey touches the mended patch with two fingers and says it is a good robe. The reader turns because somebody gave him something, and in the morning they must walk.

## PAGE 10

An illustrated-prose page. One wide image of a long road through empty hills under a huge sky: near the bottom, the small monk leading his bay horse; far ahead on a hilltop, the tiny monkey in his robe shading his eyes, and a gold streak racing back down the road toward the monk. The prose tells the first days: the monkey running ahead and back until he has walked the day's road ten times, the question of whether it is always this slow, one look back east toward his monkeys' mountain before he turns west, and the first night in five hundred years lying on his back looking at the stars. The one dominant turn is the first days, running ahead and back, and how slow a man is. The road owns the page. The reader turns because the road is slow and empty, until it isn't.

## PAGE 11

A dramatic page on the border road among boulders. Six rough men jump out at the monk: one seizes the bay horse's bridle, two tug at his bundle and his robe, and none of them says a word. Sanzang, terrified, begs that he is only a monk with nothing to steal. The dominant turn is "let me": Wukong steps out in front of his master grinning, hands on hips, delighted that this is his first fight as a bodyguard, and sends the monk behind the horse. The page ends close on two fingers holding up a tiny needle at the leader's chopper, so the reader turns to see what the needle becomes. The robbers stay a silent threat; Wukong's appetite for the fight owns the page.

## PAGE 12

A spectacle page, one image, on the border road among boulders. The needle has become the red-and-gold iron staff, swung in one enormous arc: robbers fly off in every direction, cudgels spin into the sky, and the leader lies flat on his back in the dust with his chopper gone. The dominant turn is the staff raised high in both of Wukong's hands over a man who cannot get up. The only word is the sound of the blow. Small behind the horse, the monk's mouth hangs open, silent. The reader turns because the staff is about to come down, and the joy of the fight has tipped into something the monk cannot bear to watch.

## PAGE 13

A dramatic page on the border road. Sanzang throws himself across the fallen robber under the raised staff, and Wukong shouts at him to move. The dominant turn is the quarrel: the monkey lowers the staff, furious, while the robbers run; the monk, kneeling and shaking, tells him he was going to break a man's head, hears that the road is eat or be eaten, and answers that he cannot walk with a robber. Close on Wukong's face, the hurt shows under the anger: he waited five hundred years for this man. Then the road is empty, a gold streak shoots into the sky, and the monk stands alone by the horse. The reader turns because the monk is now alone on a road that eats monks.

## PAGE 14

A dramatic page on the floor of the Eastern Sea, a world of azurite and crystal. Wukong plunges into Ao Guang's palace, scattering shrimp and crab guards, and hails the Dragon King, who freezes on his throne: not you again, the thief of his iron pillar. The dominant turn is the horrified host: the vast dragon-lord pouring tea from a tiny pot with great care, his eyes straying for one wistful moment to a small empty coral seat beside him, while Wukong sprawls with his feet up and says he quit his monk this morning because the monk called him a robber. Ao Guang says he is one. Wukong grins that robbing Ao Guang was different. The reader turns because the one he robbed is the only one he came to tell.

## PAGE 15

A dramatic page in Ao Guang's crystal palace. The Dragon King points to an ink painting on the screen behind his throne, and Wukong asks why an old man is throwing his shoe at a boy. The dominant turn is the painting itself, large and calm: an old man on a stone bridge, a shoe falling, a boy reaching up, and the story told over it, three times dropped and three times fetched, until the old man taught the boy everything. Ao Guang, hopeful, suggests going back is admirable, and far away. Wukong, on his feet with his tea slopping, calls it a terrible story and says he is going back to his monk. The painting owns the page; the old dragon's sly hope stays the joke underneath.

## PAGE 16

An illustrated-prose page on the border road, Wukong absent. The monk sits on a stone by the bay horse, his face wet, not knowing which way to go. Guanyin comes down low on her white cloud, names herself, and puts a plain cloth cap into his hands, a thin band of gold glinting inside it, and teaches him three words to say if the monkey is ever about to do a terrible thing. The dominant turn is the gift passing from her hands into his: a trap the monk now holds. The prose carries what the picture cannot, ending on her answer to what the words will do: make him stop. The reader turns because the monkey is coming back, and the monk has the cap.

## PAGE 17

A dramatic page on the border road by a plain white milestone. Wukong lands in a puff of dust and tells the monk not to make a speech; Sanzang, startled on his stone, hides the cap behind his back and says he was not going to. The dominant turn is the two of them face to face, Wukong with arms folded and not quite looking at him, explaining his return only as a dragon and a picture of a shoe he will not talk about, and the monk saying simply that he is glad. Then they walk west again, Wukong a step ahead, boasting he hit nobody the whole time and had tea. The reader turns because he came back on his own, and the monk is still hiding the cap.

## PAGE 18

A dramatic page on the border road, at a resting place. The monk holds out a plain cloth cap and calls it something to go with the robe, and Wukong, who has never been given anything he did not take, is delighted: he pulls it on with both hands and poses for the monk. The dominant turn is the cap: a gift, and he puts it on. The page belongs to Wukong's happiness and to the monk's face, which does not smile; the reader sees the thin gold glint inside the brim and knows more than the monkey does. He strides ahead promising to be good all week, and the reader turns because he has put it on gladly and nobody has told him what it is. The road and the resting place stay plain and subordinate; the cap and the two faces carry everything.

## PAGE 19

A dramatic page at a camp by the road at night. By the fire the monk asks Wukong to promise never to strike a man again, even to save him, and Wukong laughs at the idea, pulls the needle from behind his ear, and swings the grown staff to show off, so close that its end sweeps past the monk's nose. The dominant turn is the request, the needle, and the words: frightened, eyes shut, palms pressed hard together, the monk says the three words, and behind him the monkey's hand flies to his head. The page belongs to the monk's fear in the last panel, which is the cause of everything; the staff is the provocation, not the subject. The reader turns because the monk spoke out of fright and something is closing on the monkey's head. The fire and the camp stay simple.

## PAGE 20

A spectacle page, one image, at the night camp. Wukong rolls in the dust clutching his head with both hands, his staff flung aside, the cloth cap tearing apart and flying off in pieces, and around his brow a thin gold band glows. At the edge of the firelight the monk stands with both hands over his mouth, horrified at what his words have done. The dominant turn is the band tightening, the cap torn away, and the band staying. The one shout belongs to the monkey; the monk is silent. The page is about the reveal of the band on the brow and the pain it carries, not about the fire or the road. The reader turns because the cap is gone and the band is still on him.

## PAGE 21

A dramatic page at the night camp. Wukong, on his knees, pulls at the gold band and it will not move; then rage: he springs up with the staff raised over the monk, who stands his ground, terrified, palms together, and says the words again. The dominant turn is that rage and the staff over the monk, answered by three words. Then Wukong is down again, the staff dropped, crying that he has stopped; the monk, shaking, is sorry; and Wukong asks the question that turns the page: who taught you those words? The lady in white. The page belongs to the two of them in the moment the staff is over the monk; the exchange afterwards must be easy to follow line by line. The reader turns because the monkey knows whose band it is and is going to find her.

## PAGE 22

A dramatic page at the South Sea, above Guanyin's small rocky island with its still bamboo grove. Wukong lands on a rock and shouts up at Guanyin on her cloud that she tricked him, let him out from under one mountain and put another on his head, and she says yes. The dominant turn is the confrontation: she explains calmly why the monk needed a way to stop him, he insists he went back on his own, and she agrees, and still the band stays; it comes off only at the end of the road. The page belongs to the contrast between his fury, jabbing at his brow, and her stillness. He turns away, and she sends him back to the monk, who is frightened and alone. The reader turns because the band stays and the monk is alone in the dark. The sea and bamboo stay calm and subordinate.

## PAGE 23

An illustrated-prose page at the night camp, one image. The fire burns low; the monk sits on the left with his knees drawn up and his face wet, and Wukong has just landed on the right, the gold band catching the firelight. Both are awake. The prose tells what happens: the monk apologizes, for saying the words out of fear and for calling the cap a present when he knew what it was, and nobody in Heaven has ever said sorry to Wukong. The dominant turn is the fire and that apology. The page belongs to the space between the two of them across the fire, and to Wukong's not knowing where to look; it ends with him sitting down on the other side of the fire. The reader turns because they are still together and the road goes on. The camp, the horse, and the night stay quiet.

## PAGE 24

Spectacle. Eagle Grief Stream: a deep green mountain stream in a gorge of gray cliffs. Out of the water, in a column of white foam, bursts a long slim white dragon, and its jaws close over the monk's plain bay horse, saddle and all. On the bank Wukong leaps back with the monk tucked safe under one arm; on the water floats the empty bridle. The only word is the sound of the swallow. The dominant turn: the white dragon swallows the horse. The dragon and the swallow own the page; Wukong and the monk are the startled witnesses, and the reader turns because the horse is gone and the dragon is still in the water.

## PAGE 25

Dramatic. On the bank of the stream the monk points in dismay at the empty bridle drifting away, and Wukong, already furious, has his staff in hand: nobody eats his master's horse. Then the great image: underwater, in green and white, Wukong swinging the staff at the coiling white dragon, bubbles everywhere, yelling at it to come out and fight. Last, Wukong bursts out of the water spitting, the dragon's tail vanishing into a dark hole under the bank; the monk calls him to leave it be, and Wukong will not: it ate the horse. The dominant turn: Wukong is furious, he dives, and the dragon hides. The underwater fight owns the page; the monk stays the worried voice on the bank.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. Wukong cannot get at the dragon in its hole, so he stands on a rock at the water's edge, hands cupped round his mouth, and insults its family, while the monk behind him covers his ears. It works: the white dragon erupts out of the stream in fury, jaws wide, roaring that nobody talks about its father, and Wukong leaps up to meet it with the staff. Last, on the bank, the dragon's neck is pinned flat under the staff while its long body thrashes in the water, and Wukong says now they talk. The dominant turn: the shouting draws the dragon out, and Wukong pins it. The eruption owns the page; the monk stays small and silent.

## PAGE 27

Dramatic. Close on the pinned dragon's young face, its sea-green eyes wide: it is not just an eel, a lady in white told it to wait here for a pilgrim going west. Wukong asks whether that means a monk going west, and the dragon says yes. Then the great image: Wukong points back up the bank at the monk, who gives a small, weak wave, and the dragon's eyes follow his finger to the man whose horse it ate. Oh no. Last, the dragon miserable with its chin in the water, explaining it was hungry and nobody mentioned a horse; Wukong, unimpressed, has waited five hundred years under a rock. The dominant turn: the monster was waiting for the monk, and ate his horse first. The dragon's dawning horror owns the page.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. Wukong lifts the staff away and the dragon's head rises, ashamed: its father, the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, put it in the stream for setting fire to his best pearl, and Wukong had tea with him last week. Wukong, arms folded, asks what it will do about the horse; the dragon looks up the bank and offers itself to the monk as his horse instead. Then the great image: one creature turning in a spiral of white foam, the long white coils folding into a slim white horse in the shallows, the same pale-blue mane and sea-green eyes, the old saddle on its back. The monk reaches out to its nose and thanks it; Wukong scowls at the injustice; and the monk thanks Wukong too, for coming back to him. The dominant turn: the dragon names its father and becomes the horse. The transformation and the monk's thanks own the page.

## PAGE 29

Illustrated prose. Autumn hills above the gorge, red trees along the road: the monk riding the slim white horse, Wukong walking beside them with the staff on his shoulder, and the horse looking back over its shoulder at a stream far below. The prose tells the summer turning to autumn, the horse that never speaks again and stops at every stream to look down into the water, the monk's question about its father, and Wukong walking ahead all afternoon so the monk will not see his face. The dominant turn: three travelers now, a white horse, the hills, and autumn. The quiet ride owns the page; the stream far below stays small, a memory.

## PAGE 30

Dramatic page. At dusk the travellers reach a village where every door is barred, and Old Gao, peering through the crack of his gate with a lantern, tries to send them away until Wukong says he catches demons. Inside, in the lamplit front room over a feast of noodles nobody is eating, Old Gao owns the page: the strong young ploughman who married his youngest daughter turned into a pig with a rake, eats everything, and has locked her in the back house. The one dominant turn is the old man's story of the pig son-in-law, told in the front room. The monk is present and quiet; the pig is only told about, never seen. Wukong's last line turns complaint into a plan, and the reader turns because the pig is coming tonight and Wukong means to be waiting for him.

## PAGE 31

Dramatic page. Wukong snaps the lock off the back house and the daughter runs out past him into her father's arms. Inside, Wukong is caught halfway through changing, half monkey and half the young woman, while the worried monk in the doorway asks him not to hurt the pig; Wukong answers that he is going to marry him. Then the bride sits primly on the curtained bed, hands folded, perfect except for the thin gold band at her hairline. The one dominant turn is the girl let out and Wukong becoming the bride, carried by the half-changed figure facing the monk. The real daughter stays small and silent; the old man is a background figure. The joke is the gold band. The reader turns because the bride is now waiting in the dark for a pig.

## PAGE 32

Spectacle page, one image. The back house is indigo dark. A gust flings the window open, and filling it, lit only by the lantern in his own hand, the pig squeezes in: huge, floppy-eared, a rake on his shoulder and a red bridegroom's sash across his belly, calling to his darling. On the bed below, the bride sits with her back to him, still and prim. The one dominant turn is the dark back house and the pig arriving with his lantern. The pig owns the page by size and light; the bride is small, turned away, and silent, and her stillness is the joke the reader is in on. Nothing else in the room competes. The reader turns because the pig is in the room, and his bride is not his bride.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic page. The pig sits on the sagging bed courting the bride, who keeps her back to him and tells him her father says he is ugly. Wounded, hand on his chest, the pig explains he was a marshal in Heaven once, thrown out for being drunk at a party. Then the bride turns, sly, and tells him her father has hired the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. The pig freezes, ears straight up: the Monkey King is under a mountain. The bride says, not anymore. The one dominant turn is the courtship ending at the name, owned by the bride's sly turn; the pig's comedy builds toward it and stays subordinate. The reader turns because the pig is terrified of a name, and the name is sitting next to him.

## PAGE 34

Spectacle page, one image. The bride's head turns fully round, and above the pale green jacket is the grinning monkey face, gold eyes and the gold band, saying hello. The pig shrieks and bursts straight up through the roof in a storm of broken tiles, ears flying, stars above the hole. The one dominant turn is the bride's face becoming the monkey's and the pig going through the roof; both halves are one moment, the reveal first and the flight as its consequence. The monkey face owns the eye; the pig's explosion is the punchline. The room stays indigo dark, now with starlight pouring in. The reader turns because the pig is through the roof, and the monkey is going after him.

## PAGE 35

Dramatic page. Under the moon the pig flees with his rake, a broken roof tile in his hair, protesting that he worked hard; Wukong, behind him on a cloud, swings the staff and says he ate everything too, and staff and rake clash. At the crooked stone door of his cave the pig turns at bay and asks who sent him; Wukong says Old Gao asked, but he is here for a monk going west. Then the pig drops the rake, ears lifting, face lighting up in wonder: the pilgrim. A lady in white told him to wait, and he forgot and got married. The one dominant turn is the pig's wonder at the pilgrim. The fight is busy and stays small. The reader turns because the pig was waiting for the monk too, and the monk is back at the farm.

## PAGE 36

Dramatic page. At dawn in Old Gao's courtyard the pig kneels before the monk, forehead in the dust, begging to be taken on. Wukong steps between them, jabbing a finger: this pig locked a girl in a shed. The monk answers calmly that he took Wukong too, and gave him a robe. The one dominant turn is the pig kneeling and Wukong's objection met by the monk's reply, in that exchange. Then Wukong goes quiet, touching the mended patch on his robe's shoulder, and covers it with a joke. Last, the pig turns on his knees to the daughter at the gate and apologizes for locking the door, afraid she would run from his real face. Old Gao and the daughter are silent witnesses. The reader turns because he has said sorry, and she has not answered yet.

## PAGE 37

Dramatic page. At the farmhouse gate the monk asks the kneeling pig whether he will behave, hears that he will try very hard, and names him Bajie, eight rules. On his feet with the luggage on a carrying pole, Bajie haggles for two rules and asks his father-in-law to keep his bed made; Old Gao says please don't. Then the daughter looks at Bajie for a long moment and swings the gate shut on him herself. Bajie's ears droop; Wukong grins beside him, and the monk on the white horse waits beyond. The one dominant turn is the name Bajie, the bed made, and her answer: the gate shutting, owned by her silent face. The reader turns because there are three of them now and a horse, and the road goes on.

## PAGE 38

Illustrated prose. Weeks of road go by in one long image: the monk on the white horse, Bajie trudging behind under the luggage pole with his mouth open, complaining, and Wukong walking backwards in front of the pig, pointing at him and telling him to hurry. The dominant turn is that Wukong, once the worst-behaved creature in the universe, has become the big brother who bosses somebody else, and he does not mind it as much as he should. The prose carries the comedy and the warmth; the picture carries the three of them as a travelling family. The reader turns because the road is about to end at a river. What stays subordinate: the plain, the hills, the luggage itself.

## PAGE 39

Spectacle. The travellers reach the Flowing Sand River: water the colour of sand, flat and heavy, so wide its far side is lost in haze, no boats and no waves, and a tall stone tablet on the bank carved with its name and its warning. The dominant turn is the size of the obstacle and the stone's sentence: eight hundred miles, and nothing floats. The monk, the pig, and the monkey are tiny beside the tablet, staring; the river owns the page. The reader turns because there is no bridge and no boat, and the monk cannot fly. What stays subordinate: the reeds and the figures' faces, which need not be read.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic. The monk asks Wukong to fly him across on his cloud, just this once, and Wukong, not joking for once, refuses: he could fly a mountain over, but the road is the monk's, and a road you are carried over is not crossed. The dominant turn is that refusal, close on Wukong's face with the monk listening above him on the white horse. Then the monk recognises his own rule, and frightened, admits he cannot swim; the horse will not set a hoof in the sand-water. Bajie supplies the bookends: no noodle stall, and a hopeful idea about the horse. The reader turns because the river is wide and something under it has been listening.

## PAGE 41

Spectacle. The flat sand-water explodes upward and a gaunt blue-gray ogre with a wild red beard and a necklace of nine pale skulls swings a crescent-bladed spade down at the monk on his horse. Bajie throws himself in the way and blocks the blade with his rake. The dominant turn is the pig, of all of them, being the one who gets there first. Wukong is on the far side, his staff only now coming out. The one sound is the clang of blade on rake. The reader turns because the monster wants the monk and the pig is in the way. What stays subordinate: spray and sand, which must not hide the block.

## PAGE 42

Dramatic. The fight goes under. On the bank Wukong hops with his staff, unable to see into sand, while the monk reminds him he fought the dragon underwater; Wukong's answer is that this is soup. The dominant turn is under the surface, in murky gold: Bajie, better in water than anyone guessed, fighting the ogre rake against spade, the nine skulls swinging on their cord. Then Bajie lies flopped and exhausted on the bank: the monster will not come up. The monk sees the real clue, that something that big only hides when it is frightened. The reader turns because they are stuck and the pig is thinking.

## PAGE 43

Dramatic. Bajie, scratching under one big ear, works it out: the lady in white told him to wait for the pilgrim, and told Wukong too, so perhaps she told the river monster the same. The dominant turn is Bajie on his feet at the river's edge, hands cupped, bellowing to the water that the pilgrim is here, while Wukong and the monk cover their ears. It works. Out of the flat water rises a red-bearded head, eyes on the monk, asking one quiet question. The reader turns because the monster has come up and it is looking at the monk. What stays subordinate: the river's surface, which stays flat and still until the head breaks it.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. The ogre, standing in the shallows, asks the monk if he is the tenth one, and then tells him the worst thing: nine monks came to this river going west, and he ate them; their skulls would not sink, so he wears them. The dominant turn is that confession, close on the ogre with his hand closed over one skull and the monk's face beyond his shoulder. Then the lady in white's instruction, and the honest answer to why he swung: he hears feet, not faces, and he thought the Monkey King had come for him. Last, Wukong, low and tight on his staff, gives the monk the choice. The reader turns because the monk has to answer.

## PAGE 45

Dramatic. The monk climbs down off the white horse and walks toward the ogre alone, frightened, one hand out, and says what he noticed: the ogre told the worst thing first, and nobody made him. The dominant turn is the ogre on his knees in the shallows, head bowed, and the monk standing over him, palms together, taking him in and naming him Wujing, at his back every day, unafraid. Then Wujing, the cord of nine skulls off his neck, lays the first skull on the sand-water and it glows pale gold, while the monk bows his head for the nine. The reader turns because the skulls are off and the river is still eight hundred miles wide.

## PAGE 46

Spectacle. Sunset over the sand-coloured river, and the nine skulls laid in a ring on the water have become a broad raft of pale light. On it the whole company crosses: the monk on the white horse, Wujing poling with his spade, Bajie clutching the luggage, Wukong at the front with a hand shading his eyes. The dominant turn is the crossing itself, the impossible made possible by what the ogre carried, the monk on the water every mile. One caption tells the reader the raft carried them all the way and vanished on the far bank. The reader turns because they are across and it is night. What stays subordinate: the far shore, a thin line.

## PAGE 47

Dramatic. Night on the far bank: willows, a fire, Bajie snoring on the luggage, Wujing sitting up on watch, the white horse grazing. The monk finally asks Wukong why, on the day of the robbers, he came back. The dominant turn is close on Wukong in the firelight, the gold band glinting, looking into the fire and telling the truth plainly: the painting of the old man's shoe and the boy who kept fetching it, and if a boy can go back for a shoe, he can go back for a monk. Then the monk smiles, and Wukong scowls and makes a joke so he will not have to be thanked. The reader turns because it is morning and the road west is in front of them.

## PAGE 48

Illustrated prose. Morning, and the long road west into blue mountains. Coming up it, small, the monk on the white horse, and Wujing and Bajie carrying the luggage pole between them, one at each end. Far ahead, on a hilltop, Wukong in his hemp robe with the gold band on his brow, one hand shading his eyes, looking back at them. The dominant turn is that look back: he could be there in one somersault, and instead he waits for the slowest of them all. The prose carries the last thought and ends on the single short sentence that closes the book. There is no next page. What stays subordinate: the mountains and the road, which only need to be long.
