## Cold read

I read pages 1 to 48 in order, starting at "# MOVEMENT I", before opening anything else.

**What happens, as a first-time reader would tell it.** The Monkey King has been pinned under a mountain for five hundred years. His monkeys swim the sea every spring to bring him a peach. Guanyin, a lady in white on a cloud, offers him a way out. He must guard a monk walking west, on foot, every mile, because "if you fly him there, it's your journey, not his." She has asked others to wait for the monk too.

The monk, Sanzang, is a foundling and frightened of nearly everything. His companions turned back at the border. He climbs the mountain anyway and peels off the seal. Wukong bursts out and kneels to him. That night Sanzang gives him his spare robe and mends it on him: "Nobody ever gave me anything."

Robbers attack. Wukong is about to kill their leader when Sanzang throws himself across the man and calls Wukong "no better than he is." Wukong leaves, hurt. At the bottom of the sea he takes tea with Ao Guang, the Dragon King he once robbed. Ao Guang is desperate to get rid of him and tells him a story about a boy who fetched an old man's shoe three times. Wukong hates the story and goes back to the monk.

Meanwhile Guanyin gives the weeping Sanzang a cap with a gold band inside, and three words. Sanzang gives Wukong the cap as a present. When Wukong swings the staff past his nose, Sanzang says the words out of fear. The band tightens. Wukong confronts Guanyin, who answers "Yes." Wukong returns, and Sanzang apologises, which no one in Heaven ever did.

Three recruits follow, all told by the lady to wait:
- **The dragon** eats the horse, turns out to be Ao Guang's exiled son, and becomes the new horse.
- **The pig** is courting a bride he locked in a back house. Wukong takes the bride's place, the pig bolts through the roof, then remembers he was supposed to wait. He apologises to the girl, she shuts the gate on him, and he is named Bajie.
- **The ogre** lives in an 800-mile river of sand and wears the skulls of nine monks he ate. Sanzang asks to be flown across and Wukong refuses, using the monk's own rule against him. Bajie fights in the river and then thinks of shouting "PILGRIM." The ogre confesses the worst thing first. Sanzang takes him in, and the skulls become a raft.

By the fire Wukong finally explains the shoe. In the morning he runs ahead, looks west, and waits for them.

**Where I stopped or reread:**
1. **p43, panel 1.** "So what if she told the river monster?" I read it first as Bajie talking back ("so what?") to "Don't look so pleased, Pig." Only when he starts bellowing in panel 2 did I re-parse it as "what if." This is Finding 1.
2. **p42, panel 1.** "I can't even see my own staff in it!" The panel never shows Wukong putting the staff into the river; he is "hopping… unable to reach." Small hitch (note A).
3. **p35.** "Heaven? Old Gao?" This is the first time anyone says the farmer's name aloud. The context carries it (note B).
4. **p10.** "the stars, which he had not seen for five hundred years." His face was open to the sky on pp 1–2, and he looked up at Guanyin. A careful reader blinks (note C).
5. **p46.** For a moment I asked why a magic raft is allowed when the cloud was not. The book's answer (the monk earned the crossing by taking in the monster) came quickly, but the wording could make it automatic (note D).

## Is it alive?

**Yes.** This is a good book, and I wanted to turn the pages. The quarrel is the plot, as the volume mandate asks. The monk is frightened, stubborn, wrong once (the band) and brave twice (the robber, the ogre). The monkey is loud and hurt in every movement.

- **I (pp 1–4).** Alive from the first balloon: "get in line. Everyone in Heaven already had a turn." Wukong wants out and haggles. Guanyin wants a guardian and sets the price. The "grandchildren" joke tells a cold reader how long five hundred years is without a lecture. The prose page on the monk ends on "He kept walking," which is exactly the right note.
- **II (pp 5–10).** "I looked down." The prayer on the summit is the monk's first act of courage, shown rather than told. The robe scene on p9 is the emotional seed of the whole book. "Kings get paid" against "Nothing. It's a robe" is real subtext: a creature who has only ever taken things, being given one.
- **III (pp 11–17).** The strongest heat in the book. On p13 each man wants something the other cannot give: Wukong wants credit for saving him, Sanzang wants him not to be a killer. "I waited under a rock for five hundred years for you" is pure hurt. Ao Guang is a wonderful, frightened host. He tells the shoe story to get the monkey out of his house, so the most useful lesson in the book comes from fear, which is excellent want-from. "That was different. That was you" and "Don't tell anyone you were right" both land. On p17, "Don't make a speech" / "I wasn't going to" is two people both refusing to say what they feel.
- **IV (pp 18–23).** The cap scene is a real moral trap. The reader knows what the present is, Wukong is delighted, and "It suits you" is a lie in four words. Guanyin's "Yes." is the best line in the volume. On p23 the apology reverses who is afraid of whom: "Everybody's frightened of me. That's the point of me."
- **V (pp 24–29).** Comic, and earns it. The insult that hits "my father," the "Oh no," and "he gets a thank-you?" give Wukong envy and appetite. Ao Guang's glance at the empty coral seat on p14 pays off without anyone explaining it. This movement has the lowest stakes between the two leads, but "Thank you too, Wukong. For coming back" keeps it tied to them.
- **VI (pp 30–37).** Mischief that works. The pig has a voice of his own: vain, greedy, wounded ("I ate most of it on the way"). The daughter's one act, shutting the gate on him herself, gives her a choice and the scene its ending. "I took you, didn't I? And I gave you a robe" brings the robe back to Wukong's own guilt, and the touch on the mended patch says the rest.
- **VII (pp 38–48).** The best movement. On p40, Wukong refuses to fly the monk across, using the monk's own rule. That is the book's argument coming back without a moral being stated: Sanzang asks "just this once," and it is the monkey who holds the line. The ogre's confession ("Their skulls wouldn't sink, so I wear them") is the darkest beat in the book. Sanzang's "You told me the worst thing about yourself first. Nobody made you" answers his own apology on p23. "You're slower than a shoe" and "Then he waited" are a real ending. Page 42 is the only page in the book I would call merely functional.

**Voices.** I applied the attribution-strip test to pp 13, 22, 33 and 44. Every voice survives: Wukong (jokes inside short furious sentences), Sanzang (plain, frightened, final), Guanyin (brief and exact), Ao Guang (polite panic), Bajie (food and vanity), Wujing (flat confession).

**Logistics and aphorism.** There is no logistics dialogue. The road rule is spoken three times (pp 3, 8, 40), but each time it is a refusal inside a negotiation, never an epigram. The third time is a reversal, not a repeat.

## Mandatory findings

**1. Page 43, panel 1. Bajie's idea reads as its opposite.**
- **Defect:** The exchange is "Me, and the horse. Don't look so pleased, Pig." followed by "So what if she told the river monster?" Without a comma, "So what if…" is the everyday dismissive idiom ("who cares if…"). Coming straight after "Don't look so pleased," it reads aloud as Bajie sulking back at Wukong, not as the insight that solves the river.
- **Reader harm:** This is the hinge of Bajie's one moment of being the hero, the line that causes the shout, the head rising and the confession. A first-time reader takes it as a retort, then has to rebuild the meaning when panel 2 shows him bellowing. That is the "understandable after looking back" failure (anti-terse rules 7 and 8: read the exchange aloud).
- **Required fix:** Make the idea a complete, unmistakable thought with its object named. For example: **"Then what if she told the river monster to wait for the pilgrim too?"** Or split it into "Wait — what if she told the river monster too? Maybe he's waiting for the monk!" Keep Wukong's "Don't look so pleased, Pig" ahead of it.

## Nonblocking observations

- **A. Page 42, panel 1.** "I can't even see my own staff in it!" describes an action the panel does not show. Restage so Wukong is jabbing the staff into the sand-water and its end vanishes in the murk. Or change the line to "I can't see a thing in it!"
- **B. Pages 30 and 35.** No one names Old Gao until the pig says "Heaven? Old Gao?" on p35. Context makes it clear, but one cheap clause on p30 removes the guess, for example: "I'm Gao. This is my farm, and we've got trouble here!"
- **C. Page 10.** "which he had not seen for five hundred years" conflicts with pp 1–2, where his face is open to the sky. Suggested wording: "lay on his back, which he had not been able to do for five hundred years, and looked at the stars."
- **D. Page 46.** The caption could tie the raft to the rule so no reader feels the road was skipped. For example: "…It carried the tenth pilgrim across, every mile of it, on the water, and on the far bank it was gone."
- **E. Wukong's own monkeys.** The p1 peach and p3's "grandchildren" set up his kingdom, but after his release he never glances toward home. One clause on p10 (looking east at the green mountain across the sea before he turns west) would give that thread its small aftermath and deepen what staying costs him. Optional.
- **F. Page 1 caption.** "The great hand that beat him" leaves the hand's owner unnamed. That is acceptable as mythic mystery, since no plot step depends on it.
- **Five-word audit.** I checked all 43 listed balloons. Each is a complete thought, a direct answer to the line before it in the same panel, or a deliberate exclamation with one visible referent. None fails. "The tenth what?" is a deliberate puzzle answered on the same page.
- **Page architecture.** Every page has one mode, one turn and a dominant panel, with at most two locations (the p30 gate and front room are one farmhouse). Text fits the budget; p44, the densest page, is about 84 words in short balloons. The mode split is 31/7/10 (65/15/21), which meets the mandate's seven-prose-page floor. The book has no chronicle pages, as its mandate directs.
- **Fidelity and cast.** There is no functional crowd; the six robbers are unnamed scenery. Every named character changes what Wukong or Sanzang wants, knows or chooses. The three "lady in white told me to wait" recruitments are a designed pattern, and each one turns differently (hunger, forgetting, guilt). The third is reached by Bajie working it out, so it builds rather than repeats.
- **Ledger and mandate.** The script does what the ledger says each character wants. The wound line "nobody ever gave me anything" shows up in some form in every movement except the first. In Movement I it is only implied by the tribute peaches, which is acceptable.

Findings: 1 (above). Once Finding 1 is fixed, I would expect this script to pass. A fresh critic should re-audit the whole script.
