## 1. Cold read

A monkey's face sits in a mountain, five hundred years after he fought Heaven and lost. A young monkey has just left him a peach. Guanyin, a woman in white on a cloud, offers him a way out. A monk is walking west to fetch the holy books, and if the monkey guards him the mountain will open. The catch is that the monk has to walk every mile; flying him there would make it the monkey's journey, not his. Others have been asked to wait for the monk too.

The monk is Sanzang. He was found as a baby in a river basket, and he is afraid of almost everything. His companions turned back at the border. He climbs the mountain anyway, terrified, and peels off the seal. The monkey bursts out, kneels, and names himself: Sun Wukong. That night Sanzang gives him a spare robe and mends it while he wears it. Wukong has never been given anything. "It's a good robe."

Robbers attack. Wukong's needle becomes his staff, and he is about to kill the leader when Sanzang throws himself across the man and calls Wukong a robber. Wukong flies off, hurt. He goes to Ao Guang, the Dragon King he once robbed of that staff, and is given nervous tea. Ao Guang shows him a painting: an old man drops his shoe off a bridge three times, and a boy fetches it three times. Wukong hates the story and goes back to his monk. Meanwhile Guanyin gives the weeping monk a cap with a gold band sewn inside and three words to say.

Wukong returns on his own, says he didn't hit anybody, "I had tea," and puts the cap on gladly. That night, frightened when the staff swings past his nose, Sanzang says the words. The cap tears away and the band stays. Wukong flies to Guanyin: "You tricked me!" "Yes." The band will come off in the west. Back at the fire, the monk apologises. He used the words out of fear, not because Wukong had done anything wrong, and he called the cap a present when he knew what it was. Nobody had ever apologised to Wukong before.

A white dragon eats the horse. Wukong taunts it out of the water and pins it. It too was told to wait for the pilgrim. It is Ao Guang's exiled son, so the empty coral seat on p14 pays off. It turns into a white horse.

At Gao Village a pig-headed son-in-law has locked up his bride. Wukong takes her place, scares the pig through the roof, and chases him. The pig was also told to wait, and forgot because he got married. He kneels, apologises to the girl, and she shuts the gate on him herself. He is named Bajie.

At the river of flowing sand, Sanzang asks to be flown across "just this once." Wukong refuses: it's the monk's road. An ogre wearing nine skulls attacks, Bajie holds him off, and Bajie works out that the ogre was told to wait too. The ogre confesses he ate nine monks. Sanzang takes him in, names him Wujing, and the skulls become a raft. At the fire Wukong finally explains the shoe. In the morning he runs ahead, and waits.

- **p40, panel 2.** "I could fly a mountain across. But I can't fly you over it, not one step of it." I reread it: across what? Over the river, or the road? Steps of a river? This is Finding 1.
- **p40, panel 3.** "And I can't swim," said while sitting on a water dragon, with Bajie's "Pigs float" left hanging. I guessed. See note 2.
- **p44.** Nine balloons. The confession is buried under two explanations. I did not stop, but I felt the weight drain. See note 1.
- **p17.** "the white milestone": the one from p4, before the mountain? Has the monk gone back east? See note 4.
- **p28.** "For coming back." I paused on which coming-back he means, then accepted it. It is welcome either way.
- **p7.** How did the monk get down from the summit so quickly? Brief; the "run!" on p6 covers it.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes, plainly. It is funny, warm, and built on a real quarrel. It had me from "get in line" and "before they run out of grandchildren," and it ends on a monkey who waited five hundred years under a rock now choosing to wait. That is a true ending, not a stop. "Then he waited." echoes Volume I's "Someone will come. Wait." without leaning on it.

- **Movement I.** Strong. The scene is driven by want. Wukong wants out, and Guanyin wants a guard who walks. The price arrives in two stages, and the second stage ("walk?") makes the page turn. The prose page about Sanzang earns its place with "Sanzang was frightened of almost everything. He kept walking."
- **Movement II.** The climb has heat: a coward's prayer on the summit. The robe scene is the emotional engine of the book: "Nobody ever gave me anything." / "Then hold still. This is the first." It never says what it means, and the patch pays off twice more.
- **Movement III.** The best movement. The quarrel is real on both sides: "He had a chopper for your head!" against "I can't walk with a robber." The hurt under the anger is staged in close-up. Ao Guang is a comic delight: polite terror, hoping the monkey leaves. "Don't tell anyone you were right" is great subtext, since he concedes by refusing to concede. "I had tea" is a perfect deflection.
- **Movement IV.** Morally the most sophisticated, and enacted, not narrated. The monk misuses the band out of fear, and the script lets him be wrong on the page. Guanyin's "Yes." is exactly in her voice. The apology in prose lands because "That was worse" is specific about what he did. "You'll have to say them a lot" is a complete character in one line.
- **Movement V.** The most formula-shaped movement (fight, taunt, pin, "a lady told me"), but the comedy carries it ("THAT monk. And you ATE his HORSE." / "Oh no."). The empty coral seat pays off as a gift to the reader, and "next time I need tea" gives the dragon an aftermath. It is alive, but a notch below III and IV.
- **Movement VI.** Pure mischief, then a genuine turn. "That was different. I'm better-looking," with two fingers on the mended patch, is a running gag that carries a wound. The daughter's silent gate is a better answer than any line she could have spoken, and she has agency.
- **Movement VII.** It pays off the book's argument. Wukong now holds the rule the monk once held, defers the choice ("This is your choice, not mine"), and Bajie gets to be the hero by shouting. The shoe is finally explained, and "You're slower than a shoe" lands as a callback to "Don't be glad." The one soft spot is that the hinge line of the whole book, on p40, is garbled (Finding 1).

**The presence tests**
- **Voices.** Each character is distinct: Wukong brags and jokes, Sanzang is plain and frightened, Guanyin is exact, Ao Guang is polite to the edge of panic, Bajie talks about food, the ogre is flat. On every page I checked, I could cover the speaker names and still tell who was talking.
- **Logistics dialogue.** None.
- **Aphorisms.** None that are blocking. "On this road it's eat or be eaten" is Wukong's predator creed in his own mouth. "It's a long road, and it eats monks" becomes the book's motif.
- **Wukong's appetite.** It never goes slack. He is loud, hurt and greedy on every page he appears.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**1. p40, panel 2: the book's hinge line is garbled at the moment the volume's argument is paid off.**
- **The defect.** "I can't, Master. I could fly a mountain across. But I can't fly you over it, not one step of it." Read aloud, a listener asks "fly a mountain across *what*?" The next "it" could be the river or the road, and both are in play. "Fly you over… not one step" mixes flying with walking, so the claim smears.
- **Why it matters.** The mandate says this rule is "the book's whole argument," planted in Movement I and paid off here. It is also Wukong's turn from Movement I's "put him in the west by lunchtime."
- **Reader harm.** A first-time reader has to untangle the sentence at the exact moment it should hit. That is a §7.4 antecedent failure and a §7.8 read-aloud failure on the most consequential balloon in the movement. "Recoverable" is not a pass.
- **Required fix.** Rewrite the balloon with full objects, for example: "I can't, Master. I could carry a mountain across this river on my back. But I can't carry you, not over this river, not one step of your road."
- **Strongly recommended, same page.** Let Sanzang recognise that his own rule from p8 has come back to him. After "Because it's your road. If I fly you over, you never crossed it.", add SANZANG: "That's what I told you, the first day." and only then "Then I have to cross it myself. And I can't swim." The page currently plays the reversal without either character registering it, and that recognition is where this page's heat is. The page has room: it is about 60 words against an 80-word budget.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **p44 is dense and dilutes the confession.**
   - **Density.** About 101 words in nine balloons across four panels, and the 15% panel 4 carries 28 words from two speakers. That is within the method's large-lane allowance, but above the 45–80 norm and the roughly six-balloon guide.
   - **Duration.** The darkest revelation in the book ("I ate them") is followed on the same page by two pieces of mechanism: why he swung, and why he hid. That presses on §4.8. The ogre also talks more than his ledger voice ("few words") suggests.
   - **Option.** Merge the two explanations into one ogre balloon, for example: "Under the sand I hear feet, not faces. And every time the pig dragged me up, I saw the Monkey King, and thought he'd come for me." That cuts one exchange without losing anything causal.

2. **p40, panel 3: two loose ends at the river.**
   - The monk says "I can't swim" while sitting on a former water dragon, and Bajie's "Pigs float" offer is never picked up again.
   - A sharp reader will ask why the horse or the pig can't carry him. One line closes it, for example BAJIE: "And the horse was a dragon!" / SANZANG: "Look at him. He won't put a hoof in it." That also gives the white horse a moment of his own.

3. **p45 to p46: the skulls turning into a raft happens between pages.** p45 shows Wujing lifting off the cord; p46 shows the raft already formed. The most wondrous moment is told in a caption rather than shown. If convenient, restage p45 panel 3 as Wujing laying the first skull on the sand-water, glowing as it touches. The line can stay as written.

4. **p17: "the white milestone."** The definite article points back to p4's milestone, which stands before the mountain. That implies the monk has gone back east. Make it "a white milestone" or drop it.

5. **p37 and p45: only one new companion's name is explained.** Bajie's name is glossed ("eight rules") and Wujing's is not. It's optional, but a short gloss in Sanzang's voice would match the pattern.

6. **p30: the shut village is promised but barely shown.** p29's page-turn promises "a valley where every door is shut," and p30 shows only one barred gate. Panel 1 could add shuttered doors along the lane behind Old Gao's lantern.

7. **Five-word audit: all 43 balloons pass.** Each is either a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before it, with one visible referent. "Are you the tenth one?" is a deliberate mystery, answered immediately by "The tenth what?" and the confession. Guanyin's "Yes." answers a complete accusation and is the strongest single word in the book.

8. **Architecture and balance.**
   - Every page has one mode, one turn and one dominant panel, and no page has more than two locations.
   - The mode balance is 31 dramatic, 7 illustrated prose, 10 spectacle (65 / 15 / 21). That meets the volume's prose floor of seven pages.
   - There are no Chronicle pages, as the mandate requires. There are no functional crowds, and every character earns their place.
   - Introductions and re-identifications hold up for a reader who has not read Volume I.

Fix p40's hinge line and this script is ready to approve. My only reservation about it as a reader is that one sentence.
