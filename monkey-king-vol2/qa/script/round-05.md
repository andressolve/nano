## Cold read

I read from "# MOVEMENT I" through page 48 before opening anything else. Here is the story as a first-time reader would tell it.

A monkey has been trapped for five hundred years under a mountain shaped like a giant hand. His own monkeys swim the sea every spring to bring him a peach. A lady in white named Guanyin offers him a way out. A monk is walking west to fetch holy books, and the monkey must guard him the whole way, on foot. He is not allowed to fly the monk there, because then it would be the monkey's journey, not the monk's. She has asked others to wait for the monk too. The monk, Sanzang, was found as a baby in a basket on a river. He is afraid of nearly everything, and his companions have turned back.

Sanzang is terrified of heights, but he climbs the mountain and peels off the seal. The mountain bursts and the monkey, Sun Wukong, kneels to him. That night Sanzang gives him a spare robe and mends it while Wukong wears it. It is the first thing anyone has ever given him; even the peaches were tribute. Robbers attack. Wukong's needle becomes an iron staff and he is about to crush the leader when Sanzang throws himself across the man and calls Wukong a robber. Hurt, Wukong flies off to the Dragon King, the very one he once robbed of that staff. The frightened dragon shows him a painting of a boy who keeps going back for an old man's shoe, hoping the moral will make the monkey leave. Wukong hates the story and goes back.

While he is gone, Guanyin gives the monk a cap with a gold band hidden inside, and three words to say. Sanzang hands it over as a "present". Later, when Wukong swings the staff to show off, Sanzang panics and says the words. The band tightens, the cap tears away, and the band stays on. Wukong goes to Guanyin, and she admits she tricked him. Back at the fire, Sanzang apologizes, both for the words and for the false gift.

Then come three recruits, each of whom was told to wait for the pilgrim:
- **A white dragon** eats the horse. It turns out to be the Dragon King's exiled son, and it becomes the new horse.
- **A pig**, a former marshal of Heaven, has married a farmer's daughter and locked her up. Wukong disguises himself as the bride and scares him through the roof. The pig turns out to be another forgotten recruit. He apologizes to the girl, who shuts the gate on him, and Sanzang names him Bajie.
- **An ogre** in a river of sand fights off every attack. Sanzang now asks Wukong to fly him over, and Wukong refuses: it is the monk's road. Bajie's shouting brings the ogre up. He confesses that he ate nine monks before this one and wears their skulls. Sanzang takes him anyway, names him Wujing, and puts him at his back. The skulls become a raft.

At the last fire, Wukong finally explains the shoe. In the morning the fastest creature in the world runs ahead and waits for the slowest.

I could retell all of that after one read, so the paraphrase test passes.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p1, "The great hand that beat him."** I wondered whose hand. Nothing depends on it, and the finger-shaped ridges carry it. It is a mythic mystery the reader is allowed to wonder about, not a missing step.
- **p6 to p7.** Sanzang is on the summit at the bottom of p6. On p7 he is already far down the slope beside the horse. I paused for half a beat; Wukong's "get off the mountain, fast" covers it. Not a blocker.
- **p28, "For coming back."** I checked whether it means coming out of the water or returning after the robbers. Both readings work and both say the same thing, so this is resonance, not ambiguity.

Nowhere did I have to guess at cause, motive, or who someone was.

## Is it alive?

Yes, and it is good. I wanted every next page.

- **I (pp 1–4).** The image of the open eyes in the rock lands. Wukong is rude, eager, and agrees before he has heard the price. "Get me out before they run out of grandchildren!" is his voice and nobody else's. The rule against flying is planted cleanly and with heat, because it is the one thing he did not want to hear.
- **II (pp 5–10).** "Left foot! No, your other left!" / "I looked down." is the best two-line exchange in the book. The monk's prayer on the summit gives him real courage with a condition attached. The robe scene on p9 is the emotional root of the volume: "What do I owe you for it?" / "Nothing. It's a robe." Wukong's quiet "It's a good robe" says less than he feels, which is exactly right.
- **III (pp 11–17).** Real heat. Sanzang is right, Wukong is hurt, and both say the cruelest true thing ("I can't walk with a robber" / "I waited under a rock for five hundred years for you"). The Ao Guang scene is a comic gem built on subtext: the dragon wants him gone, and "Going back is very admirable. You could go back. Now. Far away." uses the moral as a broom. "Don't tell anyone you were right" is perfect.
- **IV (pp 18–23).** The strongest movement. The reader knows what the cap is and watches Wukong put it on gladly ("Another present? For me?"), and "It suits you." is a lie in three words. The monk says the words out of fear, not need, and the book lets him be wrong. The fight with Guanyin starts from "You let me out from under one mountain and put another one on my head!" / "Yes." The apology on p23 gives the leash-holder the bigger fear. "That's the point of me" is the wound under the noise.
- **V (pp 24–29).** Lighter but not idle. The insult that hits the father, "Oh no.", and "Try five hundred, under a rock. Nobody brought me a horse." keep Wukong's want on the page. The thank-you he complains about not getting, and then gets, pays it off.
- **VI (pp 30–37).** Very funny: the bride trick, "I brought you a peach. I ate most of it on the way.", "Hello, dumpling!" It is also more than a gag reel. Wukong objects to taking the pig, Sanzang answers with the robe, and Wukong's fingers go to the patch: "That was different. I'm better-looking." The daughter's gate is her own choice and the movement's real ending.
- **VII (pp 38–48).** The payoff. Wukong refusing to fly the monk reverses p8 without anyone preaching. Bajie earns his place with the idea and with being brave once. Wujing says the worst thing about himself first, and Sanzang's "at my back, every day. And I won't be afraid of you" closes the monk's own arc of fear. "You're slower than a shoe" and "Then he waited." end the book in character.

**Presence tests, checked:**
- **Want-from:** present in every scene. Examples: Ao Guang wants him gone; Old Gao wants rescue; Bajie wants to be thought handsome; Wujing wants to be taken knowing the worst.
- **Subtext:** on most dramatic pages ("It suits you.", "Going back is very admirable.", "That was different.", "Don't be glad.").
- **Attribution-strip:** passes. The eight voices are distinct.
- **Appetite:** Wukong is never serene. Even in restraint on p44 his hand is tight on the staff.
- **Logistics:** none.
- **Aphorism:** none blocking. The near-epigrams ("eat or be eaten", "Nothing that big hides unless it's frightened", "it's your road") each come from a specific person's worldview or deduction, at a moment of choice.

## Checks performed

- **Lines that set up their own answer:** "Are you the tenth one?" (answered at once by "The tenth what?").

None is compressed plot logic. All pass.

- Guanyin names herself on p2 and again on p16.
- Wukong is "the Monkey King" on p1 and gives his full name on p8, before anything depends on it.
- Sanzang is named in the p4 prose and by himself on p8.
- Ao Guang is named with his title on p14, and the pillar-to-needle history is given there.
- Old Gao, Bajie, and Wujing are each named or placed before the plot needs them.

- Seal leads to release.
- Robe leads to the wound.
- Near-killing leads to the quarrel, the departure, the shoe, and the return.
- Cap leads to the band, then rage, then Guanyin, then the apology.
- Every recruit quotes Guanyin.
- The skulls' prophecy, given by Guanyin through Wujing, explains why the raft does not break the no-flying rule.

**Continuity.** All consistent: the horse and saddle, the patch on the left shoulder, the band from p20 onward, the luggage, the seasons (spring, then autumn, then a yellowing plain), and the six robbers.

**Architecture.**
- Every page has one mode, one dominant panel, and at most two locations.
- The text budget is clean per the audit.
- No run of more than four dramatic pages.

**Balance and volume mandate.**
- Modes: 31 dramatic, 7 prose, 10 spectacle. That meets the volume's floor of seven prose pages and its permitted lean toward spectacle.
- The mandate's countermeasures are all met: every monster is a recruit; the robbers stay foes; the quarrel drives every movement; the monk is frightened, stubborn, wrong once (the band), and brave on the page (pp 6, 13, 45); the road rule is planted in I and paid off in VII without being stated as a moral.

**Aftermath.** Every consequential beat has one: the quarrel goes to the sea; the band gets three pages; the confession is followed by acceptance, then the raft, then the fire.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **Missing heading for Movement VII.** There is no "# MOVEMENT VII — THE RIVER OF FLOWING SAND · pp 38–48" heading before page 38, though the architecture and the round history both say there are seven movements. It does not render and no tool parses it. If it is added, the method treats it as a script change and the gate reopens; leaving it is harmless to the reader.
2. **Stray `>` on p44.** There is an empty `>` line between Panel 3's last balloon and the "**Panel 4**" heading. It does not render. The same caveat about reopening the gate applies.
3. **p6 to p7, the monk's descent.** If the page packet wants to make it effortless, the p6 Panel 3 image could show Sanzang already scrambling down the ridge as the seal lifts. That is a staging note for the packet, not a script change.
4. **Mild page-turn lines on resolved pages.** The turn lines on p23 ("the road goes on into the mountains") and p47 ("it is morning") are gentle. Both sit on pages that end a beat, and "You'll have to say them a lot" already gives p23 its forward pull. Acceptable as written.
5. **Wukong's wound in Movement I.** The "nobody gave me anything" wound is carried only by the peach image and is named on p9 ("Even the peaches are tribute"). That satisfies the mandate's once-per-movement clause through the plant and its payoff. Worth protecting the peach's visibility on p1 in the packet.
