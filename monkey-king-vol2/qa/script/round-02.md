## 1. Cold read

A monkey has been pinned under a mountain for five hundred years. A lady in white, Guanyin, offers him a way out: guard a monk walking west, on foot, every mile. A frightened young monk, Sanzang, climbs the mountain and peels off the seal. The monkey, Sun Wukong, bursts free and kneels to him. That night the monk gives him his spare robe and mends it on him. Wukong has never been given anything, and he doesn't know what to do with his hands.

On the road, Wukong nearly kills a robber. The monk calls him a robber too, and Wukong flies off in a rage to the Dragon King under the sea. The terrified dragon serves him tea and shows him a painting of a boy who went back three times for an old man's shoe. Wukong hates the story and goes back anyway. While he is gone, Guanyin gives the monk a cap with a gold band inside it and three words to say. The monk hands it over as a present. The first time he is frightened, he says the words, and the band clamps onto Wukong's head. Wukong storms off to Guanyin, who admits the trick. He comes back, and the monk apologizes.

Then three recruits, all told by the lady to wait for a pilgrim:
- **The white dragon.** It ate the monk's horse, turns out to be the Dragon King's exiled son, and becomes the horse.
- **The pig.** He married a farmer's daughter and locked her up. Wukong disguises himself as the bride and scares him out through the roof. The pig remembers he was supposed to be waiting, apologizes to the girl, and she shuts the gate on him.
- **The river ogre.** He ate nine monks before this one. Wukong refuses to fly the monk across the river because it is the monk's road. The pig works out that the ogre was told to wait too. The ogre confesses and is taken in, and his nine skulls become a raft.

At the last fire, Wukong admits the shoe story is why he came back. In the morning he runs ahead, and waits.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed:**
- **p14.** "Iron pillar" and then "the staff": I had to join them myself. It is recoverable, but I paused (note C).
- **p15.** I couldn't tell who was speaking over the painting. Ao Guang's sly "You could go back. Now. Far away." needs his face, and the panel doesn't contain him.
- **p23.** I expected the monk to apologize for the lie of the gift ("It suits you"). He apologizes only for using the words.
- **p41 → p44.** I stopped. Why did a creature waiting for the tenth monk swing a spade at the tenth monk?
- **p42.** "You fought the dragon in the stream!" Bajie wasn't there. I paged back to check.
- **p45.** "where I can't see you, and I won't be afraid" read to me first as the monk putting the ogre out of sight so he won't have to be scared. On a second read I took it as trust: turning his back on a man-eater. I had to reread the most important line of the climax.
- **p43, p40, p27 and others.** Several reply panels are staged without the person replying in them. I kept assigning balloons to whoever was visible.

## 2. Is it alive?

**Yes. This script is alive, and in places it is very good.** It is funny out loud, it hurts in the right places, and its spine — gift versus taking, flying versus walking — is carried by what the characters do, not by what they say about it. The voices survive the attribution-strip test:
- Wukong: brash, short sentences, laughing mid-line.
- Sanzang: plain and stubborn ("I looked down.").
- Ao Guang: panicky courtesy ("No. No, no. Not you again.").
- Bajie: food and vanity.
- Wujing: flat confession.
- Guanyin: one-word honesty ("Yes.").

Movement by movement:

- **I (pp 1–4).** A strong hook. "Get me out before they run out of grandchildren!" makes Wukong's appetite instant. The peaches are planted to pay off on p9. Guanyin's rule is planted cleanly. The p4 prose is warm and ends on a good beat ("He kept walking.").
- **II (pp 5–10).** The robe scene on p9 is the heart of the book. "Then hold still. This is the first, and I haven't finished sewing it." is exactly the kind of line the method asks for: the monk deflects tenderness into an instruction. "It's a good robe," spoken alone, earns its five words. Want-from is clear on every page.
- **III (pp 11–17).** Real heat on p13: "I waited under a rock for five hundred years for you." Ao Guang is the funniest supporting character in the book. "That was different. That was you." and "Don't tell anyone you were right." are character, not quips. p17's "I had tea" is perfect subtext. The one weak spot is p15's off-panel staging (Finding 1).
- **IV (pp 18–23).** The strongest movement. The monk's complicity on p18 ("It suits you," not smiling) is real tension: he wants to give a gift and a leash at once. p21's "You gave me a present, and it was a trap!" lands like a blow. Guanyin's "Yes." is great. The apology on p23 is lovely, but it answers the smaller wrong (Finding 2).
- **V (pp 24–29).** The most reel-like stretch. The fight on pp 25–26 is serviceable rather than surprising. It is rescued by "Don't you DARE talk about my father!" as a hook, "Oh no.", and above all "Thank you too, Wukong. For coming back." Competent with a moving ending. I felt it.
- **VI (pp 30–37).** Pure mischief, well built. "Hurt him? Master, I'm going to marry him." / "Hello, dumpling!" / "I forgot! I got married!" The p36 objection ("he locked a girl in a shed") and its answer ("I took you, didn't I? And I gave you a robe.") give the movement real moral weight. The daughter shutting the gate herself keeps her a chooser.
- **VII (pp 38–48).** On p40 the two leads have swapped places: the monk begs to be flown, and the monkey refuses. That is the book's argument enacted, not stated. Bajie earns his keep twice (blocking the blade, then thinking of the shout). The ogre's confession is the gravest scene, and "You told me the worst thing about yourself first. Nobody made you." is earned. "You're slower than a shoe." and "Then he waited." are a real ending. The defects here are causal, not deadness: Findings 3, 4 and 5.

**Presence tests:**
- **Want-from:** passes in every scene.
- **Subtext:** present on most dramatic pages (pp 9, 15, 17, 18, 23, 47).
- **Enacted, not narrated:** passes everywhere. The release, the band, the transformation, the roof and the raft are all shown.
- **Logistics dialogue:** none that blocks. One flat line is noted below (note D).
- **Aphorism dialogue:** nothing blocking. "That's what a pilgrimage is." (p8) is the closest, and it is the monk's own stubbornness.
- **Page-turn reasons:** present and real on every page.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**1. Pages 5, 14, 15, 25, 27, 28, 35, 40, 43, 44: replies spoken by characters who are not in their panel.**
- **Defect.** In these panels the script gives a balloon to someone the staging doesn't place in the frame:
  - **p5, panel 3.** Wukong's two lines; the panel is the monk looking up the ridges. The source of the voice isn't stated, though p6 does state it.
  - **p14, panel 3.** "That was different. That was you." The panel shows only "Ao Guang's face".
  - **p15, panels 1–2.** All three of Ao Guang's lines, including "Going back is very admirable. You could go back. Now. Far away." Panel 2 shows only the painting and a small Wukong.
  - **p25, panel 3.** Sanzang's "Then leave it be, and come out." The panel shows the dragon's tail and Wukong.
  - **p27, panel 3.** Wukong's "Years? Try five hundred…" The panel shows only the dragon.
  - **p28, panels 2–3.** Wukong's lines in both. Panel 2 shows "the dragon looking at the monk"; panel 3 shows the monk and the horse.
  - **p35, panel 2.** Wukong's "Neither. I'm guarding a monk…" The panel shows only the pig.
  - **p40.** Panel 2 is "close on Wukong", yet Sanzang asks "Why not?". Panel 3 is "Sanzang looking out", yet Bajie says "I can. Pigs float."
  - **p43, panel 1.** Wukong's "Me, and the horse." The panel shows only Bajie.
  - **p44, panel 3.** The ogre's "Then the lady in white came…" The panel is "Wukong, quiet, beside the monk".
- **Reader harm.** In most of these panels only one face is visible, so the reader's first guess is that the visible face is talking. "Me, and the horse." under a lone Bajie reads as Bajie answering his own question. "I can. Pigs float." under a lone Sanzang contradicts the line he just spoke. Several of these are the funniest or slyest lines in the book (Ao Guang's "Far away", Bajie's "Pigs float"), and they need the speaker's face to land. This is the "first balloon belongs to a later speaker / tail to empty space" failure, and it is cheapest to fix here.
- **Required fix.** Restage each of these panels so every speaker is in frame on their own side: a two-shot, a foreground shoulder, or Ao Guang leaning into the painting panel. Where an off-panel voice is intended (p5 from the cleft), write it into the staging as p6 does ("a balloon from the cleft at the bottom of the frame"). No line needs to change.

**2. Page 23: the apology answers the smaller wrong.**
- **Defect.** On p18 the reader watches Sanzang hand over a trap as "something for you, to go with the robe" and lie, "It suits you." On p21 Wukong names exactly that: "You gave me a present, and it was a trap!" But p23's apology covers only the panic: "I said the words because I was frightened of you… That was wrong."
- **Reader harm.** The book's wound is "nobody ever gave me anything I didn't take." The second gift Wukong ever received was false, and the reconciliation never touches it. The reader's moral ledger stays open. Worse, the monk later uses the robe as his credential (p36, "And I gave you a robe."), and that rings a little hollow when nobody has repaired what happened to the second gift. The ledger itself says the monk "works it out on page 23"; he works out only half.
- **Required fix.** Add one sentence to the p23 prose, in the monk's mouth, owning the lie. For example: "And I called the cap a present when I knew what it was. That was worse." If you like, add a beat where Wukong touches the mended robe patch, not the band, before "You'll have to say them a lot." The prose has room: it is about 100 words against a 120 ceiling.

**3. Pages 41 and 44: the ogre's attack has no motive.**
- **Defect.** On p41 the ogre bursts up swinging his spade "down at the monk on his horse", and the page-turn says "the monster wants the monk." On p44 he reveals he was told to "wait for the tenth one", and asks "Are you the tenth one?" Nobody ever explains why a creature waiting for the tenth monk tried to cut the tenth monk in half. The dragon's version of this has its excuse on the page ("I was hungry… nobody told me the pilgrim would have a horse"). The ogre's has none.
- **Reader harm.** The reader has to invent a reason: habit, hunger, or that he couldn't see through the sand. Without one, his confession on p44 sits on top of a contradiction the reader has just watched.
- **Required fix.** Give it a line on p44, ideally as a question from Sanzang that raises the heat. Sanzang: "Then why did you swing at me?" Ogre: "Under the sand I can't see faces. I hear feet on the bank, and I come up hungry. I nearly made you the tenth skull." This also deepens Sanzang's choice on p45. p44 has room (about 56 words).

**4. Page 42: Bajie knows something he never saw.**
- **Defect.** Bajie says "You fought the dragon in the stream!" Bajie joined on p37; the dragon fight was on pp 25–26.
- **Reader harm.** The reader pages back to check whether the pig was there. Characters acting on information the reader never saw them get is a named finding.
- **Required fix.** Give the line to Sanzang, who was there: "You fought the dragon under the water!" Keep Wukong's reply, "That was water! This is soup!" Alternatively, add a sentence to the p38 prose in which Wukong boasts to Bajie about the dragon fight, and keep the line where it is.

**5. Page 45: the climax line reads two ways.**
- **Defect.** "You'll walk behind me, where I can't see you, and I won't be afraid."
- **Reader harm.** The intended meaning is trust: the frightened monk turns his back on a man-eater, the one time he is brave on the page as the mandate requires. But the clause order also reads as avoidance: put him behind me so I don't have to look at him and be scared. Read aloud, a listener can't tell which, and the two readings are opposites. This is the monk's bravest moment, and the anti-terse rules say a necessary line must have one meaning.
- **Required fix.** Make the bravery explicit, using the same idea with more words. For example: "I will. I'll call you Wujing. You'll walk right behind me, at my back, where I can't see you. And I won't be afraid of you." Or: "I'll turn my back on you every day of the road, and I won't be afraid." Optionally, add a small beat in panel 2 of Wukong's eyes going wide.

## 4. Five-word audit (all 44 balloons)

All 44 short balloons pass. Each is a complete exclamation or a direct answer to a complete question in the same or previous balloon, with one visible referent. The ones I checked hardest:
- **"Yes." (p22)** works as a confession to a full accusation.
- **"Not anymore." (p33)** is uniquely tied to "He's under a mountain!"
- **"Gold, hold fast." (pp 19, 21)** was planted verbatim on p16.
- **"It suits you." (p18)** carries its subtext on purpose, and the cap is the only "it".
- **"And then?" (p15)** is a direct prompt to the story being told.
- **"Me, and the horse." (p43) and "I can. Pigs float." (p40)** pass on wording but fail on staging; see Finding 1.
- **"And then you ate them!" (p35)** passes; see note E.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

- **A. Text budget.**
  - Over the 45–80 dramatic band, within the ~105 exception that needs protected balloon lanes: p3 (about 100 words, seven balloons), p30 (about 92, including a 24-word balloon), p14 (about 90, seven balloons), p15 (about 85), p2 (about 83).
  - All prose pages are within budget, the largest at about 102 words (p4).
  - None of this is over budget, but the page prompts for these pages should reserve lanes first. If p3 crowds, p3 panel 3 could become its own beat rather than losing a word.
- **B. Mode balance.** 65 / 15 / 21 against a target of 60 / 20 / 13. The volume mandate explicitly accepts a seven-page prose floor and a spectacle lean like Volume I's. Acceptable.
- **C. p14.** A cold reader has to connect "my iron pillar, the one that held the sea down" with "the staff". Ao Guang's next line names the staff, so it is recoverable. One clause would make it immediate: "…and you shrank it down to a needle behind your ear."
- **D. p42, panel 3.** Sanzang's "And we can't cross while he's down there." is the flattest line in the book; it only states the situation. A line with want in it would be better, for example: "He's frightened of something. Nothing that big hides unless it's frightened."
- **E. p35.** "And then you ate them!" ("them" = fields) works as a joke, but "And then you ate everything that grew in them!" is cleaner.
- **F. p48.** "He looked back at the slowest of them": the monk is riding, so who is slowest is ambiguous. If the monk is meant, as the callback to "slower than a shoe" suggests, "at his monk, the slowest of them all" would fix it. The ending is lovely either way.
- **G. Mandate check: Wukong's wound in Movement I.** The mandate asks that the wound be visible at least once per movement. In Movement I it shows only indirectly (Heaven's staring, the peaches that p9 later calls tribute). It works because p9 pays it off. If you want it explicit, Guanyin's offer on p3 could draw a "what do you get out of it?" from him.
- **H. Planning-doc hygiene.** In `03-VOLUME-ARCHITECTURE.md`, the "Emotional duration" paragraph still uses the old page numbers: confession p43 and acceptance p44 should be p44 and p45, and the crossing p45 and fire p46 should be p46 and p47. This is not a script defect, but fix it before the page contract is generated.
- **I. p30.** The panels go from outside the barred gate to the front room inside. That is one property and the ellipsis is clear ("I catch demons." leads straight to the feast), so it is not a location violation.

Every finding above is a cheap text or staging fix; none needs a new page. With them done this should be a short re-audit, but it has to be a full re-audit of all 48 pages.
