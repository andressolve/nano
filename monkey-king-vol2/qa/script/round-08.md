## Cold read

The Monkey King has been trapped under a mountain for five hundred years. His monkeys swim the sea every spring to bring him a peach. Guanyin comes down on a cloud and offers him a way out: guard a monk walking to the far west, on foot, every mile, because a flight there would be his journey and not the monk's. She adds that others are waiting too. Sanzang is a frightened orphan whose companions turned back at the border. He climbs the mountain, terrified of heights, and peels off the seal. The mountain bursts and Wukong kneels to him. That first night Sanzang gives him his spare robe and mends it while Wukong wears it. "Nobody ever gave me anything."

Robbers attack. Wukong's needle becomes the staff, and he is about to kill the leader when the monk throws himself across the man. The monk calls him a robber, and Wukong flies off in a rage to Ao Guang, the Dragon King he once robbed of that staff. The terrified dragon serves him tea and shows him a painting of a boy who keeps going back for an old man's dropped shoe, mostly because he wants the monkey gone. Meanwhile Guanyin gives the weeping monk a cap with a gold band inside and the words "Gold, hold fast." Wukong comes back on his own. He puts the cap on gladly. When he shows off with the staff, the monk panics and says the words. Wukong rages, flies to Guanyin ("Yes"), and comes back again. The monk apologizes, which no one in Heaven ever did.

Three recruits follow, each one waiting for "the pilgrim":
- **The dragon** eats the horse, turns out to be Ao Guang's exiled son, and becomes the horse.
- **The pig** courts the "bride", who is really Wukong in disguise, shoots through the roof, and admits he forgot he was supposed to wait and got married instead. Wukong objects to taking a man who locked a girl in a shed. Sanzang answers, "I gave you a robe." The girl shuts the gate on the pig herself.
- **The river ogre** at a river no feather floats on confesses that he ate nine monks and wears their skulls because they would not sink. The monk takes him anyway. The skulls become a raft.

At the last fire Wukong finally explains the shoe. In the last image he runs ahead and waits for them.

## Is it alive?

Yes, plainly. It is funny and it moves, and I wanted the next page all the way through.

- **I (pp 1–4):** Wukong has real hunger ("before they run out of grandchildren"). The rule of the book is planted as a bargain, not stated as a moral.
- **II (pp 5–10):** The climb is suspense with jokes ("your other left"). The robe scene on p9 is the best page in the book. The wound is spoken plainly, and it lands.
- **III (pp 11–17):** The quarrel is real heat: each side is right. Ao Guang's "You could go back. Now. Far away." is perfect subtext, since he is persuading the monkey in order to get rid of him. "I had tea" is a lovely exit line.

- **VI (pp 30–37):** The funniest movement ("I forgot! I got married!", "Couldn't it be two?"). It earns its moral beat with the robe patch and the girl's silent shut gate.
- **VII (pp 38–48):** The reversal on p40 (the monkey refuses the monk's flight) pays off the whole book. The confession on p44 is the strongest scene in it. The skulls that "wouldn't sink" become a raft that cannot sink, a setup and payoff a child will feel. The ending ("Then he waited") is a true ending.
