## 1. Cold read

The Monkey King has been trapped under a mountain for five hundred years. The caption says the hand that beat him turned into the mountain. Every spring a young monkey swims the sea to bring him a peach.

A woman in white on a cloud, Guanyin, offers him a way out. He must guard a monk walking west to fetch holy books, and he must do it on the ground, every mile. If he flies the monk there, it becomes his journey and not the monk's. She adds that others are waiting for the monk too.

The monk, Sanzang, was found as a baby in a basket on a river. He is frightened of almost everything and keeps walking anyway. His two companions turn back at the border. He reaches the mountain, climbs it terrified, and peels off the seal. The mountain bursts open. Wukong kneels to him and offers to somersault him west. Sanzang refuses, because the road has to be his.

The first night, Sanzang gives Wukong his spare robe and sews it while Wukong is wearing it. "Nobody ever gave me anything."

Robbers attack. Wukong's needle becomes a staff and scatters them. He is about to bring the staff down on the leader when Sanzang throws himself across the man and calls Wukong a robber. Wukong is hurt and flies off. He visits the Dragon King, Ao Guang, the old victim he took the staff from, who is terrified and pours him tea. On a painted screen, an old man drops his shoe off a bridge three times and a boy fetches it three times. Ao Guang's real point is that Wukong could go back, far away, now. Wukong hates the story and goes back to his monk.

Meanwhile Guanyin gives the weeping monk a cap with a gold band sewn inside, and three words. Wukong returns on his own ("I had tea"). Sanzang gives him the cap without telling him what it is.

That night they argue about violence again. Wukong swings the staff past Sanzang's nose to show off, and Sanzang, frightened, says the words. The band tightens and the cap tears away, but the band stays. Wukong raises the staff over the monk; the words again. He flies to Guanyin: "You tricked me!" She answers, "Yes." She tells him to go back, because the monk is frightened and alone. At the fire, the monk apologises, both for using the words out of fear and for calling a trap a present. Wukong sits down on the other side of the fire.

A white dragon eats the horse. Wukong taunts it out of hiding by insulting its father and pins it. It was told to wait for the pilgrim. It is Ao Guang's exiled son, banished for setting fire to his father's best pearl. It apologises, becomes the horse, and Wukong is thanked "for coming back to me."

At Gao Village, Wukong frees a locked-up daughter and takes her place in the bride's bed. The pig-bridegroom panics at the name of the Great Sage and bursts through the roof. When he is cornered and hears the word "pilgrim," it turns out he was told to wait too, and forgot because he got married. Wukong objects to taking him along. Sanzang answers, "I took you, didn't I? And I gave you a robe." The pig apologises to the girl, and she shuts the gate on him herself.

At a river of sand eight hundred miles wide, the monk asks to be flown across. Wukong refuses, and gives the monk's own reason back to him: it is the monk's road. A man-eating ogre attacks. Bajie fights him under the sand, then shouts "PILGRIM" to draw him up. The ogre confesses first that he ate nine earlier monks and wears their skulls. Wukong leaves the decision to the monk. Sanzang takes the ogre, puts him at his back, and promises not to be afraid. The skulls become a raft. By the fire Wukong finally explains the shoe. In the morning he runs ahead, looks west, looks back at his slow monk, and waits.

**Where I stopped:**

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes. It is the warmest script I have read in this workspace. It is funny about every two pages, and it hurts in the right places.

**Movement I.** Wukong's first line, "get in line. Everyone in Heaven already had a turn," puts his whole appetite on the page at once. The scene has heat: Guanyin wants a guardian, Wukong wants out, and the price ("Walk?") lands as a comic blow. The p4 prose earns its quiet. "He kept walking" is the monk's whole character in three words.

**Movement II.** "Left foot! No, your other left!" / "I looked down." and the prayer on the summit put fear and courage side by side. The robe scene on p9 is the heart of the book. Wukong does not know what to do with his hands, and the patch becomes a gesture he repeats later. This is enacted, not narrated.

**Movement III.** The quarrel is real heat. A man's head is literally under the staff, and both of them are right. Ao Guang is the best comic character in the book. He is frightened and polite, he wants the monkey gone, and he hides it all under a parable. "That was different. That was you." The return ("Don't make a speech." / "I wasn't going to.") is full of subtext.

**Movement IV.** The best movement. The dramatic irony of p18 ("It suits you," and the monk not smiling) does the work. Guanyin's "Yes." is the strongest single word in the book. The apology on p23 is in prose, but it is quoted, intimate, and earned, and "Nobody in Heaven had ever told him they were sorry" is interior that a picture cannot carry. That is a legitimate use of the prose mode.

**Movement V.** The insult turns into a clue ("Don't you DARE talk about my father"). "Try five hundred, under a rock." Being thanked lands on the wound. The p29 coda, with the horse looking down into streams and the monk letting it, is quietly beautiful.

**Movement VI.** A pure set piece with appetite. Bajie's voice is unmistakable from his first balloon ("I ate most of it on the way"). The p36–37 split pays off. The objection, the patch, and the girl shutting the gate keep the comedy honest and give the daughter a choice.

**Movement VII.** The quietest stretch is pp38–39. From p40 on it climbs. The monk asking to be flown, and being refused in his own words, is the book's argument shown without a moral. "Your choice, not mine" proves the arc without a speech. The ogre's flat confession is the one grave note, and it is played straight. "Then he waited" echoes the five hundred years and is a true last line.

**Presence tests:**
- **Want-from:** each scene has one. Guanyin wants an answer, Ao Guang wants him gone, the dragon wants forgiveness, Old Gao wants rescue, Bajie wants food and a home, the ogre wants to be taken knowing the worst.
- **Logistics dialogue:** none.
- **Aphorism dialogue:** none. Even the rule ("it's your road") is said in anger and refusal, not as an epigram.
- **Attribution-strip:** passes cleanly across all eight voices.
- **Appetite:** Wukong is never serene.
- **Page-turns:** every page has a real reason to turn.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **p3: "Kings get paid."** Wedged between "I'll do it!" and "Five hundred years of it," it can briefly read as a demand for payment from Guanyin. Page 9 explains it perfectly on its own. Either move it to the end of the first balloon ("…to bring their king a peach, because kings get paid"), or let p9 carry it alone.
2. **p28 is the densest page of feeling.** It holds the father reveal, the transformation, and "Thank you too, Wukong." It works, but the thank-you is the movement's heart and shares the transformation panel. The packet should protect that balloon's lane.
3. **p42: why Bajie can fight in the sand-river.** One clause would answer it and reward a sharp reader. For example, on p33: "I was a marshal of Heaven's river once!" Or here: "Water's my old job, big brother."
4. **pp36–37 gate staging.** On p36 the pig kneels "in the courtyard" with Old Gao and his daughter "at the gate behind them." On p37 she shuts the gate on the departing party. Stage both pages outside the gate in the packet so the closing gate shuts the travellers out.
5. **p44 runs nine balloons.** Panel 3 holds four, about 50 words. That is inside the budget, but the builder prompt should reserve that panel's balloon lanes before placing the ogre's face and the skulls.
