## Cold read

**What happens.** A monkey has spent five hundred years pinned under a mountain shaped like a hand. A lady in white, Guanyin, offers him a way out. He must guard a monk walking west, on foot, every mile. The monk, Sanzang, is a frightened orphan whose two companions turned back at the border. He climbs the mountain, peels off the seal, and the monkey bursts out: Sun Wukong, Great Sage Equal to Heaven. Sanzang gives him a spare robe, the first thing anyone has ever given him.

Robbers attack. Wukong flattens them and raises his staff over the leader. Sanzang throws himself across the man and calls Wukong a robber, and Wukong leaves, hurt. Under the sea, a nervous Dragon King gives him tea and shows him a painting: a boy who fetches an old man's shoe three times. Wukong hates the story and goes back. Meanwhile Guanyin has given the weeping monk a cap with a gold band inside it and three words. The monk gives Wukong the cap as a present. When Wukong swings the staff past his nose, Sanzang panics and says the words, and the band clamps onto Wukong's head. Wukong confronts Guanyin ("You tricked me!" / "Yes."), comes back, and the monk apologizes.

Three recruits follow, each told by the lady to wait for a pilgrim:
- **The dragon.** A young dragon eats the horse, then becomes the new horse. It is the Dragon King's exiled son.
- **The pig.** A pig-husband at Gao Village is tricked by Wukong disguised as the bride, chased, and named Bajie.
- **The river ogre.** An ogre in an eight-hundred-mile river of sand confesses he ate nine monks. Sanzang takes him anyway and names him Wujing. His nine skulls become the raft across.

At the fire, Wukong finally explains the shoe. In the morning he runs ahead, looks back at the slowest of them, and waits.

## Is it alive?

**Yes, and that is the most important thing in this report.** This script has a pulse. The two leads want things from each other on almost every page. Wukong's voice is unmistakable with the labels stripped off, and the book's last line ("Then he waited.") earns the engine instead of stating it. The findings below are a fix list for a good script, not a rescue.

- **I. Five Hundred Years.** Strong cold open. "Everyone in Heaven already had a turn" and "Ask a rock?" give Wukong appetite and wound inside eight lines. Guanyin is crisp. The dead spot is the Old Ma line on p3: a non sequitur that also contradicts the movement after it. The p4 prose is serviceable, with a little telling ("gentle and stubborn"), but "He kept walking" lands.
- **II. The Seal.** Lovely. "Your other left!" / "I looked down." is the book's first real laugh. The prayer at the seal is quietly brave. The robe page (p9) is the heart of the volume: Wukong holding his arms out, not knowing what to do with his hands, is exactly the wound the mandate wants.
- **III. The Robbers and the Shoe.** Hot. The quarrel on p13 has real heat. *He is deciding whether the only person who ever needed him is worth being called a robber by.* The sea palace is funny ("That was different. That was you."). The return on p17 is excellent: "Don't make a speech." / "I wasn't going to." / "I had tea." One flaw: Ao Guang says the parable's moral out loud.
- **IV. The Band.** The best movement. Sanzang's eyes-down guilt on p18 ("It suits you." is real subtext). He panics into the words on p19: the monk wrong, on the page, as the mandate demands. Then Guanyin's one-word "Yes.", and an apology Wukong has never had. I felt this one.
- **V. The Dragon in the Stream.** Energetic and funny ("Your mother was a tadpole"; "nobody told me the pilgrim would have a horse"). But the quarrel goes quiet and Wukong's wound vanishes. Here he is only the weapon, which is the mandate's named failure. The architecture's turn ("the monster is somebody's son, waiting like he waited") is never felt on the page: Wukong never sees himself in the dragon. The father reveal fizzles because the reader never learned Ao Guang's name. **Competent, and the closest the book comes to monster-of-the-week.**
- **VI. The Bride of Gao Village.** The comic peak. The bride's turning head and "Hello, dumpling!" is the set piece the boys will remember. But the story rests on the pig having imprisoned his wife, and that is never answered. The quarrel and the wound are again absent. Bajie's naming is charming.
- **VII. The River of Flowing Sand.** Strong. Sanzang asking to be carried, and Wukong refusing with the monk's own rule, is a real reversal. Bajie gets his moment by shouting. Wujing's confession, "Their skulls wouldn't sink, so I wear them," is the most unsettling and moving line in the book. Sanzang choosing the monk-eater is his "brave once," and it nearly lands (see Finding 10). The p46 shoe callback and "You're slower than a shoe" are earned.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p3: "Old Ma" is unintroduced, and the peaches contradict the book's central wound (p9).**
- **Defect:**
  - "Old Ma brought me peaches until she died, and her grandson does it now." A reader without Volume I doesn't know who Old Ma is, that she was a monkey, or why Wukong brings her up in the middle of a bargain.
  - Worse, p1 *shows* a monkey bringing him a peach, and p3 says this has gone on for centuries. Then p9 says "Nobody ever gave me anything."
- **Reader harm:** The reader has to decide whether Wukong is lying, forgetful, or the book is careless. That doubt lands on the exact line the volume mandate calls the wound under the noise.
- **Fix:** Keep the peaches; they are a lovely image. Make them tribute, not gifts.
  - On p3, replace the line with something like: "Yes! I'll do it! My monkeys swim the sea every spring to bring their king a peach. Five hundred years of it. Get me out before they run out of grandchildren!" This also explains the distant green mountain on p1.
  - On p9, make the contradiction a beat: "Everything I've ever had, I took. The staff, the armor. Even the peaches are tribute; kings get paid. Nobody ever gave me anything."

**2. p3: "When he comes, he must open it himself." The antecedent fails.**
- **Defect:** "It" could be the mountain, the seal, or the way out. The seal hasn't been mentioned in words, and on p1 it is a tiny prop.
- **Reader harm:** Release is the mechanism of the whole movement. The reader learns what "it" was only on p5.
- **Fix:** "When he comes, he must climb up and peel off the gold seal himself. Nobody else can."

**3. p9, panel 3: the words contradict the picture.**
- **Defect:** "Then hold still… I haven't finished sewing it" is placed over "Wukong sitting by the fire in the robe, touching the mended patch." The sewing is visibly finished, and nobody is holding still.
- **Reader harm:** The best line on the page reads as a mistake.
- **Fix:** Move the line into panel 2, where Sanzang is still sewing. Give panel 3 a silent beat, or a new short line for Wukong touching the patch alone. That is where the scene's subtext belongs.

**4. p11: the robber leader has three balloons, which breaks the volume's hard cast rule.**
- **Defect:** The volume mandate and the ledger say everyone outside the eight locks is scenery: "unnamed, unlocked, no balloons." The ledger lists the six robbers as scenery, yet ROBBER speaks three balloons.
- **Harm:** If uncorrected, this becomes a lock and reference problem two gates from now, an unlocked speaking face. Text cannot be added at the page gate, so the decision has to be made here.
- **Fix:** Choose one, explicitly.
  - **(a) Restage silent.** The leader grabs the horse's bridle, and two men tug at the bundle and the robe. Wukong carries the scene: "You want his horse, his bundle, and the robe off his back? Master, stand behind the horse. It's my first day as a bodyguard, and I've been looking forward to it." Then: "Never seen a sewing needle like this one? Watch the needle."
  - **(b) The owner formally amends the mandate** to allow one unlocked speaking walk-on, with the reason written into the clause, as the mandate requires.

**5. p14: Ao Guang and his history with Wukong live only in Volume I.**
- **Defect:**
  - "Whatever you've come for, I haven't got another one." Another what?
  - "You are a robber. You robbed me." Of what? Ao Guang's name never renders on p14 or p15.
  - Then on p28 Wukong says "Ao Guang? I had tea with him last week." To a cold reader that is an unknown name, so the son-of-the-dragon reveal misfires.
- **Reader harm:** The joke ("That was different. That was you.") and the p28 payoff both depend on facts the reader was never given.
- **Fix:** Panel 1, Wukong lands: "Ao Guang! Dragon King of the Eastern Sea! Still got that throne?" Ao Guang: "No. Not you again. You took my iron pillar, the one that holds down the sea. I haven't got another one." This also pays off "The staff… I took" from p9. In panel 3, "You robbed me of the staff" names the object.

**6. p15: "Because he kept going back." The moral is spoken aloud.**
- **Defect:** The volume mandate says the book's argument is one that "nobody states as a moral." This line is the writer talking through Ao Guang's mouth. It also takes the discovery from p46, where Wukong's own "if a boy can go back for a shoe, I can go back for a monk" is the payoff.
- **Fix:** Cut the line. Give Ao Guang his want instead (in the ledger: never to see the monkey again, polite to the edge of panic): "The boy went back. Every time. Going back is very… admirable. You could go back. Now. Far away." Wukong's "That's a terrible story. I hate it." then responds to the hint, not to a lecture.
- **Optional, nonblocking:** plant the exiled son here. A small empty coral seat beside the throne that Ao Guang glances at would make p28 hit.

**7. Movements V (pp 24–29) and VI (pp 30–36): Wukong's wound and the quarrel go missing.**
- **Defect:** The volume mandate requires that Wukong's wound ("nobody ever gave me anything I didn't take") show at least once per movement, and that "every movement turns on the two of them." Neither happens in V or VI.
  - **V:** Wukong is purely the bodyguard. The architecture's turn ("waiting like he waited") is never rendered. When the dragon says "I've been in this stream for years and years," Wukong says nothing about his five hundred years.
  - **VI:** The monk and the monkey share one exchange ("you won't hurt him?"), and it is a setup, not a conflict.
- **Reader harm:** Two movements in a row play as monster-of-the-week, which is exactly the failure the mandate names. Wukong's arc pauses for thirteen pages.
- **Fix, V (p27 or p28):**
  - After "years and years": "Years? Try five hundred, under a rock. Nobody brought *me* a horse."
  - After Sanzang's "Thank you.", give Wukong the sting: "He eats your horse, and he gets a thank-you."
  - Let Sanzang answer him, for example: "Thank you, Wukong. For coming back." That is a gift that cost Sanzang something to say.
- **Fix, VI:** Combine with Finding 8.

**8. p30 → p36: the locked-up daughter gets no aftermath, and Bajie is never asked about it.**
- **Defect:** "He locked my daughter in the back house." That is a man imprisoning his wife. She is freed on p31, then stands in the background on p36 while the pig is welcomed, named, and gets a laugh line about keeping his bed made. Nobody, not even Sanzang (who won't let a robber be struck), addresses it.
- **Reader harm:** The comedy of the whole movement rests on an unanswered wrong. The reader is left to guess whether the book thinks it doesn't matter. The mandate says a consequential beat without its aftermath is a finding.
- **Fix:** On p36 (or a split p36a, which is cheap), let Wukong be the one who objects. That inverts p13 and gives Movement VI its quarrel and its wound.
  - Wukong: "Master, he locked a girl in a shed, and you're giving him a name?" Sanzang: "I gave you a robe."
  - Bajie turns to the daughter at the gate: "I'm sorry I locked the door. I was afraid you'd run when you saw my real face."
  - She answers silently, inside the ledger's no-balloon rule: she shuts the gate on him, or keeps it open one moment longer. The writer decides. Either is a choice that is hers.

**9. p41: "I can't fight in that soup!" contradicts p25.**
- **Defect:** On p25 Wukong fought the dragon underwater ("Underwater, green and white… bubbles everywhere"). Bajie's retort "You went to the bottom of the sea!" invites the reader to remember p25 too.
- **Fix:** Make the sand the reason. Wukong: "Bring him up, Bajie! It's sand, not water. I can't see my own staff in it!" Bajie: "You fought the dragon in the stream!" Wukong: "That was water! This is soup!"

**10. p42: Bajie acts on information he never received.**
- **Defect:** "She told the horse. She told you." Bajie joined after the dragon's confession, and the horse "never spoke again." Nobody told Bajie the lady sent Wukong; on p35 Wukong answered "Neither" to "Who sent you?"
- **Fix:**
  - Bajie: "The lady in white told me to wait for the pilgrim. Big brother, did she tell you?" Wukong: "Me, and the horse." Bajie: "So what if she told him too?"
  - Or, alternatively, add one sentence to the p37 prose: "On the road Bajie learned that the lady had told all of them to wait, even the horse."

**11. p44: Sanzang's climactic choice is blurred by "where I can hear you."**
- **Defect:** "You'll walk behind me, where I can hear you." Read aloud, a listener asks why "hear." Is it vigilance? Companionship? This is the monk's "brave once," the payoff of p23's "I'll try not to be [frightened]," and the image he chooses doesn't say what's brave about it: turning his back on a monster who ate nine monks.
- **Fix:** "I will. I'll call you Wujing. And you'll walk behind me, where I can't see you, and I won't be afraid." The courage becomes explicit and in Sanzang's plain, stubborn voice. Optionally, give the name's meaning, as Bajie's was given.

## Nonblocking observations

- **Mode balance** is 64 / 15 / 21 across 30 dramatic, 7 prose and 10 spectacle pages. That is within target and meets the mandate's 15% prose floor. Any page added by splitting should not take prose below 7 of 48.
- **Text near or over the dramatic budget:**
  - p2 is about 80 words; p3 is about 89 (lower once Finding 1 is applied); p30 is about 90; p22 is about 81.
  - They are permitted up to about 105 only with large protected balloon lanes, and the prompts must reserve them.
  - If p36 grows under Finding 8, split it rather than squeeze it.
- **p5:** The turn note says Sanzang is "terrified of heights," but nothing on the page says so. Panel 3 could carry it: "I can't even climb a ladder."
- **p10:** One image shows two Wukongs, on the hilltop and racing back. The prose covers it, but tell the builder to make it read as motion, not as two monkeys.
- **p13:** "You didn't have to break him" describes something that didn't happen. "You were going to break his head!" is more exact for the book's central quarrel.
- **p16:** "Guanyin, who had stopped at the mountain" is awkward ("who had come to the monkey's mountain"). Letting her say her name in quoted speech would also cover how Sanzang knows it on p21.
- **p24 vs p39:** Wukong carries the monk under one arm on p24 and later says he can't carry him "one step." A rescue is not travel, and most readers won't object, but a quick clause on p39 ("Snatching you out of a dragon's mouth isn't the road") would close it.
- **p28:** The dragon ate the bay horse "saddle and all," then wears that saddle as the white horse. That's charming fairy-tale logic; fine as it is.
- **p39:** "If I fly you over, you never crossed it" sits close to stating the rule as a moral. It is earned as a reversal of p8 and p3, so keep it, but don't add another line like it anywhere.
- **Strengths to protect in revision:** p6, p9, p17, p18–19, p22 "Yes.", p23, p33–34, p43, p46, and the final "Then he waited."

If all eleven findings are fixed and nothing else regresses, this should be close to approvable. A fresh critic still has to re-audit the whole script next round.
