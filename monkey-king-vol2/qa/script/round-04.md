After that I read `qa/script/audit-r04.txt`, `03-VOLUME-ARCHITECTURE.md` (standing in for the page contract), `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md` and `00-VOLUME-MANDATE.md`. ## Cold read

A monkey's face has been stuck in a mountain for five hundred years, his eyes open, with a peach his great-great-grandchildren swam the sea to bring him. A lady in white, Guanyin, offers him a way out. A monk is walking west to fetch holy books. If the monkey guards him the whole way on foot, the mountain will open, and he won't be the only one she has asked to wait. The monk, Sanzang, is a foundling raised by monks and frightened of nearly everything. His companions turned back at the border. He climbs the mountain anyway, terrified of heights, and peels off the seal. The monkey bursts out, kneels, and asks to fly him west. No, says the monk: every mile has to be his. That night the monk gives him his spare robe and mends it on him. Nobody has ever given Wukong anything.

Robbers attack. Wukong is about to break the leader's head. The monk throws himself across the man and calls Wukong no better than a robber. Wukong leaves, has terrified tea with the Dragon King he once robbed, and gets shown a painting of a boy who keeps going back for an old man's shoe. He hates the story and goes back to the monk. Meanwhile Guanyin has given the monk a cap with a gold band in it and three words. The monk gives Wukong the cap as "a present", then says the words out of fright. The band stays. Wukong storms off to Guanyin ("Yes", she did trick him) and comes back. The monk apologizes, which no one in Heaven ever did.

Then three recruits, each told by the lady to wait. A dragon eats the horse and becomes the horse; he turns out to be the tea-pouring Dragon King's son. A pig courts a "bride" who turns out to be Wukong, goes through the roof, apologizes to the girl he locked up, gets the gate shut on him, and is named Bajie. At an uncrossable river, Wukong refuses to fly the monk over. A river ogre confesses he ate nine monks before, and the monk takes him in anyway. The skulls become a raft. At the fire Wukong finally explains the shoe, and in the morning he waits for them.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p1:** "The hand that beat him." Whose hand? I wondered, and the book never answers.
- **p28:** the bay horse's saddle appears on the white horse, though it was eaten "saddle and all" on pp. 24–25.
- **pp. 40, 45–46:** the word "carry". Wukong says "I can't carry you, not one step of it" while the monk sits on a horse that carries him. Five pages later a prophecy says "what I carry will carry the pilgrim", and a caption says the raft "carried the tenth pilgrim across." I stopped and asked what the rule actually is (Finding 1).

Nothing else stopped me. Every character is named before the plot depends on them, and every returning character is re-identified. I could paraphrase every movement after one read.

## Is it alive?

**Yes, very much.** This is a good book, and I wanted the next page all the way through.

- **Movement I (pp. 1–4): alive.** The p1 image does its job: open eyes and a fresh peach. "If you've come to stare at me, get in line" gives Wukong his voice in one line. He wants out, and Guanyin wants a price he doesn't like. "Walk? Do you know how slow people are?" is the hook, and the book is built on it. The p4 prose ending, "Sanzang was frightened of almost everything. He kept walking," lands well.
- **Movement II (pp. 5–10): alive.** There is real heat in the climb: a coward doing a brave thing for a stranger who might eat him ("Then you'll find out! Climb anyway!"). The p9 robe scene is the emotional root of the book. "Everything I've ever had, I took… Kings get paid" against "Nothing. It's a robe" works because he doesn't know what to do with his hands. The scene plays the wound out instead of stating it.
- **Movement III (pp. 11–17): the best-built movement.** The quarrel is real, and both men are right ("He had a chopper for your head!"). The hurt under the anger on p13 is earned by five hundred years. Ao Guang is a comic gem whose want is plain: get this monkey out of my house. "Going back is very admirable. You could go back. Now. Far away" is subtext played as comedy. "That was different. That was you" is exactly Wukong. "Don't make a speech." / "I wasn't going to." is a fine reunion.
- **Movement IV (pp. 18–23): alive and morally serious.** Sanzang handing over the cap with "It suits you", knowing what it is, is the best subtext in the book. The script lets the monk be wrong on the page (p19), lets Guanyin answer "Yes", and lets the apology cost something ("That was worse"). "You'll have to say them a lot" is a great last line for the movement.
- **Movement V (pp. 24–29): the lightest movement, but it earns its place.** The insult flushes the dragon out, and "you ATE his HORSE" / "Oh no" is the right size for the page. The empty coral seat on p14 pays off quietly ("somebody's son"). The Wukong–Sanzang thread is thinner here: there is "Then leave it be", and "Thank you too, Wukong" on p28. It is enough.
- **Movement VI (pp. 30–37): funny, and it finds its heat at the end.** The bride gag works ("I brought you a peach. I ate most of it on the way"). Then p36 turns it: Wukong objects that the pig locked a girl in a shed, and Sanzang answers "I took you, didn't I? And I gave you a robe." Wukong touches the patch. The daughter shuts the gate herself, so she is an active chooser.
- **Movement VII (pp. 38–48): the climax works.** Sanzang, frightened, asks to be flown over, breaking his own p8 rule. Wukong refuses with the monk's own argument, which is a real reversal. Bajie earns his keep by thinking. The ogre's confession ("Their skulls wouldn't sink, so I wear them") and Wukong's "Master. This is your choice, not mine" pay off the robbers quarrel without anyone stating a moral. "Then he waited." is the right last line.

## Mandatory findings

**1. Page 40 (with the payoff on pp. 45–46): the book's central rule is worded so the climax seems to contradict it.**

- **The defect:** Wukong says, "I can't, Master. I could carry a mountain across. But I can't carry you, not one step of it." The panel stages Sanzang on the white horse, which carries him every day. Then:
  - p45: Wujing says, "One day, what I carry will carry the pilgrim."
  - p46: the caption says the raft "carried the tenth pilgrim across."
- **What the rule really is:** everywhere else the script defines it as no flying.
  - p3: "He goes on the ground… If you fly him there, it's your journey."
  - p8: "on the ground."
  - p40 itself: "If I fly you over, you never crossed it."
- **Reader harm:** this is the line the mandate calls "the book's whole argument", planted in Movement I and paid off here. At that moment a first-time reader sees the forbidden verb ("carry") used for two permitted things within six pages. The natural question is "Why can the horse and the raft carry him but Wukong can't?" The reader then has to work out that "carry" means "fly", which is solving the adaptation at the thesis moment. It also makes the raft look like a loophole rather than the reward of the road. "Recoverable from the next balloon" is not a pass.
- **Fix:** make Wukong's refusal about flying, not carrying, and keep "carry" for the permitted crossings. For example:
  - "I can't, Master. I could fly a mountain across. But I can't fly you over it, not one step of it."
  - Keep: "Because it's your road. If I fly you over, you never crossed it."

  Sanzang's ask can stay as "Carry me across on your cloud", because the cloud is what's being refused. pp. 45–46 can then keep "carry" as written. Do not cut any of the causal words; this is a vocabulary correction, not a compression.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p1, "The hand that beat him became this mountain."** Whose hand is never said. It isn't a causal gap, but it makes a reader ask a question the book never answers. Naming it costs a few words, e.g. "The Buddha's hand that beat him became this mountain", or "The great hand that beat him…" if no name is wanted.
2. **pp. 24, 25, 28, the saddle.** The script stresses "saddle and all" twice (staging and Sanzang's line), then puts "the bay horse's saddle" on the white horse. Readers will notice. The cheapest fix turns it into a joke: one more Wukong line on p28, e.g. "…and he's even wearing your saddle." Alternatively, have the dragon swallow only the horse.
3. **Text density.** p3 (~100 words), p30 (~95) and p44 (~101 words, nine balloons, four in Panel 3) sit near the ~105 dramatic ceiling. They are within budget, but p44 Panel 3 will need protected balloon lanes in the prompt. Restaging so the ogre's last balloon opens the small Panel 4 above Wukong would ease it, without cutting any words.
4. **p28, "Thank you too, Wukong. For coming back."** "Coming back" could mean the robbers day (p17) or the South Sea (p23). Either reading works thematically, so this isn't a blocker.
5. **Five-word audit.** I audited all 43 balloons in `audit-r04.txt`. Each is either a complete thought or an intentional answer to the line just before it, has one visible referent, and is clear without notes. "Yes." (p22), "Not anymore." (p33), "Then take it off." (p22), "It suits you." (p18) and "The tenth what?" (p44) are forceful, not compressed. None fails.
6. **Architecture.** Every page has one mode, one turn, a marked dominant panel, and at most two locations (p30's gate-to-front-room is the entry turn). Mode balance is 31/7/10, about 65/15/21: inside the target and at the mandate's seven-prose floor. There are no chronicle pages, as the mandate specifies.
7. **Ledger against the page.** The voices match what the ledger describes. The mandate requires the "nobody ever gave me anything" wound to show at least once per movement, and it does: pp. 3, 9, 18, 23, 27/28, 36, 47–48. Sanzang is frightened, stubborn, wrong once and brave once, all on the page.

**Summary:** the script is alive, funny and moving. One blocker remains: the word "carry" in the book's central rule at the climax (p40). Fix that one word and it is ready for a fresh audit.
