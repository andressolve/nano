## 1. Blind account (proofs only, 600 × 900, read in order once, story material closed)

**Retell.** The Monkey King has been trapped under a five-ridged mountain for five hundred years. His monkeys swim the sea every spring to bring him a peach. Guanyin comes down on a cloud and offers him freedom: a monk is walking west to fetch holy books, and Wukong must guard him the whole way. There is one rule. The monk walks every mile; Wukong may not fly him. She has asked others to wait for the monk too.

Sanzang (found as a baby in a basket, gentle, stubborn, frightened of almost everything) is left alone at the border when his two companions turn back. Terrified of heights, he climbs the mountain and peels off the gold seal. The mountain bursts with a BOOM and Wukong rises out of it. Wukong offers to somersault him west by supper; Sanzang refuses, because the road must be his. That night Sanzang gives him a spare robe and sews it on him. "Nobody ever gave me anything." "It's a good robe." The road is slow. Robbers attack; Wukong turns his needle into the staff (WHAM) and is about to break the leader's head when Sanzang throws himself over the man. They quarrel ("I can't walk with a robber"), and Wukong flies off hurt.

Under the sea, Ao Guang nervously serves him tea and shows him a painting: an old man drops his shoe off a bridge three times and a boy fetches it three times. "That's a terrible story. I hate it." Wukong goes back. Meanwhile Guanyin gives the monk a cap with a gold band sewn inside and teaches him the words "Gold, hold fast."

Wukong returns ("Don't make a speech"). Sanzang gives him the cap as a present. At the fire, Wukong refuses to promise never to strike a man and swings the staff past the monk's nose. Frightened, Sanzang says the words. The band tightens and the cap tears away, but the band stays. When Wukong raises the staff in rage, the words come again. Wukong flies to Guanyin: "you put another mountain on my head." She replies, "Now he can stop you with three words," and the band comes off only in the west. Back at the fire, Sanzang apologizes for the fear and for the false gift. "Everybody's frightened of me. That's the point of me."

A white dragon swallows the horse. Wukong fights it underwater and then taunts it out with insults about its father. It is Ao Guang's exiled son, also told to wait for the pilgrim. It apologizes and becomes the horse, saddle and all. Sanzang thanks Wukong "for coming back to me." At Gao farm, a pig son-in-law has locked his wife in the back house. Wukong takes the bride's shape ("Not anymore"), shows his monkey face ("Hello, dumpling!"), and chases the pig through the roof. The pig turns out to be another one told to wait for the pilgrim. Sanzang takes him ("I took you, didn't I? And I gave you a robe") and names him Bajie. The daughter shuts the gate on him.

At the Flowing Sand River (eight hundred miles, no feather floats), Sanzang asks to be flown across. Wukong refuses in Sanzang's own words: "If I fly you over, you never crossed it." A red-bearded, skull-necklaced ogre bursts up. Bajie blocks it and fights it underwater. Bajie works out that the ogre was told to wait too and shouts PILGRIM. The ogre confesses that he ate nine monks. Wukong offers to send him back ("Your choice, not mine"). Sanzang takes him and names him Wujing: "at my back, every day. And I won't be afraid of you." The nine skulls become a raft and carry them across. At the fire, Wukong finally explains the shoe: "if a boy can go back for a shoe, I can go back for a monk… You're slower than a shoe." In the morning he runs ahead, looks west, could be there in one somersault, looks back at his slow monk, and waits.

**Cast as I identified it blind:** Sun Wukong; Guanyin; Sanzang; Ao Guang (the Dragon King); the white dragon / white horse (Ao Guang's son); Old Gao and his daughter; the pig, later Bajie; the ogre, later Wujing; the robbers. I misidentified no one and gave no line to the wrong speaker. The bride lines on p31 panel 3 and p33 read as Wukong, because p31 panel 2 shows the transformation and the gold band marks the bride.

**Causal chain:** the offer → the seal → the robe (his first gift) → robbers, a near-killing, and the quarrel → the shoe painting sends him back on his own → the cap is a false gift and its words are used in fear → Guanyin explains → the apology → the dragon, then the pig, then the ogre: each was told to wait, and each is taken in after confessing → the no-flying rule comes back in Wukong's mouth → the raft → the shoe explained → he waits.

**Ending, its cost, and why the final image matters.** The band is still on his head, the west is very far, and he could be there in one somersault. The cost is that the fastest creature in the world has chosen to measure himself by the slowest walker. The last image is the wild king of Movement I standing on a hill and *not leaving*. It answers the shoe parable without saying so.

**Reading pace.** The book never stalled. Every page turn pulled me on. The spectacle pages (7, 12, 20, 24, 34, 41, 46) land as releases, not as padding. The prose pages give the road its length. The cliffhanger on p12 (staff over a downed man) and the quiet beats on p9 panel 3 and pp 23, 29 and 47 were the strongest moments.

3. **Gold band showing before Wukong gets the cap (small-object slip, highest priority of these).** A thin gold band is already on Wukong's brow on p15 (all panels where his head shows), on p18 panel 1 (the beat just before the cap is handed over), and faintly on p14 panels 2–3. It is correctly absent on pp 9–13 and p17. By the script, the band only exists inside the cap (p16 prose, p18 panel 2) and is first fixed on his head on p20. The p16 prose and the p20/21 reveal carry the meaning, so this is not blocking, but it is worth an edit: remove the band from Wukong's brow on p14, p15 and p18 panel 1, leaving his fur bare (on p15 the needle behind his ear can stay).
