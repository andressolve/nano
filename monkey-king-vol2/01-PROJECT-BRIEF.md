# Monkey King, Volume II: The Road West — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, ~47 pages.
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** *Journey to the West* (Xiyouji), attributed to Wu Cheng'en, 16th
century, chapters 8, 13–15, 18–19, and 22, with a sentence each from
chapters 9 and 12. Consulted in the Anthony C. Yu and W. J. F. Jenner
translations as evidence of incident, never for wording. Sequel to
`../monkey-king-vol1/`.

---

## The engine

**The fastest creature in the world is let out from under a mountain on one
condition — walk west, every step, beside a frightened monk he could break in
half — and the hardest thing on the road is not the monsters; it is staying.**

Volume I asked whether anything could stop him. This one asks whether anything
can keep him. People still tell this part of the story because of the odd
couple at its center: the strongest being on the road takes orders from the
weakest, and the weakest is the only one brave enough to give them. The comedy
is the mismatch. The heart is that nobody has ever given Wukong anything he
did not take, and this monk keeps giving him things. Both are protected.

**If the protagonist reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a
stretch, the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.

## The spine

After five hundred years under the mountain, Wukong is offered a way out by
Guanyin, who has been sent to find guardians for a pilgrim: protect a monk on
the road west, on foot, all the way. The monk who climbs up and peels the seal
is young, kind, and terrified of everything, and the first time Wukong saves
his life by nearly killing a man, the monk calls him no better than a robber,
and Wukong leaves. He comes back, of his own choice, after the Dragon King he
once robbed shows him a picture; and the monk, who has been given a golden
band by Guanyin while he was gone, puts it on him. The band teaches Wukong
nothing. What teaches him is that the monk apologizes. On the road after that,
every monster they meet turns out to have been told to wait for this same
pilgrim: a dragon who eats the horse and becomes the horse, a pig who has
married a farmer's daughter, and a river monster wearing the skulls of the
nine pilgrims who came before. At the river of flowing sand Wukong admits the
thing the whole book has been walking toward: he could carry the world across
in one leap, and he cannot carry the monk one step. The last page is four
travelers and a horse walking west, slowly, and the fork it lands on is this:
the band is still on his head, and he is no longer the one who would leave.

## What this volume is

- A complete story: the mountain to the far bank of the river, the company
  gathered. No cliffhanger the reader has to resolve elsewhere.
- A buddy story with a real quarrel: the two leads disagree about the most
  important thing (whether you may hurt someone to protect someone) and each
  is partly right.
- A gathering story. Each recruit is a set piece with its own joke and its
  own wound, and each one joins.
- The three tricks of Volume I, used for new reasons: the cloud to leave and
  to come back; the shape-change to play a bride; the staff, now held back.
- A book where the monk is braver than the monkey once, and the reader sees it.

## What this volume is not

- Not a demon catalogue. Three recruits, one set of robbers, no other monsters.
- Not a book about a saint. Sanzang is frightened, fussy, stubborn, and wrong
  about one thing; he is also the only one who walks every step.
- Not a book about obedience. The band is a mistake two people make, and what
  holds Wukong is not the band.
- Not a travelogue of kingdoms, temples, and abbots. Nine locations.
- Not a recap of Volume I. One prose sentence on what he did; the reader of
  this book alone must still follow every beat.

**Collapses.** Guanyin's disciple Moksa, who ties the skulls into a raft with
her gourd, **merges into Sha Wujing himself**: he makes the raft from his own
necklace, because Guanyin told him that one day what he carried would carry
the pilgrim. The Dragon King of the Western Sea, the white dragon's real father
in the source, **merges into Ao Guang**, so that the dragon in the stream is
the son of the one face the reader already knows, and the Dragon King who sent
Wukong back to the monk turns out to have had a son waiting on the same road.
The old woman on the road who gives Sanzang the cap in the source **is Guanyin
undisguised**. The hunter Liu Boqin, who escorts Sanzang to the mountain,
**is cut to nobody**: Sanzang arrives alone, which is the point.

**Old Ma** died in the years under the mountain (Volume I's last page). She is
not in this book except in one line of Wukong's and in the peach on page 1,
set down by a younger monkey, who is scenery.

**Cut to nobody**: the Buddha, the Jade Emperor, Laozi, Erlang, the Emperor
Taizong of Tang, the Dragon King of the Jing River and his execution, the Ten
Kings of the Underworld, the Chen Guangrui murder story, Sanzang's mother, the
abbot of the Guanyin Temple, the Black Bear Demon, the Crow's Nest Zen
Master, the Yellow Wind Demon, Lingji, Moksa, the Gao daughter's voice (she is
scenery, rescued, no balloons), the six robbers' names, the tiger.

**Source names in these chapters:** roughly thirty. **Locked here: eight.**

## The audience promise

**What these readers already enjoy.** A hero they already love, back. A
grumpy big brother and a new little one. A disguise joke that lasts a whole
scene. Monsters who turn out to be on your side. A fight in a river. A rule
that even the strongest can't break. A magic headband that hurts when the
grown-up says the word.

**The repeatable pleasures.** Each recruit's arrival as a monster and joining
as a friend. Wukong's mouth, now aimed at the monk, who keeps answering
calmly. Pigsy's appetite. The running joke that every monster on the road was
told to wait and has forgotten until somebody says "pilgrim."

**The emotional promise.** The reader knows what it is to be told to stay
with someone slower, weaker, more careful, and more annoying than you. Wukong
leaves. The reader sees why he comes back, and that the band is not why.

**The central dramatic question.** *Will he stay?* The book's answer is yes,
and that he chose to, before the band and after it.

**Complexity budget.** Eight names, five of them new. One realm: the road on
earth, with one dive to the sea floor. One time jump: the five hundred years,
already over on page 1. Zero assumed knowledge: Volume I is told in one prose
sentence on page 1 and one line of dialogue on page 3; a reader who has not
read it must follow everything. No reader needs to know what a bodhisattva, a
scripture, or a pilgrimage is: each is a person with a want or a thing in
somebody's hands.

**What this project refuses to become.** A sermon about obedience. A list of
demons. A Volume I rerun with a monk in it. A book where the monk is right
about everything.

## Length

Working count **47**, reached by cutting: twelve source chapters reduced to
six sequences; three demons (Black Bear, Yellow Wind, the Gao cave fight) cut
outright; the entire Tang court frame (ch 9–12) reduced to one prose page. The
count follows the script and will not shrink by compression. If it grows past
the low fifties, the arc boundary for a split is after Movement IV, where the
band goes on, and the owner decides.

## The reader

A first-time reader who will never read the source. They must never have to
solve the adaptation, supply a missing causal step, or recognize a character
the book has not introduced. "Recoverable," "eventually clear," and
"understandable after looking back" are revision flags, not passing grades.
