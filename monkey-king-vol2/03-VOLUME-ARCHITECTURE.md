# Volume Architecture — 48 pages

*(Drafted at 47. At script round 1 page 36 was split into 36–37 — Wukong objects to taking the pig, the pig apologizes to the girl, she shuts the gate on him — and former 37–47 became 38–48. Movement VII's table below carries the new numbers.)*

Mythic China, five hundred years after Volume I. One realm: the road west
from the Mountain of Five Elements, with one dive to the floor of the Eastern
Sea and one cloud above the South Sea.

---

## I — FIVE HUNDRED YEARS · pp 1–4

**Emotional entry:** stillness. **Exit:** a young monk alone at the foot of
a mountain he has been told to cross, and a voice from the rock.

Spring on the Mountain of Five Elements. A young monkey climbs up with a peach,
as monkeys have every year, and sets it down by the face in the rock. Then a
woman in white on a cloud: Guanyin. Wukong is rude to her from habit. She
offers him the way out: a monk is coming from the east, sent to fetch
scriptures from the far west. Walk with him and guard him, all the way, on
foot. Wukong says yes before she finishes, and then asks why on foot when he
could put the monk there before lunch. Because then it would not be the monk's
journey, she says. He promises. She tells him he is not the only one she has
asked to wait. Then the monk: found as a baby in a basket on a river, raised in
a monastery, chosen by an emperor, sent west with two companions and a horse;
the companions turn back at the border. He arrives at the mountain alone.

**The turn:** the creature who could not be held is offered a way out, and
the price is the slowest thing in the world.
**Page-turn out:** a voice in the mountain shouts "Master!"

## II — THE SEAL · pp 5–10

**Emotional entry:** fear. **Exit:** a monkey in a monk's spare robe running
far ahead on the road and running back.

Sanzang hears the voice and finds the face. Wukong begs, flatters, and orders
him up the mountain to the golden seal. The monk is afraid of heights and of
demons and climbs anyway, slowly, and peels it off. The mountain cracks and
the monkey comes out, filthy, golden-eyed, in the rotten rags of Heaven's
armor, and the monk falls on his back. Wukong kneels: "Master." The first
night, Sanzang gives him his own spare robe, and mends it on him, and Wukong
does not know what to do with his hands. In the morning they start walking,
and Wukong discovers how slow a man is.

**The turn:** he is free because someone weaker than him chose to climb, and
the first thing the someone does is give him something.
**Page-turn out:** the road has people on it.

## III — THE ROBBERS AND THE SHOE · pp 11–17

**Emotional entry:** swagger. **Exit:** a monkey dropping out of the sky
onto the road, landing in front of the monk, pretending he never left.

Six robbers. Wukong takes the needle from behind his ear and in one page there
are six men on the ground. He lifts the staff over the leader, and Sanzang
throws himself across the man. The quarrel: you were going to kill him; he
was going to kill you; then you are a robber too. Wukong, who spent five
hundred years under a rock for this man, somersaults away. He drops into the
Dragon King's palace under the Eastern Sea, the one he robbed of the staff,
and Ao Guang, horrified, gives him tea. On the wall is a painting: an old man
on a bridge who drops his shoe three times, and a boy who fetches it three
times, and becomes great. Wukong understands it immediately and hates it.
Meanwhile, on the road, Guanyin comes to the weeping monk and gives him a cap
with a band of gold in it, and three words, and tells him they are for the
day he needs them. Wukong comes back.

**The turn:** the first time in his life he goes back to someone, it is his
own choice.
**Page-turn out:** the monk has a present for him, and the reader knows
what it is.

## IV — THE BAND · pp 18–23

**Emotional entry:** gladness. **Exit:** two travelers by one fire, the
gold band catching the light, both of them awake.

Sanzang offers the cap. Wukong, who has been given one thing in his life, puts
on the second without asking. That evening the quarrel comes back: the monk
asks him to promise he will never strike a man again, and Wukong laughs, and
the needle is in his hand to show what he thinks of the promise, and the monk,
panicking, says the words. The band tightens. Wukong rolls in the road and the
cap tears away and the band stays. He goes for the monk with the staff; the
words again; the staff drops. He knows whose band this is and leaps to the
South Sea and Guanyin: you tricked me. She does not deny it. She says the band
was for the day he left again, and that it comes off at the end of the road.
He comes back again. And at the fire, the monk does what nobody in Heaven
ever did: he says he is sorry. He was frightened. He promises to say the words
only if he must. Wukong says he'll have to, a lot.

**The turn:** the man holding the leash is more frightened than the monkey
wearing it, and says so.
**Page-turn out:** a horse, a stream, a splash.

## V — THE DRAGON IN THE STREAM · pp 24–29

**Emotional entry:** irritation. **Exit:** the monk riding a white horse
nobody bought, and the horse looking back at the water.

At Eagle Grief Stream a white dragon bursts out of the water and swallows the
monk's horse, saddle and all, and is gone. Wukong is outraged on the monk's
behalf. He dives; the dragon fights and hides; he shouts it out of the water by
insulting its mother; he pins it. The dragon gasps that it has been waiting for
a pilgrim, as the lady told it, and Wukong points out that it has just eaten
the pilgrim's horse. The dragon says its father is the Dragon King of the
Eastern Sea, that it burned its father's pearl and was sent to this stream to
wait. Wukong has drunk tea with its father. The lady said, when the pilgrim
comes, carry him. The dragon becomes a white horse, saddle and all.

**The turn:** the monster is somebody's son, waiting like he waited.
**Page-turn out:** a village where every door is shut at dusk.

## VI — THE BRIDE OF GAO VILLAGE · pp 30–37

**Emotional entry:** mischief. **Exit:** a pig in a monk's procession with
all the luggage, looking back at the village and its kitchens.

Gao Village is shuttered at dusk. Old Gao, the farmer, wants a demon-catcher:
three years ago a strong young man married his youngest daughter and worked
like ten men, then turned back into what he was, a pig with a rake, and ate
the village bare, and locked the girl in the back house when they asked him to
go. Wukong lets the girl out and turns himself into her. In the dark of the
back house, the pig arrives, tender and greedy and vain, and the "bride" is
cold to him, and tells him her father has hired the Great Sage Equal to
Heaven. The pig is terrified of that name. The bride turns into a monkey. The
pig goes through the roof. The chase ends at his cave mouth: who are you
working for? A monk going west. The pig drops his rake. *The pilgrim?* The
lady told him to wait. He forgot. He comes back to kneel to the monk, asks
Old Gao to keep his bed made, and is given a name: Bajie.

**The turn:** the monster was waiting for them too, and now Wukong is the
big brother.
**Page-turn out:** a river with no other side.

## VII — THE RIVER OF FLOWING SAND · pp 38–48

**Emotional entry:** weariness. **Exit:** four travelers and a white horse
on a road going west, the slowest one in front.

Weeks of road with a pig who complains, and Wukong is no longer the newest or
the worst. Then a river eight hundred miles wide, the color of sand, that
will not float a feather. Sanzang asks: carry me over on your cloud. And
Wukong says the truth: he cannot. He could carry a mountain; he cannot carry
the monk a single step of the road; the road will not let him; every step
has to be the monk's. Out of the water comes a monster with a crescent spade
and a necklace of nine skulls and grabs for the monk; Bajie blocks; the fight
goes into the river, where the pig is better than the monkey. The monster
will not come up. It is Bajie who thinks of it: he stands on the bank and
shouts PILGRIM. A head rises. The monster tells the tenth pilgrim the truth:
nine came before, and he ate them, and wears them because they would not sink.
And the lady told him that one day what he carried would carry the pilgrim.
The monk takes him. The skulls go onto the water and become a raft. They
cross. On the far bank, at the fire, Sanzang asks Wukong why he came back
after the robbers. Wukong answers. In the morning they walk.

**The turn:** the road cannot be skipped, only walked, and now there are
four of them to walk it.
**Page-turn out:** (none: the book ends.) The road goes on west.

**No movement runs more than five dramatic pages without a prose or spectacle
page.** Longest runs: 25–28 (four), 42–45 (four), 35–37 (three), 13–15 and 17–19 (three
each, broken by the monk's prose page 16, which also gives his loneliness its
own duration). The prose and spectacle pages are what buy the dramatic pages
their room; they cannot be cut for budget.

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
The release (p 7) and the kneeling (p 8) are separate pages. The quarrel
(p 13) leaves before the sea (p 14). The band (p 20) gets its rage (p 21),
its confrontation (p 22), and its apology (p 23) on three pages, not one. The
confession (p 44) and the acceptance (p 45) are separate; the crossing (p 46)
is its own page before the fire (p 47).

**One dominant turn per page.** Every row above names the one thing that
changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence comes back here,
not to the prompt.
