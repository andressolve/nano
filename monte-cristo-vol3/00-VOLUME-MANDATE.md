## The adaptation stance — say it first

> This volume is an adaptation. Fidelity to the source is not a goal, not a
> tiebreaker, and not a defense. Every element earns its place by what it does
> for this reader in this book, or it is cut.

Dumas spreads Villefort's fall across four hundred pages, braided with three
other households, a false Italian nobility, a jewel robbery, a forged
marriage, a telegraph fraud and a dozen servants. Almost all of it goes. What
stays is the one thing that has kept this half of the novel alive: **a man
who buries whatever threatens him — an innocent sailor, a newborn child, a
poisoner in his own house — and a man he buried who has come back to dig it
all up.**

## Volume constraints

- **Deaths are real and never drawn.** Two people die in this house by
  poison: Valentine's grandmother, and at the end Madame de Villefort and her
  son. No body, wound, convulsion or face in death is drawn for the boy at
  all; his death is a closed door, a tin hussar on the floor, and the faces
  of the men in the corridor. Madame de Villefort may be shown fallen on the
  stair, eyes closed, with dignity. The grandmother is a still shape in a
  curtained bed. The cost is shown in the living.
- **Poison is never a recipe.** The vial is a red liquid in cut crystal.
  "One drop gives back a life; ten take one" is the whole pharmacology. No
  plant names, no doses, no preparation.
- **The buried child is told, never shown.** The night of 1817 exists only in
  words — the Count's, Villefort's, Benedetto's. No infant, no box in the
  ground, no burial is drawn. The empty hole under the tree is drawn.
- **Noirtier speaks only with his eyes.** He has no balloons. His answers are
  drawn — one long blink for yes, many quick blinks for no — and spoken aloud
  by whoever is asking ("Yes." / "No."). When he spells, the asker recites the
  alphabet and the letters he chooses are spoken by the asker. Every page with
  Noirtier makes the method readable without notes.
- **The Count's reserved black.** Unrelieved black on a tall columnar figure
  with swept-back black hair and pallor belongs to the Count alone. Villefort
  also wears black; he is separated by his grey hair, his narrow inverted
  triangle and his rigid high collar, and by his magistrate's robe on the trial
  pages. **No extra wears unrelieved black** on any page the Count is on.
- **Names cost.** Eight people have names. The boy, the grandmother, the
  doctor who does not appear, the police, the gaolers, the court, the card
  players, the workmen, and Danglars (mentioned once, never drawn) are
  scenery, unnamed or unlocked, and have no balloons.

## The specific failure this volume must avoid

**The serene avenger, and the soap opera.**

The first tempting book is the noble Count of the films: a calm, wise,
slightly sad man setting things right. Volume II spent fifty pages proving he
is not that — he is hungry, he plays with his food, and he arms people. In
this volume he **hands a poison to a woman he knows will use it**, and he
does it on the page, with pleasure. If he reads as merely clever, or merely
just, for more than a page at a stretch, the engine has been written out
(script-gate blocker). His appetite must survive the discovery that the girl
in danger is loved by the son of the man he once saved; it bends, it does not
break, until the last movement.

The second tempting book is the household melodrama: weddings arranged,
wills read, notaries, inheritances explained, servants who carry tea and
news. **Logistics is never the drama.** Who inherits what is said once, in
one character's mouth, as a want ("Valentine is worth a million now. My son
is worth nothing."). No notary, no will, no marriage contract appears.

The third is **Villefort as a cardboard monster.** He must be frightening
and pitiable at once: brilliant in court, cold to his daughter, adoring of
his small son, and — every time — choosing to bury rather than to know. The
reader should understand him completely by the time the ground gives way
under him.
