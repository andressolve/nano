# The Count of Monte Cristo, Volume III: The King's Attorney — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, 56 pages.
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** Alexandre Dumas, *Le Comte de Monte-Cristo* (1844–46) — the
Villefort material. **Parent:** `~/Documents/nano/monte-cristo-vol2/`
(Volume II, shipped); grandparent `~/Documents/nano/monte-cristo-expanded/`
(Volume I, shipped).

---

## The engine

**The man Gérard de Villefort buried alive comes back to dig up everything
else the King's Attorney ever buried — and enjoys every spadeful, until the
poison he handed out with his own hand reaches the people who did nothing.**

Protected absolutely. Everything else is held loosely.

The operative words are still **enjoys** and now **his own hand**. Volume II
proved the Count is hungry. Volume III makes him an accomplice: he gives a
poison to a woman he knows is a poisoner, because the King's Attorney's house
deserves one. The reader should be thrilled by how clever and how cruel he is
right up to the moment the glass is in Valentine's hand — and then keep
reading because he does not stop.

**If the Count reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a
stretch, the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.

## The spine

Volume I ended on **mercy**; Volume II on **judgment and its price** (Morcerf
dead, Mercédès gone, "One."). Volume III is **judgment that cannot choose
its victims.**

Villefort is the worst of the three men: the only one who *knew* Edmond was
innocent. His whole life is one move repeated — when something threatens his
name, he buries it: a sailor in 1815, a newborn in a garden at Auteuil in
1817, and now a poisoner in his own house. The Count digs each one up — the
child is alive and grown and a murderer; the poisoner is Villefort's wife,
armed by the Count; and the girl the poison finds is Valentine, Villefort's
daughter, who is loved by Maximilien Morrel, the son of the man the Count
saved in Volume I.

The Count saves Valentine by stealing her death — she is buried, and he lifts
her out, the one thing he digs up that he wanted to save. He destroys
Villefort in open court through the mouth of the son Villefort buried. And
the bill arrives the same afternoon: Madame de Villefort takes the poison,
and takes her son with her. The Count stands in a corridor with his vial in
his hand and nothing in it that can help.

The last fork: on the night after, he tries to say **"Two."** — and cannot.

## What this volume is

- One kill, Villefort, fully dramatized: brilliant, cold, almost sympathetic,
  and destroyed by the thing he buried.
- A thriller: a poisoner loose in a house, a girl in danger, a hidden door in
  a wall, a false death, a tomb opened at night, a trial where the prisoner
  names the prosecutor as his father.
- A love story the Count did not plan for: Maximilien and Valentine at an
  iron garden gate.
- The volume where the Count's appetite costs a child's life and he knows it.

## What this volume is not

- Not Danglars' ruin (Volume IV). Danglars is mentioned once, as a plant.
- Not Haydée's ending (Volume IV). She is the confidant and the nurse; her
  feeling for the Count is planted, never spent.
- Not the renunciation (Volume IV). The Count's doubt is planted — he cannot
  say "Two" — and nothing more.
- Not Valentine's return to Maximilien (Volume IV). The reader knows she
  lives; Maximilien does not; the Count asks him to wait one month.
- Not a household drama of wills, notaries and marriage contracts.
- Not an ensemble. Eight faces, hard ceiling.

## Cast ceiling — 8. Hard.

The Count · Villefort · Valentine · Maximilien Morrel · Noirtier · Madame de
Villefort (Héloïse) · Benedetto · Haydée.

Plus **one unnamed recurring figure with a sheet and no balloons**:
Villefort's son, about eight.

Collapses, structural rather than cosmetic:

**Bertuccio + Caderousse + the Busoni confession → the Count himself.** In
Dumas a Corsican steward witnessed the burial, dug up the child and raised
him, and confessed it all to Abbé Busoni. Here the confession is told in one
line — "Years later he confessed it to a priest. I was the priest." — and the
witness never appears. That deletes the vendetta, the steward, the
foster-mother, and Caderousse's whole Paris career.

**Doctor d'Avrigny → Villefort.** The King's Attorney has prosecuted
poisoners; he recognises the signs himself at his mother-in-law's bedside.
Making Villefort the one who *knows* and chooses not to ask is the volume's
best characterization of him, and it removes a doctor who would need lines.

**Barrois, the Saint-Mérans, Franz d'Épinay, the notary, the will →
Valentine's grandmother (one page, no lines) and one line of want.** The
grandmother arrives, dies, and Valentine inherits; that is all the reader
needs.

**The Cavalcanti father, Danglars' daughter, the engagement, the
Caderousse murder → the Count's own anonymous letter.** Benedetto is a
convict who killed a guard escaping the galleys at Toulon. The Count dresses
him as "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti", sits him at Villefort's table, and then
tells the police who he is. Villefort, humiliated, insists on prosecuting him
himself.

**Ali, Baptistin, Bertuccio and every servant → nobody.** Haydée remains the
confidant, as in Volume II.

## The audience promise

Francisco and Sebastian have read Volumes I and II. They know Edmond Dantès,
the Château d'If, Faria, the treasure, the three men, the red purse, and the
Count's first kill. They loved being on the side of a brilliant, dangerous
man. This volume promises them:

- **A mystery they are let in on.** The reader knows from page 3 that the
  dashing "prince" is Villefort's buried son, and from page 8 that the vial
  in Madame de Villefort's hand came from the Count. Every dinner, every
  handshake, every glass of water is dramatic irony.
- **Set pieces:** runaway horses on a hill; a paralysed man who can only
  speak with his eyes and is the cleverest person in the house; a door cut
  through a wall; a poisoner caught in the act by a man standing in the dark;
  a false death; a tomb opened in the rain; a courtroom detonation.
- **A love story worth rooting for** — and a reason to fear for it.
- **The bill.** The Count is still the hero they are reading for, and he is
  wrong, and he finds out.

Complexity budget: eight names (four returning: the Count, Villefort,
Haydée, Noirtier; four new: Valentine, Maximilien, Madame de Villefort,
Benedetto); one alias (Prince Cavalcanti, which is Benedetto's disguise and
the Count's tool); one remembered alias (Abbé Busoni); three houses (the
Count's, Villefort's, the house at Auteuil) plus the court and the cemetery;
no time jumps except the told past. **Every returning face and debt is
re-introduced in story, never assumed.**

## Length

**56 pages**, six movements. Volume II was 49 for one target; this volume
runs two threads (the poison and the buried son) into one detonation, and
every major beat gets its aftermath page: the grandmother's death, Valentine's
false death, the trial, and the boy. Reached by cutting the Cavalcanti
nobility, the Danglars engagement, Caderousse, Bertuccio, the will, the
notary and the doctor, never by compressing.

## The reader

A first-time reader who has read Volumes I and II and will never read Dumas.
They must never have to solve the adaptation, supply a missing causal step,
or recognize a character the book has not introduced. "Recoverable,"
"eventually clear," and "understandable after looking back" are revision
flags, not passing grades.
