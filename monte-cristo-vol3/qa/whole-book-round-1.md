# Whole-book gate: *The Count of Monte Cristo, Volume III: The King's Attorney*, pages 1–56

## 1. Blind account (written before I opened the script or the contract)

**Reading-pace record.** I read all 56 pages once, in order, at 600 × 900, with all story material closed. Nothing stalled me long enough to look back. Pages 3, 12, 31 and 53 are the densest. The prose pages work as breaths between the dialogue runs: 10, 14, 18, 23, 27, 31, 35, 39, 47 and 53. The silent and near-silent pages land as the big beats: 7 (the carriage), 22 (the empty hole), 32 (the wardrobe), 38 (out of the tomb), 42 (the packed court), 49 (the toy at the door) and 56 (the hand). The fastest run is 40–52, where every page turn pulls.

**Retell.** Twenty-three years ago three men sent Edmond Dantès to the Château d'If. He came out as the Count of Monte Cristo with a fortune and a list. Fernand is already dead. Now he wants Villefort, the King's Attorney, who knew Edmond was innocent.

- **The secret.** At the house he has bought in Auteuil, the Count shows Haydée the bare ground under a great plane tree. Twenty-one years ago Villefort buried a newborn there, his child by a woman who was not his wife. A man who hated Villefort dug it up alive and later confessed this to "the Abbé Busoni", who was the Count in disguise. The child is Benedetto: thief, liar, galley escapee, murderer. The Count finds him in a cellar, makes him "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti", and promises him a rich father's name.
- **The poison.** The Count meets Villefort in court; Villefort has no idea who he is. The Count sells Madame de Villefort's husband half-broken horses and points them down his own hill. He stops the runaway carriage himself and revives her limp little son with one drop from a red vial. Then he tells her ten drops would stop a heart without trace, and lets her keep it. Haydée sees exactly what he has done.
- **Inside the house.** Villefort ignores his daughter Valentine and dotes on his son. The Count asks to meet the paralysed grandfather, Noirtier, who answers with his eyes: one slow close for yes, many blinks for no. Alone with him, the Count says Villefort burned Noirtier's letter and jailed the sailor who carried it, and that he was that sailor. Noirtier does not want him to stop, but asks about Valentine. The Count gives his word she has nothing to fear from him.
- **The complication.** At the Morrels' house, where the Count is loved and unknown, Maximilien confesses that he loves Valentine de Villefort. Haydée urges the Count to take the bottle back. He refuses because he wants to hear Villefort's house fall, and promises that Valentine walks out alive. Haydée answers: "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage."
- **Dinner at Auteuil.** Villefort shakes his unknown son's hand and calls the boy's father a fool. The Count tells a "ghost story" under the tree. At two in the morning Villefort digs and finds the hole empty while the Count watches from a window.
- **The first death.** Héloïse poisons Valentine's grandmother's milk in front of Noirtier, and Valentine inherits. Villefort guesses there is a poisoner and refuses to learn who. Héloïse all but tells the Count that Valentine is next. He lets it ride, bets she will wait, and writes the anonymous letter that gets Benedetto arrested on the Opéra steps. Villefort will prosecute him and ask for his head.
- **Valentine.** She drinks poisoned chocolate and survives only because Noirtier's medicine has been dosing her. The Count admits to Noirtier that the vial was his. He cuts a door through the wall into Valentine's wardrobe, watches Héloïse pour drops into her glass, and gives Valentine a death-like draught. The house mourns her. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE to Villefort. At the tomb Maximilien accuses the Count of breaking his word. That night the Count carries Valentine out, but she does not wake.
- **The trial.** The Count gives Benedetto his father's name and his birth date. That morning Villefort tells his wife to take her own poison before he comes home, and kisses his son goodbye. In court Benedetto gives his name, his birth date and Auteuil, then names his father: "my father is you." Villefort blurts "It wasn't breathing", then admits everything. The Count has the face he waited for.
- **The bill.** Villefort races home, remembering that his son is with his wife. Héloïse has drunk the vial and taken the boy with her. Over her body the Count tells Villefort, "I am Edmond Dantès." He comes out of the boy's room with the empty vial: "He's dead." Villefort's mind breaks, and he digs in his own frozen garden for a buried son. The Count picks up the tin soldier.
- **The cost.** In the snow the Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine is alive but asleep, and Noirtier says yes, he wishes he had stopped him. Maximilien comes to say goodbye with his father's pistol case; the Count buys one month. On the fourth night Valentine's hand closes around Haydée's.

**Causal chain.** The buried baby lived → the Count builds a prince → he arms the mother with the vial → he gets into the house → he gives Noirtier his word about Valentine → Maximilien's love raises the stakes → Haydée asks him to take the bottle back and he refuses → the tree story and the empty hole → the grandmother dies → Villefort chooses not to know → the Count bets she will wait and writes to the police → Benedetto is arrested → Valentine is poisoned and survives → the wardrobe → the false death → HÉLOÏSE is spelled → the tomb → Villefort's order to his wife → the trial confession → wife and son dead by the Count's own vial → "I am Edmond Dantès" → Villefort mad → the Count cannot say "Two" → Noirtier's "yes" → one month for Maximilien → Valentine's hand closes.

**Ending: what it cost and why the last image matters.** The revenge worked exactly as designed, and it cost a boy who had done nothing, by the Count's own vial, in the house where he swore he would see everything. The last image is Valentine's hand closing around Haydée's while the Count stands in the doorway holding the dead boy's soldier and does not come in. One life comes back from his poison and one does not. The man who caused both cannot step into the room. It is earned and it lands.

## 3. Acceptance questions by movement

**I (pp 1–8).** The Count wants Villefort destroyed through what Villefort buried. He chooses to make the son a prince and to hand the mother a poison. His reason is shown, not told: he smiles at the hole and watches her face. Benedetto, Héloïse and the boy are affected. He now believes the plan is in his hands. We move on because the poison is in her reticule. Nothing depends on outside information: page 1's prose and page 3's Busoni line give everything needed.

**II (pp 9–18).** He wants entry to the house and the old man's complicity. He chooses to tell Noirtier he is the sailor, and to keep the bottle in play despite Haydée. His reason: "I want to hear it fall." Valentine is put at risk by his word. He believes he will see every road of the house, and page 18 says so outright. We move on because of the invitations.

**III (pp 19–27).** Villefort wants silence; Héloïse wants the money; the Count wants the fall. Villefort chooses not to know. The Count chooses to wait and to write to the police. His reason is page 26: to watch Villefort do it once more. Those affected are the grandmother, who dies, and Valentine, who is now the target. The Count still believes he controls it. We move on because Valentine is still in the house.

**IV (pp 28–39).** He wants to save Valentine without giving up the fall. He chooses the false death, and lets Maximilien grieve. His reason is in the page 39 prose: "a hope I may have to take back." Those affected are Valentine, Maximilien, Noirtier and Villefort through HÉLOÏSE. He is smiling about tomorrow. We move on to the trial.

**V (pp 40–47).** Benedetto wants fame. Villefort wants his name clean, and then wants to survive. The Count wants that face. Benedetto chooses to name his father. Villefort chooses to confess, and before the trial chose to order his wife to die. Everyone in the court sees Villefort, and the boy is left in the house. The Count has what he came for. We move on because Villefort realises his son is with his wife.

**VI (pp 48–56).** It turns on the cost. He reveals "I am Edmond Dantès," cannot say "Two," and asks Noirtier whether he wishes he had stopped. Noirtier's answer is yes. He now doubts God is on his side. We move on to Maximilien's month and Valentine's hand.

Every movement passes on the page alone.

## 5. Nonblocking observations (each fixable by in-generator edit)

**Typos and wrong letters**

- **p1, title card:** rendered "Volume Iii · The King's Attorney". Scripted "VOLUME III · THE KING'S ATTORNEY". The numeral reads as the word "Iii".
- **p26, panel 2, second Count balloon:** rendered "…I wanted to watch him do it once more, with iis eyes open." Scripted "…with his eyes open."

**Spacing slips** (stray double spaces)

- **p34, panel 2, second Count balloon:** rendered "And  I will bring you out." Scripted "And I will bring you out."
- **p53, prose field 1:** rendered "On the  night Fernand died". Scripted "On the night Fernand died".
- **p46, panel 2, Villefort balloon:** rendered "It is true.  Everything he said is true." Scripted "It is true. Everything he said is true."

**Punctuation style.** Several pages mix straight apostrophes into otherwise curly lettering: p23 "Noirtier's", p27 "Count's", p40 "trial's", p47 "God's". These are typographic only, with no change in reading.

**Small-object drift: the tin hussar.** The script fixes the boy's toy as a tin hussar on a rearing horse, red and gold. That is how it renders on p10, p25 and p49. On p8 (panels 3–4), p41 (panels 1, 3 and 4), p50 (panels 2 and 4), p51 (panels 1 and 4) and p56 (panel 1) it is a standing, unmounted red soldier. The sharpest jump is p49 to p51: mounted outside the door, then picked up unmounted. Readers will still take it as "the boy's toy soldier", and the p53 prose calls it "a tin soldier". If edits are being made anyway, restoring the horse on p51 panel 4 and p56 panel 1 would tighten the motif.

**Other observations (no reading harm)**

- **p39:** the prose says Haydée found him "at the window, smiling". The rendered Count at the window is grave rather than visibly smiling.
- **p53:** the prose callback "looked across the roofs at this very window" leans on Volume II. It is self-explaining enough, because the "One." and the list carry it.
- **Season continuity:** snow shows in the windows on p24 and p41, but autumn leaves appear outside on p28. This is cosmetic.
- **Valentine's hair:** it reads redder and looser in the bed scenes (p33–35, 38, 39, 56) than the light chestnut knot of p10–11, p19 and p28. She is never misread, because costume and context hold her.
