VERDICT: APPROVED

## Cold read

I read all 56 pages in order before opening any other document.

**The story as a first-time reader would tell it.** The Count of Monte Cristo has already finished the first man on his list, Fernand. Now he stands under a plane tree in a garden at Auteuil that he has bought. He tells Haydée why. Twenty-one years ago Villefort, the King's Attorney and the one man who knew Edmond was innocent, buried his own newborn bastard child here without stopping to listen. A hidden enemy dug the baby up alive, and years later confessed it to the "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count in disguise.

- **Arming the house.** The child is now Benedetto, a card-sharp who killed a guard escaping the galleys. The Count dresses him as "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti". He takes Villefort's measure in court. Then he sends half-broken horses down his own hill with Madame de Villefort and her small son in the carriage. He saves the boy with one red drop, and hands the vial to a woman whose face tells him exactly what she will do with it.
- **Inside Villefort's house.** He finds a cold father, a loved son and an ignored daughter, Valentine. He tells the paralysed grandfather, Noirtier, that he was the sailor. Noirtier does not want him to stop. The Count swears Valentine has nothing to fear from him, then learns that Maximilien Morrel, the son of the man he once saved, loves her. Haydée asks him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall."
- **The dinner and the first death.** At dinner Villefort shakes his own son's hand. Under the tree the Count tells a ghost story about a buried child. At two in the morning Villefort digs and finds the hole empty. Valentine's grandmother is poisoned in front of Noirtier. Villefort works it out and chooses not to know who did it. Héloïse all but tells the Count that Valentine is next. The Count bets that she will wait, and denounces Benedetto to the police.
- **Valentine.** She is poisoned and lives, because Noirtier had been dosing her with his own medicine. The Count cuts a door through into her wardrobe and watches Héloïse pour the drops. He fakes Valentine's death, stands at the tomb while Maximilien says "You promised me", and lifts her out. She does not wake.
- **The trial and the bill.** In court Benedetto names his father. Villefort confesses. He races home to the wife he told to die that morning. She has taken the boy with her. The Count says "I am Edmond Dantès" in the corridor and comes out of the boy's room with the empty vial. Villefort digs in the wrong garden. The Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine lives, and Noirtier wishes he had stopped. He asks Maximilien for one month. Valentine's hand closes.

I could say all of that after one read without looking back. That is the paraphrase test, passed.

**Where I stopped.**

- **p30.** For a beat I asked how Noirtier knew his own medicine would protect her against the stepmother's drops. "Grandfather keeps looking at me as if he's proud of me" and the Count's "A little every day, so that a big dose can't kill her" settle it as Noirtier's deliberate act. Since p23 he has seen the red drops with his own eyes. I did not need to look back.
- **p54.** I briefly asked why the Count tells Noirtier but, a page later, still not Maximilien, who is holding a pistol case. p39 gave the reason ("I won't give him a hope I may have to take back"). "You have kept my name since the day I told you. Keep this one too" says why Noirtier is different: he can keep a secret and cannot act on it. The asymmetry reads as character, and the pistol-case page plays on it.
- **p41.** Villefort's "three days" against the night count on p39. By p39's count the gap from the spelling to the trial is closer to three and a half days. A man's approximation does no harm to the reader.
- **Nothing else.** No other stop, reread or guess. No transition made me reorient: every jump in place has a caption, a prose bridge, or an opening line that places it (p9 "Those horses came from your stables"; p14 "There was one house in Paris…"; p54 "Then he went back into the house.").

## Is it alive?

**Movement I (pp 1–8): yes, completely.** This is an opening with teeth.

- "And you're smiling. Who was he?" Haydée names the engine in five words.
- The tavern pair (pp 4–5) is the funniest and most dangerous scene in the movement. Benedetto's voice is entirely his own ("my lord", "Rich how rich?", "You do have a sense of humour"). The Count plays with him the way he plays with everyone.
- On p6, "I have never once let a guilty man go free." / "No. I don't imagine you have." is dramatic irony a ten-year-old will feel in his stomach.
- p8 is the book's thesis enacted, not said. "And if someone gave more than one drop?" is her want in her own mouth. "Keep it, madame. You may need it one day." is his appetite in his.

**Movement II (pp 9–18): yes.**

- p9 has the heat: she accuses, he owns it, and "That is Villefort's problem." shuts like a door.
- The Noirtier pages are as good as anything in the series. A man who can only blink is the most alive face in the house. "I was the sailor." lands because of whom he is telling.
- p16 is the first time the pleasure leaves the Count's face. It works because fifteen pages have shown how much pleasure there was.
- p17 is the best Haydée scene in the series so far. "I want to hear it fall, Haydée. Every floor of it." / "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage." She wins the exchange and he has no answer. That is exactly right.

**Movement III (pp 19–27): yes.**

- Valentine at the gate is warm and funny ("pretending to look at snails"), and it buys her death its weight later.
- "To fathers." Two words, and the whole dinner table is a trap.
- The empty hole with the Count's face at the window is the kind of silent page these boys stop on.
- p24, "Stop. Don't tell me.", is Villefort understood completely in one gesture: the mandate's third trap avoided.
- On p25 Héloïse says the worst thing in the book most sweetly. The Count's silence, then "You are careful with it, I hope?", is complicity with a smile.
- On p26 he answers Haydée from appetite ("I wanted to watch him do it once more, with his eyes open") and makes a bet with someone else's life.

**Movement IV (pp 28–39): yes, and this is where I was most gripped.**

- The cost of the bet is enacted three times. His hand closes at "should be dead" (p29). He confesses the bottle to the one man who trusted him ("I know because I gave it to her. And I will still have your son."). p31 states plainly, "she had not waited a week."
- The wardrobe page is a set piece.
- On p34, "Your father will bury you, the way he buries everything", said with the corner of his mouth lifting, keeps the appetite alive while he saves her.
- Valentine stays an active chooser: "He does. So I will."
- I felt p37.

**Movement V (pp 40–47): yes.**

- Benedetto's cell scene is gleeful ("My lord, I wouldn't miss it.").
- p41 gives Villefort his one gentle line, "Be good for your mother. I'll be home tonight.", in the same scene where he orders his wife to die. It is the cruellest and best-built page in the movement.
- The trial rises one question at a time. "You didn't look." collects p2's "He didn't stop to listen."
- On p47, Villefort's realisation in the carriage ("And then he remembered that his son was in the house with her") is the right use of prose.

**Movement VI (pp 48–56): yes. I felt the ending.**

- "I couldn't leave him behind. Not in a house with your name on it." The boy's death is a closed door and a toy, which is stronger than anything drawn.
- The Count names himself at the worst possible moment. It is the right moment, because his triumph and his bill meet on one landing.
- "I buried a son once, and somebody dug him up. I'll dig this one up too." Then "Then I'll dig up every garden in Paris." Madness that grows out of everything Villefort has been.
- p53 turns the "One." from Volume II into a failure to say "Two". Writing his own boast ("He had meant to be standing in every road of that house") against "All morning he had been in the gallery, watching Villefort's face" names the exact failure without moralising.
- p54's double "Yes." from Noirtier is devastating.
- p55's "five minutes to eleven" is a near-reveal that will make Francisco shout.

**Appetite across the whole book.** It survives, bends, and breaks exactly where the mandate asks.

- **Visible appetite (pp 2–46):** p2, 3, 5, 8, 9, 12, 13, 17, 20, 21, 22, 25, 26, 30, 34, 39 and 46.
- **His only quiet stretches:** the Morrel pages (pp 14–16, where warmth itself is the point) and the false-death run (pp 35–38, where he is absent or silent by design, and p39 restores him "smiling").
- **The break:** it comes only at p51.

**Want-from, page by page.** Every scene passes. Each speaker wants something from the other person in the room, and none of it is "to inform". A few examples:

- Benedetto wants the name; the Count wants the man.
- Héloïse wants the Count's blessing without asking for it.
- Villefort wants his father to stop.
- Valentine wants to be taken away now.
- Maximilien wants his promise back.

**Subtext.** Subtext runs through most dramatic pages: p6, 9, 16, 20, 21, 25, 26, 37 and 55 all say less than they mean.

**Attribution-strip.** It passes cleanly:

- Benedetto's "my lord"
- Villefort's "monsieur" and his half-finished sentences when frightened
- Héloïse's endearments
- Haydée's flat questions
- Maximilien's whole-hearted rush
- Valentine's dry tenderness

**Dead registers.** I found no logistics dialogue. The arrangements (the house next door, the workmen, the police letter) are in prose, one line each. I found no aphorism dialogue. The nearest candidate is Villefort's "The law cannot weigh a man's hunger…", and it belongs to a prosecutor in court; nobody else could say it.

**Is this any good?** Yes. It is a cruel, funny, propulsive book with a protagonist who wants things, and it earns its ending. I wanted to turn every page.

## Nonblocking observations

None of these is a defect. They are offered only if the owner wants them, and none should hold the gate.

1. **p33.** Valentine learns in one sentence that her grandmother was murdered *and* that she herself is next. "Who would do that?" absorbs both, so the grandmother gets no beat of her own. An optional fix: open her line with "Grandmother was poisoned?" before "Who would do that?". That gives her the shock in her own mouth at little cost. It is not needed for comprehension.
2. **p41.** "I have waited three days for you to spare me this." By p39's count of nights, the gap is nearer four days. No reader will be hurt by it, and a man counting his own dread may round down. I note it only as a fact check.
3. **p30, packet note rather than script.** In the "questioning" panel 5, Noirtier's eyes should read as a demand, not confusion, so that "You want to know how I know…" feels pulled out of the Count. This is art direction only.
