VERDICT: REVISE

# Script gate, round 9: *The Count of Monte Cristo, Volume III: The King's Attorney*

## Cold read

I read pp 1–56 in order before opening any other document. This is the story as a first-time reader would tell it.

Twenty-three years ago three men sent Edmond Dantès to the Château d'If. He is now the Count of Monte Cristo. Fernand is dead, and Villefort, the King's Attorney, is next. The Count has bought a house at Auteuil because of a tree. Under it, twenty-one years ago, Villefort buried a baby he thought was dead. A man hiding behind the wall dug it up alive and later confessed this to "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count in disguise. That baby is now Benedetto: a convict, a murderer and a card cheat. The Count dresses him as Prince Andrea Cavalcanti and promises him dinner with the King's Attorney.

The Count watches Villefort win in court. He arranges for half-broken horses to bolt down his hill with Villefort's wife and small son in the carriage, stops them with his own body, and revives the boy with one red drop. Then he gives the vial to Madame de Villefort, a woman who is visibly hungry for it ("Ten drops would stop a heart"). He admits all of this to Haydée.

The Count gets into Villefort's house. He meets Valentine, the neglected daughter, and Noirtier, the paralysed grandfather who speaks only with his eyes. He tells Noirtier who he really is. Noirtier does not want him to stop, only to spare Valentine, and the Count gives his word. Then he learns that Maximilien Morrel loves Valentine. Haydée asks him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall."

At the Auteuil dinner Villefort shakes his unknown son's hand. In the garden the Count tells a "ghost story" about the tree. At two in the morning Villefort digs and finds an empty hole. The grandmother is poisoned with milk while Noirtier watches. Villefort recognises a poisoning and chooses not to know who did it. Héloïse all but names Valentine as next. The Count bets that she will wait and writes to the police. Benedetto is arrested, and Villefort will prosecute him himself.

The bet fails: Valentine collapses after drinking chocolate. She survives because Noirtier has been dosing her with his medicine. The Count admits to Noirtier that the bottle was his. He waits inside a wardrobe, watches Héloïse pour, wakes Valentine and gives her a false death. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE for his son. Maximilien stands at the tomb ("You promised me"). The Count lifts Valentine out, but she does not wake.

In a cell the Count gives Benedetto his father's name and birth date. Villefort tells his wife to take her own poison, then kisses the boy goodbye. In court Benedetto names Villefort as his father, Villefort lets slip "I thought it was dead", and then confesses outright. The Count gets the face he waited twenty-three years for. In the carriage Villefort remembers that the boy is at home with her. On the stair Héloïse says she took the boy with her. The Count picks up the empty vial and reveals himself as Edmond Dantès. The boy is dead, and Villefort goes mad, digging in his own garden. The Count cannot say "Two". He tells Noirtier that Valentine is alive, and Noirtier says he wishes the Count had stopped. He buys Maximilien one month with "five minutes to eleven". Valentine's hand closes.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p31**, "He had waited fourteen years for less." I reread it and still had to guess what "less" means.
- **p39**, Haydée's hand on his sleeve while he smiles about tomorrow. I paused: is she comforting him or holding him back?
- **p54**, "I'm telling you because you're the one man who can't tell anyone." I stopped hard here. Eighteen pages earlier I watched this man tell his son a name, letter by letter. (Finding 1.)
- **p26**, "He buried her in two days…" I took a beat to fix "He" as Villefort, since nobody has named him in the preceding balloons.

I paused nowhere else. I did not reread any other page.

## Is it alive?

Yes, and it is the most propulsive script in this series that I have read. I wanted to turn nearly every page, and the last movement hurt.

**I (pp 1–8).** This is a superb opening. The Count smiles over a baby's grave (p2). "Yours has a rich father in it" (p4) is a hook. "I enjoy watching a man get what he deserves" against Villefort's "I have never once let a guilty man go free" (p6) is a duel in two lines. p8 is the engine in a single panel: "You may need it one day." Heat on every page.

**II (pp 9–18).** "Half broken. And I told him the hill road… was the prettiest drive in Auteuil" is cruel and funny, which is exactly the register this volume wants. The Noirtier pages are the best dialogue scene of the book. The want-from is precise: the Count wants a witness who cannot betray him, and Noirtier wants Valentine's safety bought. The silent eye-movement to the door is enacted, not said. p16's "Of every house in Paris, it had to be that one" is the first crack, and p17 seals it at once with appetite ("Every floor of it"). Haydée's "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage" is the volume's prophecy, and the book collects it.

**III (pp 19–27).** "To fathers" (p20) is the funniest line in the book. p22 is a perfect spectacle page. Héloïse's p25 is the creepiest scene: sweetness as a confession, and the Count's "You are careful with it, I hope?" is pure subtext. In p26 Haydée lands the charge ("Did you see the milk?") and the Count dodges it, which is honest characterization.

**IV (pp 28–39).** Dread is sustained throughout. Noirtier's secret dosing is a real reveal that the reader was set up for on p23. "She will be kind to you every night until you are dead" is chilling. Valentine's "He does. So I will" is earned. The Count's smile over the pastille and at the window keeps his appetite alive through the rescue.

**V (pp 40–47).** The trial works like a fuse burning down: name, age, date, place, father. "You didn't look" answers p2's "He didn't stop to listen". The caption "He had waited twenty-three years for that face" is the climax of the Count's appetite, and the book makes us share it a beat before it charges for it.

**VI (pp 48–56).** It earns its ending. The vial coming back up the stair, "If it were full, it would still be too late", the hussar in his pocket, the "Yes" from Noirtier: I felt it. The one weak line in this movement is the subject of Finding 1.

**Presence tests:**
- **Want-from:** passes on every dramatic page.
- **Subtext:** present on most pages (pp 9, 13, 17, 20, 25, 26, 34, 37, 55).
- **Attribution-strip:** passes. Benedetto's "my lord", Héloïse's endearments, Haydée's bluntness, Villefort's legal formality and Valentine's dry jokes ("pretending to look at snails") are each unmistakable.
- **Logistics:** none in dialogue. The arrangements sit in prose on p31, where they belong.
- **Aphorism:** none of the blocking kind.
- **Appetite:** the Count is never serene for more than a page.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p54, panel 4: the Count's stated reason contradicts what the book showed on p36.**

> COUNT: "She hasn't woken. I don't know if she will. I'm telling you because you're the one man who can't tell anyone."

- **The failure.** On p36 the reader watched Noirtier tell his son a name, letter by letter, for a quarter of an hour. That page was the turn of Movement IV. So the Count's justification here, at the most emotional dialogue page of the aftermath, is false inside the book.
- **What the reader has to repair.** A first-time reader, the 10-year-old especially, will object: "But he *can* tell — he spelled HÉLOÏSE." They then have to decide whether the Count is wrong or whether the scene means something else. That also opens a worry the book never intends: will Noirtier now spell VALENTINE to Villefort, or to Maximilien?
- **The missed opportunity.** The line throws away a richer truth the book has already earned. Noirtier has been able to spell DANTÈS to his son since p12, and he never did. He kept the Count's secret.
- **Revision.** Replace the second sentence with a reason that is true, for example: "You've kept my name since the day I told you. Keep this one too." (Or: "I'm telling you because you're the one man in this house who has kept every secret I gave him.") Keep "She hasn't woken. I don't know if she will." as written.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p31, "He had waited fourteen years for less."** The comparison has no clear object, and I reread it. Consider "He had waited fourteen years in the Château d'If; he could wait three nights in a wall," then cut "He waited three nights", or keep it as the last beat.
2. **p26, panel 2.** "He buried her in two days…" is the first word of the reply, and the antecedent is a man not in the room and not named in the preceding balloons. "Villefort buried her in two days…" costs one word and removes the beat of hesitation. The next balloon ("That is the man who sent me to If") does resolve it, so this is not blocking.
3. **p39, Haydée's hand on his sleeve while he smiles about the trial.** After p17 and p26 it is unclear whether this is tenderness or a check. If it is meant as a check, one clause would steer it ("…put her hand on his sleeve, as if to hold him back. He did not move it away."). This is an art-direction note for the packet either way.
4. **p23.** Héloïse doctors the milk in Noirtier's room "where nobody ever looked". This is just enough motive, and the round-8 change is what makes pp 24 and 30 work. Leave it.
5. **p24.** This is the first Noirtier yes/no exchange since p13. A one-time spoken cue from Villefort ("Close your eyes once for yes, Father.") would make the convention re-teach itself, so "VILLEFORT: Yes." cannot be misread as him answering himself. This is optional, since p11's explanation carries it.
6. **p56 / p17.** Haydée's silent look at the hussar is the only collection of her "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage". It is strong as a silence, and her line is planted, not spent, which fits the volume-IV deferral. It can stay as written.

**Five-word audit.** I gave every balloon of five words or fewer the explicit audit. All pass: each is a complete thought or a direct answer to a complete question in the same or the preceding panel, with a single possible referent and nothing that needs notes.
- **Answers:** p2 "Gérard de Villefort."; p3 "Villefort's son."; p34 "He does. So I will."; p40 "My lord, I wouldn't miss it."; p55 "One month."
- **Questions, each with its object present:** p2 "What was in the box?"; p3 "And the child?"; p4 "Who's asking?"; p9 "To whom?"; p15 "Why hopelessly?"; p16 "Who is her father?"; p33 "Who would do that?"; p34 "Maximilien trusts me. Do you?"; p36 "Héloïse? My wife?"; p40 "Him? The one who buried me?"; p43 "Your age?", "Where?"; p48 "Where is my son?"; p50 "Who's there?"
- **Forceful short lines:** p9 "That is Villefort's problem."; p12 "I was the sailor."; p17 "I know where she sleeps." (the referent is the girl; the menace is intended); p20 "To fathers."; p37 "You promised me."; p41 "You have your poison. Use it."; p44 "Monsieur, my father is you."; p45 "You didn't look." (it answers p2); p50 "I am Edmond Dantès."; p56 "She's holding on."
- **Noirtier answers spoken by the asker:** each "Yes." and "No.", and p36 "H.", is fixed by the drawn blink or the recited alphabet.

**Architecture and balance.**
- Every page has exactly one mode, one named turn and one marked dominant panel. No page has more than two locations (p50's stair and corridor are contiguous, and the move between them is the transition).
- Text inventory is within budget on every page. The heaviest prose pages (pp 23, 31, 53, 18, 10) run about 110–130 words, and the heaviest dramatic page (p40) runs about 100 words.
- Modes are 38 dramatic, 11 illustrated prose and 7 spectacle (68 / 20 / 12), which is within the mandate's stated lean and above the 18% prose floor. No run of more than five dramatic pages.
- No chronicle pages; the mandate opts out.
- Aftermath exists for every consequential beat: the grandmother (pp 24–26), Valentine's collapse (pp 29–30), the false death (pp 35–37), the trial (p47), the boy (pp 51–54), and the protagonist (pp 53–54).

**Introductions and continuity.**
- Every returning face and debt is re-introduced in story: the Château d'If and the list (p1), Fernand (p1), Haydée (p2), Busoni (p3), Noirtier and the 1815 letter (pp 11–12), the Morrels and the purse (p14), the eleven o'clock pistol (p55), "One." (p53), and Danglars (p18).
- The new cast is named and given a want before the plot depends on them.
- Time is consistent: 1815 + 23, 1817 + 21, Thursday, Tuesday and "third night", "last week", "three days", and the night count from p39 to p56. The vial and the hussar are tracked consistently from start to finish.

**Fidelity anxiety.** None. No functional crowd. Every character changes what the Count wants, knows or chooses.
