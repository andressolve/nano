## Cold read

The Count stands at night in a garden at Auteuil with Haydée. Twenty-one years ago Villefort buried a newborn baby under the plane tree there, without checking whether it was breathing. A hidden man dug it up alive and later confessed this to "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count in disguise. The child is now Benedetto: a thief, an escaped galley convict, and the killer of a guard. The Count dresses him as "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti" and aims him at the King's Attorney's dinner table.

The Count watches Villefort win a case in court. He then stops a runaway carriage carrying Villefort's wife and small son. The carriage was set up: he sold her husband half-broken horses and recommended the hill road past his gate. One drop from his red vial revives the boy. Madame de Villefort asks what more than one drop would do, and he gives her the vial knowing what she is.

Inside Villefort's cold house the Count meets Valentine, the daughter nobody looks at. He also meets Noirtier, the paralysed grandfather who speaks with his eyes, and tells him his son burned the 1815 letter and that the Count is the sailor. Noirtier does not ask him to stop; he asks about Valentine, and the Count gives his word she is safe. At the Morrels' the Count learns Maximilien loves Valentine, and for once the pleasure leaves his face. Haydée tells him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall."

Dinner at Auteuil, "To fathers", the ghost story under the tree, and Villefort digging an empty hole at two in the morning while the Count watches. The grandmother dies of the milk and Valentine inherits. Villefort recognises poison and tells his father: "Stop. Don't tell me." Madame de Villefort as good as tells the Count that Valentine is next, and he says nothing. Benedetto is arrested on the Count's anonymous letter, and Villefort will prosecute him himself.

Valentine collapses and survives because Noirtier has been dosing her with his medicine. The Count cuts a door into her wardrobe, watches the stepmother pour the drops, and gives Valentine a false death. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE. Maximilien says "You promised me" at the tomb. The Count lifts Valentine out, and she does not wake.

The Count gives Benedetto the date of his birth. Villefort tells his wife to use her poison and kisses his son goodbye. In court Benedetto says "Monsieur, my father is you," and Villefort answers "It is true." Villefort races home: the stair, the falling vial, the hussar outside a closed door. "I am Edmond Dantès." "Come and see what you have done!" The boy is dead, and Villefort goes into his garden to dig. The Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine is alive and asks Maximilien for one month. Valentine's hand closes.

## Is it alive?

**Yes. This is a very good script, and at its best it is better than good.** I wanted to turn nearly every page, and several pages are the kind a ten-year-old reads aloud to his brother: "To fathers.", "Your father will bury you, the way he buries everything.", "Monsieur, my father is you.", "You didn't look." The two findings below are local, not a judgement on the book's life.

**Movement I (pp 1–8).** This movement is excellent. The Count is enjoying himself on p2 ("And you're smiling.") and the reader enjoys it with him. The heat is high in every scene.
- p4–5: Benedetto has his own voice ("my lord", "You do have a sense of humour") and wants something from the Count in the room: his father's name, money. The Count wants Benedetto's appetite.
- p6: two men face to face, one of whom doesn't know the other buried him.
- p8: the best single page of the opening. Panel 4 is pure subtext, with her eyes on the vial and his on her. "Keep it, madame. You may need it one day." is an offer, a test and a crime in one line.

**Movement II (pp 9–18).** This movement is alive until p18.
- p9: the reveal that he staged the horses is a great turn, and "That is Villefort's problem." is the appetite in five words.
- p12–13: Noirtier is gripping without one balloon. "Do you want me to stop?" / many blinks / "No." is a real want-from between a man who can't move and a man who can.
- p14–16: the Morrel pages carry the book's one warm house, and the fall in p16 panel 3 ("Of every house in Paris, it had to be that one.") is the first crack in the Count.
- p17: works as heat. Haydée wants the bottle back, and he wants the fall and says so with relish ("Every floor of it."). But the line that closes the page relies on a promise the reader never heard (Finding 1).
- p18: the one page in the book where I felt nothing. It re-narrates p17 (Finding 2).

**Movement III (pp 19–26).** This is the most pleasurable stretch of the script.
- p19: the lovers' banter is specific to them ("pretending to look at snails", "That's the trouble with you").
- p20–22: a nearly perfect run: the handshake, "Then your father was a fool.", the ghost story, the spade, and the face at the window.
- p24: the best Villefort page in the book. He is frightened, intelligent, and makes the burial choice in front of us ("Stop. Don't tell me."). This is the third tempting book (Villefort as a cardboard monster) avoided.
- p25: the Count's silence is the event. Her want ("Unless something happened to her, of course. One mustn't think such things.") is delivered as an endearment, exactly in her voice. Then the hussar is pressed into his hand and given back. The Count is not serene here; he is complicit, and the page knows it.

**Movement IV (pp 27–38).** Thriller engine at full power.
- p29: lands both the mechanism and the accusation: "It was my bottle. I gave it to her. And I will still have your son."
- p31: the wardrobe view is the set piece the brief promised.
- p32–33: heat, and Valentine as an active chooser ("He does. So I will.").
- p33: the smile with the pastille keeps the appetite alive in the act of rescue.
- p36: Maximilien's "You promised me" hurts, as it should.
- p38: the Count smiling at the window the night before the trial, with Haydée's hand on his sleeve, is exactly right.

**Movement V (pp 39–46).** A courtroom detonation built as a sequence of short, clean questions. Name, age, where, father: each answer is a step down, and the reader sees every one coming and still flinches.
- p44: "You buried me alive, Father." / "It wasn't breathing. I thought it was dead —" / "You didn't look." These are the callbacks paying off.
- p45: the caption "He had waited twenty-three years for that face." is the appetite at its peak, just before the bill.
- p40, p46: these two pages are what make Villefort pitiable. The gentle line to the boy turns the carriage page into terror.

**Movement VI (pp 47–55).** It hurts, and it should.
- p47: "Not in a house with your name on it." is Héloïse's love and her crime in one sentence.
- p48: the hussar outside the door is how to show a child's death without drawing one.
- p49: "I am Edmond Dantès" said into a grief he hasn't seen yet, so the appetite overreaches at the worst moment. That is correct.
- p50–52: the Count's aftermath is enacted: the empty vial, the hussar picked up and kept in his pocket, the word he can't say.
- p53–55: three quiet, earned pages.

The one thing I missed is Haydée. She is the person who warned him about the child, and she never sees him again with that child dead (nonblocking N1).

**Appetite across the whole book.** It survives. It bends at p16, reasserts at p17, p25, p29, p33, p38 and p45, and breaks only at p50. It never sleeps for a page at a stretch. The serene-avenger failure is avoided.

## Mandatory findings

### 1. p9 → p17: Haydée throws back a promise the reader never saw him make

**The failure.** p17 panel 3: the Count says "Whatever happens in that house, Valentine walks out of it alive. I promise you that." Haydée replies: "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage."

Nowhere in the script does the Count say the boy would be safe. On p9 Haydée says "There was a child in that carriage." He answers "And now his mother owes me her son, and her husband owes me his door." That is a boast about debt, not a claim that the boy was never at risk.

**What the reader has to repair.** This is the page's turn line and the volume's moral prophecy, which pp 48–50 pay off. A first-time reader stops, flips back to p9, doesn't find the line, and has to invent an off-page conversation, or decide Haydée is misquoting him. The page-turn note ("he has no answer for her") lands only for a reader who fills the gap. This is a character acting on information the reader never saw.

**Prescription.** Plant the claim on p9, in the Count's mouth, where Haydée confronts him about the child. In panel 2, after "There was a child in that carriage.", have the Count answer first with the claim of control, then the debt. For example:

> **COUNT:** "The boy was never in danger, Haydée. I was standing in the road."
>
> **COUNT:** "And now his mother owes me her son, and her husband owes me his door. It opens on Thursday."

That adds about 13 words to a page of about 60. p17's line then becomes a true callback. The text is the same, but now the reader remembers it. Leave p17's line unchanged; it is the best line Haydée has.

### 2. p18 repeats p17: no turn of its own, and narration restating the scene before it

**The failure.** p17 dramatizes the decision. Haydée asks him to go back tonight and ask Madame de Villefort for the bottle. He refuses because he wants to hear the house fall, and promises Valentine walks out alive.

p18, in the same room on the same night, narrates the same decision again:

> "He could have gone back to Villefort's house, asked Madame de Villefort for the little red bottle… He did not. Villefort's house was going to fall, and the Count wanted to hear it fall. He would simply make sure that one girl was standing outside it when it did."

Its page-turn reason ("he has chosen to let the house burn and carry one girl out of it") is p17's turn re-worded. Only the Danglars sentence is new.

**What the reader has to repair.** Nothing to decode, but momentum dies on the last page of a movement, just when Movement III should be pulled forward. The narration repeats what the dialogue already made clear (§6), and the page has no dominant turn of its own (§8 test 3). The reader learns nothing new and feels nothing new. It is the one page in the book I would skim.

**Prescription.** Keep p18 as illustrated prose: the prose share is 11/55, close to the 18% floor, and the architecture protects the page. Rewrite it to carry the two things the book needs and doesn't yet have here.

1. **What Haydée's last line did to him.** This is a private belief that can't be spoken, which narration is allowed to carry. For example: he tells himself the boy lived because he was standing in the road, and that he will be standing in every road of that house. That gives the reader the exact belief pp 48–50 destroy.
2. **The forward step into Movement III.** He writes the invitations for the dinner at Auteuil, to the King's Attorney and his wife and to Prince Andrea Cavalcanti, and chooses the house Villefort knows. Right now the dinner on p20 simply happens; this page can make it his plan and his pleasure.

Keep the Danglars sentence as the last line. Rewrite the page-turn reason around the invitation, for example: "Villefort is coming to dinner in the house where he buried a child, and the Count has seated his son beside him."

Stay inside 140 words. Cut the sentences that restate p17 rather than squeezing them.

## Nonblocking observations

**N1. p55: Haydée never meets the Count after the boy dies.** She is the one who said "There was a child in that carriage" (p9) and "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage" (p17). The boy's death has aftermath for the Count (pp 50–52), Villefort (pp 50–51) and Noirtier (p53), but not for the one person who predicted it. Haydée's arc belongs to Volume IV, and the doorway is a legitimate held beat, but the reader will want the look exchanged.

Without converting the page out of spectacle: in panel 1, have the Count in the doorway with the tin hussar in his gloved hand. Haydée looks at the hussar, then at him, and does not take her hand from Valentine's. The prophecy is answered without a word.

**N2. p27, caption "Nobody else was in the room."** For a beat it reads as a clue about who did it. Make the meaning certain, for example: "Nobody else was in the room, and Noirtier could not even cry out."

**N3. p35, panel 1, "stared at Valentine's empty stool all morning."** This contradicts p34, where he spent the morning at her bedside and would not be wheeled away. Use "since they wheeled you back" or "all afternoon".

**N4. p50, "It would be too late now if it were full."** The word order makes the listener work. Try: "One drop of this brought him back on the hill. If it were full, it would still be too late. He's dead, Villefort."

**N5. p52: where he said "One."** The prose puts him "outside Fernand's house… [looking] at this very window"; the panel note says it is the same street and the same upward look. Per the volume mandate, Volume II's last page has him saying "One." under Villefort's lit window. Reconcile the prose and the panel note with that page, so the callback is a picture the reader recognises rather than geography to work out.

**N6. p30: why the Count expects night and the glass.** Everything else on this page is clear. One clause would close the question a sharp reader will ask, for example: "She would not try the chocolate twice; next time she would come at night, to the glass by the bed."

**N7. p33, "And Maximilien?"** The fragment works, but the Count's evasion reads as an evasion only if the question has an object. Try "And Maximilien? Will you tell him I'm alive?", so that "Maximilien trusts me. Do you?" is visibly a dodge, and p36 and p38 land as its cost.

**N8. Balloon density on p32 and p3.** p32 has about 103 words in 8 balloons, five of them in the 30% panel 3. p3 has about 94 words, five balloons in panel 3. Both are under the roughly 105-word ceiling, but each needs protected balloon lanes. On p32, consider giving "Then I'll tell my father in the morning…" / "Two people have been poisoned…" a panel of its own; that exchange is the page's heat and deserves its own beat. Do not cut words.

**N9. p49, "You never asked who I was, monsieur."** This is slightly soft, since Villefort had no reason to ask. Something like "You never once wondered who I was, monsieur." keeps the blade.

**N11. Balance and architecture.** 37 dramatic, 11 illustrated prose and 7 spectacle pages: 67 / 20 / 13, within the volume mandate. Every page has one mode, one dominant panel and one location (p22's window and p30's sliver of the next room are views from a single location). Every prose page is within 140 words. There is no logistics dialogue: the inheritance is said once, as Héloïse's want, on p25, and the police jurisdiction appears once, as Maximilien's obstacle, on p28. There are no aphorisms that survive the attribution-strip test. The Count, Villefort, Benedetto, Héloïse, Haydée, Maximilien and Valentine each sound like themselves.
