VERDICT: REVISE

## Cold read

I read the script in order, start to finish, before opening any project document.

The Count takes Haydée into the garden of a house he has bought at Auteuil. Under a plane tree, twenty-one years ago, Villefort buried his newborn bastard son and walked away. A hidden enemy dug the child up alive. The Count, disguised as the Abbé Busoni, later heard this as a confession. The child is now Benedetto: twenty-one, a card cheat, and a guard-killer escaped from Toulon. The Count dresses him as "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti" so that he can dine with the King's Attorney.

The Count watches Villefort win in court. He sells Madame de Villefort half-broken horses, catches her bolting carriage himself, and revives her limp son with one red drop from a vial. He watches her want it, and gives it to her. He gets inside Villefort's house. There he meets Valentine, the daughter nobody looks at, and Noirtier, who can only speak with his eyes. He tells Noirtier who he is. Noirtier says do not stop, but protect Valentine, and the Count gives his word. At the Morrels' he learns that Maximilien loves Valentine. Haydée tells him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall."

At the Auteuil dinner, Villefort shakes his son's hand. The Count tells a ghost story under the tree. Villefort comes back at 2 a.m. with a spade and finds an empty hole. Valentine's grandmother arrives and dies. Villefort sees it was poison and chooses not to know who did it. Madame de Villefort as good as tells the Count that Valentine is next. The Count has Benedetto arrested. Valentine is poisoned and survives, because Noirtier had been dosing her with his medicine. The Count cuts a door into her wardrobe, watches the stepmother pour, and has Valentine fake her death. He lets Maximilien grieve at the tomb, then lifts her out of the coffin. She does not wake. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE for his son.

The trial is a detonation: "Monsieur, my father is you." Villefort confesses. That morning he had ordered his wife to poison herself. She does, and takes the boy with her. The Count says "I am Edmond Dantès" to a man kneeling at his dead son's door. Villefort goes mad, digging in his own garden. The Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine lives, and asks Maximilien for one month. Valentine's hand closes.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p6, "the Count de Morcerf's."** Page 1 named the first man only as "Fernand." I had to join the two names myself.
- **p25 → p27.** The Count has just been told who is next, and does nothing. At p28 he is startled, and I asked: but you knew. Why didn't you act? The script never answers.
- **p23 → p38.** Haydée predicted the first death on p17. It happens on p23, and she says nothing about it for twenty pages.
- **p29, "You're right. It was my bottle."** How does Noirtier know that? I reread the page.
- **p36.** Why won't he whisper "she's alive"? Page 38 partly answers this later.
- **p52, "whether God was on his side."** Nothing in this volume plants that belief.

## Is it alive?

Yes, emphatically, with one dead patch where the engine should be hottest.

**Movement I (pp 1–8)** is the best opening in the series that I have seen in script. The Count smiling over a baby's grave, "I'm going to give him back", Benedetto's insolence ("You do have a sense of humour"), and "Ten drops would stop a heart… Keep it, madame." The appetite is on the page and the reader is complicit from page 8. Heat on every page.

**Movement II (pp 9–18)** is excellent. "That is Villefort's problem" is chilling and exactly in the Count's voice. The Noirtier scenes on pp 12–13 are the book's secret weapon: a confession to the one man who cannot repeat it, and "Do you want me to stop?" / No. The Count's face going dead at "Valentine de Villefort" is earned. Haydée on p17 is the moral engine: "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage." The attribution-strip test passes everywhere: Benedetto's "my lord", Villefort's legal blade, Héloïse's sweet breathlessness, Haydée's bluntness, and Valentine's dry snails joke are all distinct voices.

**Movement III (pp 19–26)** has a superb dinner. "To fathers." The spade at 2 a.m. is a great silent page, and "Don't tell me" is Villefort's whole character in one gesture. **But this is exactly where the protagonist goes missing.** The first person the Count's vial kills is an old woman with no debt to him. His promise ("nothing would happen there that he did not see") breaks on the very next prose page after he makes it, and he has no reaction. Haydée, whose warning just came true, is absent. On p25 he is told outright that Valentine is next. He says "You are careful with it, I hope?", which is good, ambiguous and complicit. Then he spends the next page framing Benedetto. Is he too hungry to care, or confident he can manage it? The script knows (its own p25 page-turn says "he did not stop her") but never lets the reader watch him choose. It is the moral hinge of the engine ("until the poison… reaches the people who did nothing"), and it happens off the page.

**Movement IV (pp 27–38)** is a genuine thriller: the chocolate, Noirtier's terror, the wardrobe, "She will be kind to you every night until you are dead," the pastille, the tomb. Valentine chooses. "He does. So I will." is lovely. The Count's appetite survives ("I will still have your son"; "He had waited fourteen years for less"; smiling about the trial).

**Movement V (pp 39–46)** is a perfect detonation. "You didn't look." The confession is earned, and the carriage page holds the dread exactly as long as it needs to.

**Movement VI (pp 47–55)** is devastating and restrained. The hussar outside the door, the Count revealing his name to a man kneeling at his son's door, "I'll dig this one up too," the failed "Two," and Noirtier's "Yes" to "Do you wish now that I had?" I felt this movement. The ending is earned, except that p52's self-indictment would cut twice as deep if the reader had already watched him fail once at p25.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p6 (with p1): Fernand and the Count de Morcerf are never joined.**
- **Failure:** p1 says "The first man on the list, Fernand, was already dead." p6 has Villefort say "We met at the Count de Morcerf's — before his unhappy end." The book never says these are the same man, yet the brief promises "every returning face and debt is re-introduced in story, never assumed."
- **Reader repair:** On p6 the reader has to work out who Morcerf is and whether his "unhappy end" is Fernand's death. That moment of recognition is the line's whole point: Villefort is chatting pleasantly about the first victim to the man who killed him.
- **Revision:** Join the names once, on p1: "The first man on the list, Fernand, who had made himself the Count de Morcerf, was already dead." Leave p6 as written.

**2. pp 23–26: the protagonist's aftermath of the first death, and his choice not to protect Valentine, are missing.**
- **Failure:** p18 has the Count promise himself "Nothing would happen there that he did not see." Five pages later (p23) the grandmother dies of his vial. On p25 Héloïse says "Unless something happened to her, of course" about Valentine, and the Count does nothing. p26 is the Benedetto arrest, p27 is Valentine poisoned, and on p28 his "poised right hand closes" in surprise.
  - Haydée, who predicted exactly this on p17, vanishes from p17 to p38.
  - The book's own aftermath rule ("every major beat gets its aftermath page") gives the grandmother's death an aftermath for Villefort (p24) but none for the Count.
- **Reader repair:** At p28 a first-time reader has to supply why the Count, who was told on p25 who is next and had given his word to Noirtier and Haydée, let the poisoning happen. Was it appetite, overconfidence, or the Benedetto trap taking priority? Each reading gives a different Count. The reader is solving the protagonist instead of watching him. This is also a serene-protagonist risk: three pages on which the man who handed out the poison registers its first victim only as a "very still" hand.
- **Revision:** Add one dramatic page between the current p25 and p26: the Count's study, Champs-Élysées, night, Haydée and the Count. In it:
  - Haydée names the cost plainly, for example: "An old woman who had never heard your name drank your bottle in a cup of milk."
  - She cites his promise back to him: "You said nothing would happen in that house you didn't see. Did you see the milk?"
  - She names the threat: "His wife told you today who is next."
  - The Count answers with appetite, not remorse. Villefort sat by that bed, knew the face, and chose not to ask: "I have waited twenty-three years to watch him do that."
  - He makes a visible, spoken choice about Valentine that the reader can hold him to. For example, the stepmother will not move again while the house is in mourning, so first he will put the prince in the dock in front of his father; or "I will be in that house before she lifts the bottle again."
  - End on Haydée's silence, or on a line he cannot answer.

  The anonymous letter on p26 then becomes the consequence of a choice the reader watched him make. p28's closing hand becomes the bill for that choice, and p52's "He had meant to be standing in every road of that house" lands as the second time he has failed, not the first. The page fits the balance: 38 / 11 / 7 over 56 pages is 68 / 19.6 / 12.5, still above the mandate's 18% prose floor. Renumber downstream.

**3. p29, panel 5: the Count answers an accusation Noirtier has no way to make.**
- **Failure:** "Noirtier's eyes fixed on the Count's face: an accusation." The Count replies: "You're right. It was my bottle. I gave it to her."
- **Reader repair:** Noirtier was not at Auteuil and never saw the vial handed over. "You're right" implies he knew, so the reader stops to work out how. The volume's central confession is being spent on a logic snag.
- **Revision:** Make the Count volunteer it. Panel 5: Noirtier's eyes on him, questioning, because the Count has just said he knows what is in the stepmother's bottle. Count: "You want to know how I know what's in her bottle. I know because I gave it to her. And I will still have your son." The accusation in Noirtier's eyes comes after the confession, not before it.

## Nonblocking observations

- **Five-word audit.** Every balloon of five words or fewer was audited.
  - **Pass (direct answers to a complete question, or forceful with a single referent):** p2 "What was in the box?"; p3 "And the child?", "Villefort's son.", "Gérard de Villefort."; p4 "Who's asking?"; p9 "To whom?", "That is Villefort's problem."; p12 "I was the sailor.", "I'll be just outside, Grandfather."; p15 "Why hopelessly?"; p16 "Who is her father?"; p17 "I know where she sleeps."; p19 "I galloped across Paris. Forgive me?"; p20 "To fathers."; p24 "No. Then who —"; p32 "Who would do that?"; p33 "Maximilien trusts me. Do you?", "He does. So I will."; p35 "H.", "Héloïse? My wife?"; p36 "You promised me.", "Come away from here, Maximilien."; p40 "You have your poison. Use it."; p42 "Your age?", "Where?"; p43 "Monsieur, my father is you."; p44 "You buried me alive, Father.", "You didn't look."; p47 "Where is my son?"; p49 "Who's there?", "I am Edmond Dantès."; p54 "One month."; p55 "She's holding on."
  - **Pass (Noirtier's Yes/No):** every "Yes." and "No." spoken for Noirtier (pp 12, 13, 24, 29, 35, 53) follows the convention and answers a complete spoken question.
  - **Weakest: p51 "Then I'll dig them all."** "Them" could mean gardens or trees. Madness covers it, but "Then I'll dig up every garden in Paris" costs four words and removes the doubt.
- **p36, the Count's silence at the tomb.** p38's reason ("she may never wake") only applies after the draught fails, and at the funeral he still expects her to wake. Consider one line on p36 that gives the reason in the moment, for example "Come away, Maximilien. The whole house is watching your face." That makes the cruelty purposeful and in character.
- **p52, "whether God was on his side."** The book never establishes this belief. A single earlier line would let p52 break something the reader has already watched him hold, for example p3: "God kept that child breathing for twenty-one years. For me."
- **Héloïse's motive arrives on p25, two pages after the murder on p23.** It works as a short mystery, and the mandate says the inheritance is spoken only once, so I am not prescribing a second statement of it.
- **Text density near the ceiling:** p32 (~103 words, 8 balloons), p39 (~100), p3 (~98). These are within the exceptional ceiling; they will need protected balloon lanes in the prompt. Do not trim causal language.
- **p34/p35:** Noirtier is wheeled to Valentine's upper-floor bedside and back down to his ground-floor room. Harmless, but "carried up to her" would be cleaner if anyone asks.
- **Mode balance:** 37 dramatic, 11 illustrated prose, 7 spectacle, which is 67 / 20 / 13. There are no Chronicle pages, as the mandate specifies. No location count exceeds two. Every page declares one mode and one dominant panel.
- **The planning docs lag the script.** Several page references in the ledger and settings are off by one, e.g. the vial "pouring (30)" and Benedetto "Prisoner (pp 38, 41–44)". The script is the authority; fix the docs before the page contract is built.
