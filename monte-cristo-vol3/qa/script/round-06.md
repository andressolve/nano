VERDICT: REVISE

## Cold read

I read pages 1–56 in order before opening anything else.

**What happened, in plain words.** Twenty-three years after Villefort buried Edmond Dantès in the Château d'If, the Count has bought a house at Auteuil. He shows Haydée the bare ground under its plane tree. Twenty-one years ago Villefort buried a newborn there, a child he had by a woman who was not his wife. A man hiding behind the wall dug the box up and found the baby breathing. The child is now Benedetto: a thief, a liar, and since April a murderer. The Count finds him in a cellar and turns him into "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti."

The Count watches Villefort win in court. Then he arranges the runaway horses, saves Madame de Villefort and her small son, and gives her the vial ("Ten drops would stop a heart"), knowing what he is reading in her face. Inside Villefort's house he tells Noirtier who the sailor was. Noirtier does not want him to stop, and asks about Valentine. The Count gives his word she has nothing to fear from him.

At the Morrels' he learns that Maximilien loves Valentine. Haydée asks him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall." The dinner at Auteuil puts father and son hand to hand. The ghost story under the tree sends Villefort back at two in the morning with a spade, to an empty hole.

The grandmother dies of warm milk. Villefort knows a poisoner's face and chooses not to ask. Madame de Villefort as good as tells the Count who is next, and he says nothing. He bets that she will wait, and denounces Benedetto to the police. She does not wait. Valentine survives the chocolate because Noirtier has been dosing her with his medicine.

Then come the wall, the wardrobe, the red drops, the false death, the funeral, and the tomb. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE. Villefort tells his wife to use her poison. At the trial Benedetto names his father, and Villefort confesses. He races home to find his wife dying on the stair and his son dead behind a door. Villefort goes mad digging in his own garden. The Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine lives, buys a month from Maximilien, and on the fifth night Valentine's hand closes.

The paraphrase test passes easily. I could tell this story to someone at dinner.

**Where I stopped, reread or guessed:**

1. **p26, "You said nothing would happen in that house that you didn't see."** I stopped and turned back. He never said that to Haydée. It is his private belief in the p18 prose, which opens after "Haydée had gone to bed" and says "he told himself." Same page: "I saw Villefort sit by that bed, know that face, and choose not to ask." How did he see it? The only scene where Villefort shows he knows that face (p24) is behind a shut door with Noirtier.
2. **p50, "You never once wondered who I was, monsieur."** I stopped. This man told a ghost story about a buried child in his own garden, and Villefort came back at 2 a.m. and dug an empty hole there (p21–22). After that the script never shows Villefort reacting to the Count at all. I had been waiting since p22 for Villefort to turn on his host.
3. **p48 → p50, the vial.** On p48 it slips from her hand and falls "down the white steps." On p50 the Count lifts it "from beside her hand" on the top step. I glanced back.
4. **p27, "Three days later the police received a letter."** I had just watched him fold that letter on p26. Why three days? It was a small stop and I worked it out (he waited until the funeral).
5. **p46's caption "He had waited twenty-three years for that face"** felt like a line I had already heard. I had: p26, "I have waited twenty-three years to watch him do that."

## Is it alive?

Yes, and it hurts in the right places. This is a cruel, funny, propulsive book, and the Count's appetite is its engine from the first page to p46. Then the book takes it away from him and leaves him unable to count. I wanted to turn nearly every page.

**Movement I (pp 1–8).** The best opening I have read in this series: lantern, bare ground, and "And you're smiling." The cellar scene runs on two appetites, Benedetto's for money and the Count's for the joke he is setting up ("Yours has a rich father in it"). p8 does the thing the brief demands: he hands the poison over on the page, pleasantly, reading her. "Keep it, madame. You may need it one day" is chilling. Heat: he is arming a murderer in front of her child.

**Movement II (pp 9–18).** p9 is the movement's best page. "Half broken. And I told him the hill road past my gate was the prettiest drive in Auteuil" is appetite as a confession, and "To whom?" / "That is Villefort's problem" is pure subtext. Noirtier's eyes work without notes; "I was the sailor" said to the one man who cannot repeat it is a real event. The Morrel pages are warm without going sugary, and p16 panel 3 is the first time the pleasure leaves his face. That is earned. p17 is a genuine fight: Haydée wants the bottle back, he wants the sound of the house falling, and her last line ("That is what you said about the boy in the carriage") lands because p9 planted it.

**Movement III (pp 19–27).** The gate scene is the only place in the book where anyone flirts, and Valentine's "pretending to look at snails" gives her a voice nobody else has. The dinner is dramatic irony at full heat ("Then your father was a fool." / "To fathers."). The empty hole at 2 a.m., with the pale face in the window, is the movement's image. Villefort's "Stop. Don't tell me" is him in four words. p25 is the best-written scene in the book: Héloïse says the worst thing most gently, the Count answers "You are careful with it, I hope?", and the boy hands him the hussar.

The new p26 is right in conception. Haydée's question has a real object ("Did you see the milk?"), and his answer dodges in a way that tells us everything. But its central line rests on words Haydée never heard (Finding 1).

**Movement IV (pp 28–39).** Dread is handled well. Noirtier as a statue with terrified eyes is a strong spectacle inside a dramatic page. On p30 the Count says outright "I know because I gave it to her. And I will still have your son." The appetite bends here and does not break, exactly as the mandate wants. The wardrobe is a thriller set piece, and Valentine's "He does. So I will." is the best five-word balloon in the book. The funeral works because the Count says none of the things he could say.

One quieter gap: the Count's lost bet (p26, "She won't dare again") is acknowledged only by a hand closing on p29. I let it pass, but see the nonblocking notes.

**Movement V (pp 40–47).** The cell scene is a delight. Benedetto is the one character who can match the Count's pleasure ("My lord, I wouldn't miss it"). The trial is built like a trap closing: name, age, date, place, father. "You didn't look" calls back to "He didn't stop to listen" from p2, and it lands like a blow. The carriage page is exactly the right prose page in exactly the right place.

**Movement VI (pp 48–56).** Héloïse's "Not in a house with your name on it" is devastating and entirely hers. The hussar outside the closed door does the work no drawn body could. "I'll dig this one up too" makes Villefort's madness grow out of who he has always been rather than out of plot. p53 is the moral fork, and its line "All afternoon he had been in the gallery, watching Villefort's face" is the bill stated in one sentence. Noirtier's final "Yes" is the cleanest moral judgment in the series, delivered by a man with no voice.

**Voice / attribution-strip.** Passes everywhere. Benedetto's insolence, Héloïse's endearments, Villefort's "monsieur" and his half-stopped sentences when frightened, Haydée's plainness, Maximilien's all-at-once frankness, Valentine's dry tenderness, and the Count's doors-shutting sentences are all identifiable without names. There is no aphorism dialogue and no logistics dialogue. The arrangements (the house next door, the workmen, the letter) are in narration, one line each, as they should be.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p26 (with p17–18): Haydée quotes a thought she never heard, and the Count claims to have seen a scene he could not have seen.**

*The failure.* Haydée: "You said nothing would happen in that house that you didn't see. Did you see the milk?" The Count never says this to her. Its only appearance is p18's private prose: "Long after Haydée had gone to bed… He would be standing in every road of Villefort's house. Nothing would happen there that he did not see." The prose even marks it as self-talk ("he told himself"). Her question is the spine of the new page, and it rests on information the reader watched her not receive.

His reply, "I saw Villefort sit by that bed, know that face, and choose not to ask," is the same kind of problem. The only place Villefort shows he recognises a poisoner's face is p24, behind a shut door with Noirtier, and the Count was not there.

*What the reader must repair.* At the page's key line the reader has to invent an off-page conversation, or decide the book slipped. Then, one balloon later, they have to invent a way for the Count to know about a private scene. It is the volume's first moral reckoning, and it lands while the reader is doing continuity work.

*Revision.*
- (a) Give the boast to the Count aloud on p17, before her retort. For example, in panel 3: COUNT: "I was standing in the road for the boy, and I will be standing in every road of that house. Nothing will happen there that I don't see. Whatever happens, Valentine walks out of it alive. I promise you that." Then HAYDÉE: "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage." The retort gets sharper, because he has just repeated the road.
- Then make p18's second paragraph his private doubt about the promise rather than its first statement. For example: "He had told her he would be standing in every road of that house. He believed it."
- (b) Change the Count's p26 answer to something he could have seen in public. For example: "I saw Villefort bury her in two days without sending for one doctor or one policeman. He knows that face, Haydée. He prosecuted four of them. And he chose not to ask."

**2. p22 → p50: Villefort's aftermath of the empty hole is missing, and p50's line contradicts what the reader saw.**

*The failure.* p21–22: the Count tells a ghost story about a child buried under his tree, and Villefort comes back at 2 a.m., digs, and finds nothing. The page-turn line says it outright: "Villefort now knows that someone dug the box up." This is one of the book's big beats. After it, Villefort never once reacts to the Count. He does not question him or avoid him, and he goes on letting him kneel in Noirtier's room (p30). On p50 the Count then says, "You never once wondered who I was, monsieur." A first-time reader's answer is: of course he wondered, he dug in your garden.

*What the reader must repair.* Between p22 and p50 the reader has to supply Villefort's reaction to the most frightening night of his life, and then excuse a payoff line that the evidence contradicts. The mandate's own description of Villefort ("every time — choosing to bury rather than to know") would explain the silence perfectly, but only if the reader is shown that he chose it.

*Revision.* Put the choice on the page once, then let p50 collect it.
- In p27's prose, which has room at about 85 words, add after "sat at a table with him": "He had dug under the Count's tree in the dark, and never asked the Count one question about it. He did not ask now."
- Or put it in Villefort's mouth on p24, before he asks about the poisoner. For example: "Someone dug up my garden at Auteuil, Father, twenty years ago. Now someone has done murder in my house. I will not ask about either."
- Then rewrite p50: "You dug under my tree at two in the morning, monsieur, and you never once asked who I was. I've come to tell you."
- The rest of p50 stays as it is.

**3. p48 / p50: the vial's position contradicts itself.**

*The failure.* p48 panel 2, the dominant image: the vial is "slipping from her other hand and falling down the white steps." p50 panel 1: "the empty cut-crystal vial just lifted from beside her hand — his own, come back to him." The book has trained the reader to follow this one object from p8 onward. The reader sees it bounce down the stair and then finds it back at the top, at her hand. It is also a contradiction that the page packets and both page critics would inherit.

*What the reader must repair.* At the moment the vial "comes back to him," the reader has to decide which picture to believe.

*Revision.* Keep p48 as it is. The vial falling down the white stair is the stronger image. Restage p50 panel 1 so the Count, coming up the stair, stoops and lifts the empty vial from the step where it fell, below her outstretched hand, with his eyes on her face above him. Update the object table in `05-SETTINGS-AND-OBJECTS.md` to match when the docs are resynced.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p26 / p46, "twenty-three years" used twice.** p26's "I have waited twenty-three years to watch him do that" spends the exact phrase that p46's caption needs for the climax ("He had waited twenty-three years for that face"). The phrase reaches the reader the second time as an echo, not a release. Keep it for p46. On p26 try: "That is the man who sent me to If. I wanted to watch him do it once more, with his eyes open."
- **p27, "Three days later the police received a letter."** The reader has just watched him fold it. Either "The morning after the funeral, the police received a letter…" or "He posted it three days later, after the funeral."
- **The lost bet (p26 → p28–31).** The Count bet that she would wait while the house was in mourning, and she did not wait a week. Only the closing hand on p29 registers this. One sentence in p31's prose would make his appetite visibly take a hit and keep going: "He had bet that she would wait. She had not waited a week." It would also deepen p53.
- **p31, last paragraph.** About 75 words in one paragraph, well past the 2–5-line paragraph instruction for prose fields. Break it after "to the glass by the bed."
- **p53, "All afternoon he had been in the gallery."** The trial is "in the morning" (pp 39, 41). "All that day" or "All morning" keeps the sense without the snag.
- **The inheritance is stated three times** (p23 prose, p24 Villefort, p25 Héloïse). p24 and p25 each do dramatic work: his suspicion of his own daughter, and her want. p23's clause could shrink to "…and that Valentine was now very rich," so the mandate's "said once, as a want" rests on p25.
