## 2. Cold read

**What happened, as a first-time reader who has read Volumes I and II.**

**The setup.** At night the Count takes Haydée into the garden of a house he has bought at Auteuil. He shows her a patch of bare ground under a plane tree. Twenty-one years ago Villefort buried a newborn there, his child by a woman who was not his wife. He thought it was dead and did not listen. A man hiding behind the wall dug it up alive and later confessed this to "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count in disguise. The child is now Benedetto: a convict who escaped the galleys and killed a guard doing it. The Count finds him cheating at cards in a cellar and makes him "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti". He watches Villefort win a case in court and trades a cold, polite threat with him.

**The vial.** Then the Count's own half-broken horses bolt down the hill, carrying Madame de Villefort and her little son. The Count stops them in the road. One red drop from his vial revives the boy. She asks what more than one drop would do. He tells her ten would stop a heart, and he gives her the vial. That was the moment I knew what book I was in.

**Villefort's house.** Haydée works out that the horses were his, and he admits it. At Villefort's house he meets Valentine, the daughter nobody looks at, and Noirtier, who speaks only with his eyes. Alone with Noirtier, the Count tells him who he is. Noirtier does not want him to stop, but asks about Valentine, and the Count gives his word. Then, at the Morrels' house, Maximilien tells him he loves Valentine. For the first time the pleasure goes out of the Count's face. Haydée tells him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall." He promises Valentine will walk out alive, and Haydée throws the boy in the carriage back at him.

**The dinner and the first death.** The dinner at Auteuil, the ghost story under the tree, and Villefort digging an empty hole at 2 a.m. are delicious. The grandmother dies of the red drops, poured in front of Noirtier. Villefort recognises a poisoning and stops his father from naming the poisoner. Héloïse, sweetly, tells the Count who is next. Haydée confronts him. He bets the poisoner will wait, and loses within a week.

**Valentine.** Noirtier's secret dosing saves Valentine once. The Count confesses to Noirtier that the vial was his. He then cuts a door through the wall, watches from inside the wardrobe, and fakes her death. He lets Maximilien grieve at the tomb, lifts her out of the coffin, and she does not wake.

**The trial and the bill.** Benedetto is given his father's name and names him in court. Villefort confesses, then remembers the order he gave his wife that morning. She did what he told her and took the boy with her. The Count says "I am Edmond Dantès" in that corridor, carrying an empty vial. Villefort digs in his own garden. The Count cannot say "Two." He keeps his promise to Noirtier, buys Maximilien a month, and Valentine's hand closes.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed.** All are minor; details and fixes are in section 5.
- **p26:** for a beat I wondered how Haydée knew about the milk and about Héloïse's "who is next" conversation. I supplied the answer myself: he tells her things.
- **p30:** I wanted one look from Noirtier after "I gave it to her". The page moves on before I got it.
- **p37:** "The whole house is watching your face". I paused over why that matters.
- **p56:** "On the fifth night". I briefly counted nights back to p39.

None of these sent me back through the book, and none left a causal step missing.

**Paraphrase test: passes.** After one read I can say who acted, what they wanted, what they chose, why, and what changed, movement by movement.

## 3. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a cruel, funny, propulsive book, and the cruelty belongs to the protagonist. That is what the engine asks for.

**Movement I (pp 1–8).** This is the best opening the series has had in script.
- p2 ends with "And you're smiling. Who was he?" / "Gérard de Villefort." The book starts with appetite, not exposition.
- p4–5: Benedetto has a voice you could pick out with the names covered ("Rich how rich?", "You do have a sense of humour").
- p8 is the moment to buy the book. The want-from on that page is plain: she wants the vial, he wants her to want it, and both sets of eyes are drawn.
- Heat: he is arming a murderer on purpose, and the reader is his accomplice.

**Movement II (pp 9–18).** This movement is cold pleasure, then a crack.
- p9, "That is Villefort's problem." The Count dismisses whoever she poisons in four words.
- pp 12–13: confessing to the one man who cannot repeat it is the Count's secret want, enacted. Noirtier's "No" to "Do you want me to stop?" is chilling.
- p16, "Of every house in Paris, it had to be that one.", is the first bend in his appetite, and it is shown on his face.
- p17 is the best page of the movement. He refuses with appetite, makes a promise he cannot guarantee, and Haydée's line ("That is what you said about the boy in the carriage") hangs over the rest of the book.
- Subtext: "I know where she sleeps." is defensive, evasive, and in character.

**Movement III (pp 19–27).** This movement is delight curdling.
- p19 is the only lightness in the book, and it is earned ("pretending to look at snails").
- p20, "To fathers.", is a three-syllable dramatic-irony detonation.
- p22: the spectacle page is enacted, not discussed.
- p23: the round-7 fix lands. The poisoning happens in front of Noirtier, and the prose's irony ("the one man in the house who could not tell") is a character beat for Héloïse, not just plumbing.
- p24: Villefort's "Stop. Don't tell me." characterises him perfectly. He buries by reflex.
- p25: Héloïse says the worst thing most gently ("my poor boy is worth nothing at all"), and the Count's "You are careful with it, I hope?" is an answer that does not answer.
- p26 still dodges Haydée's "Did you see the milk?" and replies with hunger ("I wanted to watch him do it once more, with his eyes open"). That is the right kind of evasion.

**Movement IV (pp 28–39).** This movement is dread, and the Count keeps his teeth.
- p30, "I know because I gave it to her. / And I will still have your son.", is the line the engine needs in the middle of the rescue plot.
- p31: "she had not waited a week" puts the lost bet on the page, and "He had waited fourteen years for less" shows the appetite and the patience together.
- pp 33–34 is a terrific two-page scene. "She will be kind to you every night until you are dead" is pure Count. "He does. So I will." is Valentine choosing for herself. The mouth lifts on "So you will die."
- The tomb (p38) is silent and earned.
- p39: Haydée's hand on his sleeve, and him smiling about the trial, keeps both threads alive at once.

**Movement V (pp 40–47).** This is the courtroom detonation, and it plays.
- pp 43–45 escalate one question at a time: name, age, where, father.
- "You didn't look." pays off p2's "He didn't stop to listen" exactly.
- p46's caption names the appetite without moralising.
- p47 turns triumph into a race with a clock in four sentences.

**Movement VI (pp 48–56).** The bill is devastating and restrained.
- Héloïse's "Not in a house with your name on it." is the most frightening line in the book, and it is hers alone.
- p50 collects Villefort's silence ("never once asked who I was") and turns the reveal into an accusation.
- p51: the empty vial held up, and "If it were full, it would still be too late."
- p53 collects both "standing in every road" and God.
- p54, "Do you wish now that I had?" / "Yes.", is the moral fork of the volume, put in the mouth of the man who told him not to stop.
- p56 closes with Haydée looking at the hussar. It is the wordless payoff of p17.

**Appetite through the whole book.** It survives. There is no stretch where the Count reads as serene, and the collapse arrives only after triumph.

**Would I read it?** Yes. I wanted to turn nearly every page, and I felt the last movement.

## 5. Nonblocking observations

None of these blocks approval. Each costs a few words.

1. **p26: Haydée knows things the reader never saw her told** (the milk; "his wife told you today who is next"). The same pattern appears on p17, where she knows Maximilien loves Valentine. Confidants learning things off-page is acceptable, but one clause would remove the pause and add appetite. For example, Haydée: "You came home tonight and told me she thanked you for it. Did you see the milk?"

2. **p30: Noirtier never reacts to "I know because I gave it to her."** The Count has just confessed to the one man who took his word, and the page moves to "And I will still have your son." without Noirtier's response. Stage panel 5 so his eyes are drawn hardening, staring and unblinking, after the confession, before the last line. This is art direction only; no words need to change.

3. **p37: why it matters that "The whole house is watching your face" is not clear.** Whose house, and so what? A cold reader can't say. Try "Come away, Maximilien. Her father is watching your face." That gives the Count a concrete reason (the romance is secret, and the extraction is tonight) and keeps the evasion.

4. **p56: "On the fifth night."** Counting from p39 ("the third night… The trial was in the morning"), the trial night is the fourth. So p56 is either the next night or the reader stalls on the count. Either make the caption "The next night, Valentine's hand closed." or keep the number and accept the pause.

5. **p9: "her husband owes me his door"** is a little figurative for its job. p10 clears it up immediately, so this is only a note: "her husband owes me an invitation to his house" would be plainer.

6. **Benedetto's aftermath is a single clause on p47** ("still grinning, the most famous prisoner in France"). That is adequate for this volume, since his ending belongs to Volume IV, and it is noted only so the hand-off is deliberate.

7. **Budget notes for packets, not script changes.**
   - p3, panel 3 carries five balloons alternating between Haydée and the Count. Reserve left and right lanes.
   - p23 (about 133 words) and p31 (about 128 words) are past the 120 norm but inside the exceptional ceiling. Compose the prose field first.

8. **Why didn't Noirtier spell the poisoner's name to Valentine?** A sharp reader might ask. p31's "she would never believe it of her stepmother until she saw it with her own eyes" covers it implicitly, and p24 shows him trying to tell his son. No change needed.
