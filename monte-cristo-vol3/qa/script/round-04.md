VERDICT: REVISE

## Cold read

I read pages 1 to 55 in order before opening any other document.

**The story as a first-time reader would tell it.** The Count of Monte Cristo has bought a dark house at Auteuil. He takes Haydée into the garden at night and tells her what is under the bare earth beneath a plane tree. Twenty-one years ago Villefort buried a newborn there, the child of a lady who was not his wife. He thought it was dead. A man hiding behind the wall dug it up alive, and later confessed this to "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count in disguise. That child is Benedetto: a cardsharp, an escaped convict, and the killer of a guard. The Count turns him into "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti" and promises him a rich father later.

The Count watches Villefort win a case in court. Then he sends half-broken horses, which he sold Villefort himself, down the hill past his own gate with Madame de Villefort and her small son in the carriage. He stops them in the road and revives the limp boy with one red drop from a crystal vial. He reads exactly what is in the mother's face, tells her what ten drops would do, and gives her the vial.

That gets him into Villefort's house. There he finds Valentine, the ignored daughter, and Noirtier, the paralysed grandfather who speaks with his eyes. The Count tells Noirtier who he is. Noirtier does not ask him to stop; he asks only about Valentine, and the Count gives his word. Then Maximilien Morrel says he loves Valentine. The pleasure leaves the Count's face for the first time. Haydée tells him to take the bottle back. He refuses: "I want to hear it fall." He promises Valentine will walk out alive, and Haydée throws his own words back at him.

Then come the dinner, the toast "To fathers", the ghost story under the tree, and Villefort digging at 2 a.m. into an empty hole. The grandmother dies and Valentine inherits. Villefort refuses to hear the poisoner's name. Héloïse all but tells the Count that Valentine is next, and he says nothing. The Count's anonymous letter gets Benedetto arrested, and Villefort vows to prosecute him.

Valentine survives her chocolate because Noirtier has been dosing her with his own medicine. The Count hides inside her wardrobe, catches Héloïse pouring, and gives Valentine a false death: "Your father will bury you, the way he buries everything." Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE for his son. Maximilien says "You promised me" at the tomb. The Count lifts Valentine out, and she does not wake.

At the trial Benedetto names his father: "You buried me alive, Father." / "You didn't look." Villefort confesses in open court. He has told his wife that morning to use her poison, and he races home. Héloïse has taken the boy with her. The Count reveals himself at the dead child's door, and Villefort digs in the wrong garden. In the snow the Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine lives, asks Maximilien for one month, and Valentine's hand closes.

**Where I stopped, reread or guessed:**

- **p29.** The Count tells Noirtier that "every morning since her grandmother died" he has had Valentine drink from his glass, and that it is the same substance as the bottle. I stopped: how does he know? I had the clue from p27 ("Bitter, like your medicine"). He was not in that room. (Finding 1.)
- **p47 → p49.** On p47 Héloïse sinks onto the top stair, eyes closing. On p49 the Count "has arrived" at the top of that stair, holding the vial he picked up from it. I stopped to ask where she is. She is not in the staging. The next mention of her is one clause on p53: "His wife is dead." (Finding 2.)
- **p17.** "You know exactly where she keeps it." I paused: how would Haydée know he knows, and what is "where"? (Nonblocking.)
- **p38.** "The draught should have worn off by morning." I briefly read this as the morning after she swallowed it, which would have woken her before her funeral. (Nonblocking.)
- **p52 → p53.** He is in the street under the windows, then kneeling in Noirtier's room inside the same house. I supplied the "he went back in" myself. (Nonblocking.)

Nothing else stopped me. Every returning face and debt is re-introduced in story:

- Fernand dead (p1)
- Haydée and her father (p2)
- Busoni (p3)

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a very good script, and in places it is cruel and thrilling in exactly the way the engine asks. I wanted to turn nearly every page.

**Movement I (pp 1–8).** It is alive from the first balloon. The Count smiling over the bare earth ("He didn't stop to listen") is a perfect plant for "You didn't look" on p44. Benedetto is the funniest voice in the book from his first line ("That chair's taken, my lord"). The vial scene on p8 is the volume's thesis, and it is enacted: her eyes on the vial, his on her, "Keep it, madame. You may need it one day." That is the arming on the page, with pleasure, as the mandate requires.

**Movement II (pp 9–18).** p9 is the best Haydée has ever been: "Half broken." / "There was a child in that carriage." / "That is Villefort's problem." Plenty of heat, all of it in subtext. Noirtier's pages (11–13) are the best set piece in the first half: "I was the sailor," told to the one man who cannot repeat it. The p16 falter ("Of every house in Paris, it had to be that one") is earned. p17's "I want to hear it fall, Haydée. Every floor of it" keeps the appetite alive past the discovery, as the mandate demands. "That is what you said about the boy in the carriage" is a knife.

**Movement III (pp 19–26).** "To fathers." is a toast I will remember. The tableau under the tree and the empty hole with the pale face in the window are spectacle earned by clarity. p24, "Stop. Don't tell me," is Villefort's whole character in one gesture. On p25 Héloïse says the worst thing most gently, and the Count's "You are careful with it, I hope?" is complicity disguised as concern. The boy pressing the hussar into his hand, and the Count giving it back, is the movement's best silent beat.

**Movement IV (pp 27–38).** This is dread done right. The wardrobe-eye spectacle (p31) and the "Ask yourself why" turn (p32) are excellent. "Nobody hunts for a girl who is dead" works because it is strategy in his mouth, not theme. "He does. So I will." is Valentine's finest line. p29's "And I will still have your son" keeps him hungry at the worst moment. My one disappointment is that p29 hands the Count a deduction without its clue (Finding 1).

**Movement V (pp 39–46).** This is the best run of pages in the volume. The prosecutor hears his son give, as his birth date and birthplace, the night and garden of the burial. "Monsieur, my father is you." The slip "It wasn't breathing", the answer "You didn't look", and then "It is true." The caption "He had waited twenty-three years for that face" lands because the appetite has been fed for forty-four pages. p46 turns triumph into a race with one remembered sentence.

**Movement VI (pp 47–55).** Devastating, and mostly well judged. The hussar outside the closed door, the reveal "I am Edmond Dantès" made to a man kneeling at his son's door, "Come and see what you have done!", and "I'll dig this one up too" are horror without one drawn body. The Count's bill is enacted (the empty vial held up, the hussar picked up) and only then narrated (p52). Haydée's look at the hussar on p55 says more than any line could.

The one hole in this movement is Héloïse. The woman he armed dies off-page, between two pages, on the stair he climbs. The Count never looks at her. In a book whose engine is "the poison he handed out with his own hand," the hand that held it deserves one panel (Finding 2).

**Appetite.** It survives the whole book. It bends at p16 and is reasserted at pp 17, 20, 25, 29, 33, 38 and 45. It breaks only where the architecture means it to (p50 onward). At no point is the Count serene for a page at a stretch.

**Voice (attribution-strip test).** It passes everywhere. Benedetto is insolent with "my lord"; Héloïse is breathless and loving; Villefort uses "monsieur" like a blade and breaks off mid-sentence; Haydée is blunt; Valentine has dry humour; Maximilien says everything at once. I found no aphorism dialogue and no logistics dialogue. Arrangements (p30) sit in narration, as they should.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p29: the Count acts on information the reader never saw him acquire.**

- **The failure.** He says: "Every morning since her grandmother died, you've asked her with your eyes to drink from your glass. Your medicine." Then: "It's made from the same thing that is in her stepmother's bottle." The only staging is that he looks at the glass and then at Noirtier.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The clue lives on p27 ("Bitter, like your medicine —"), spoken to Noirtier alone. The Count was not there. Nothing on p28 or p29 gives it to him. He doesn't live in the house, so "every morning since her grandmother died" is exact knowledge he has no way to have. Nothing on the page shows how he knows what is in Noirtier's medicine. The reader must assume "he knows everything." That is the reader solving the adaptation, and it happens in the one scene meant to show the Count as the cleverest man in the room.
- **Revision.** Give him the clue in the scene, which also gives Valentine a line in her own voice. In panel 1, after "as if he's proud of me", add a Valentine balloon such as: "He's made me sip his horrid medicine every morning since Grandmother died. I only did it to please him." In panel 2, stage the Count lifting the lemon-water glass and breathing it in before he looks up at Noirtier. His line then becomes a deduction from what he has just heard and smelled ("Every morning since her grandmother died. Your medicine."). Keep panels 4 and 5 as written. Watch the dramatic budget: the page is near 82 words now, so offset by trimming panel 2's line to its new, shorter form.

**2. p47 → p49: Héloïse's body is missing, and her death has no on-page aftermath.**

- **The failure.** On p47 panel 3 she "sinks down onto the top stair, her eyes closing." On p49 panel 1, "Behind him at the top of the stair, the Count has arrived… the empty cut-crystal vial he has just picked up from the stair." She is not in the staging. She next exists as one clause on p53: "His wife is dead, and the boy."
- **What the reader has to repair.** Continuity of bodies: the Count is standing where she fell. The reader, and the builder, must guess whether she is lying there, was moved, or is still alive. And a consequential death lands with no reaction from anyone. The book's own mandate says what this moment is: the poison he handed out with his own hand reaching the people who did nothing, and the woman who held it dying on the white stair. The Count picks the vial up from beside her and the page never lets him look down. That skips the one image that joins p8 to the ending. The mandate explicitly permits "Madame de Villefort… shown fallen on the stair, eyes closed, with dignity."
- **Revision.** Restage p49 panel 1. Héloïse lies fallen on the top step in violet, eyes closed, her hand open where the vial slipped from it. The Count stands over her, the empty vial just lifted from beside her hand, his eyes on her face for a beat. Villefort kneels in the open doorway down the corridor, back to us. If that crowds the panel, give it its own small panel before the reveal. Either way, the Count's first sight on arriving should be the woman he armed. Optionally add a wordless detail, such as her hand still on the iron rail. No dialogue is required, and none should be added to her.

## Nonblocking observations

**N1. Text budget near the ceiling.** By my count:

| Page | Words | Balloons |
|---|---|---|
| p3 | about 102 | 8 |
| p9 | about 103 | 9 |
| p32 | about 103 | 8 |
| p39 | about 99 | 7 |

All four sit at the ~105 ceiling that `06-TYPOGRAPHY.md` allows only with large protected balloon lanes. p3 and p9 have wide, dark compositions that can protect lanes. p32 is tighter: its panel 4 carries two balloons of 16 and 18 words in a close bedroom shot. Ensure the page prompts reserve lanes. If p32 will not hold, prefer moving "Then I'll tell my father in the morning…" and its reply to the top of p33 over cutting a word of causal language.

**N2. p17.** "You know exactly where she keeps it." Haydée has no way to know this, and the line reads as a fact about a reticule. Something like "Then go back tonight and ask her for it. She'll give anything to the man who saved her son." keeps the bluntness and gives the line a real lever.

**N3. Closing the "standing in the road" ring on p52.** The book plants it three times:

- p9: "I was standing in the road"
- p17: Haydée throws it back
- p18: "He would be standing in every road of Villefort's house. Nothing would happen there that he did not see."

At the climax he was in the gallery watching Villefort's face. One sentence on p52 would turn dramatic irony into the Count's own knowledge, for example: "He had meant to be standing in every road of that house. All afternoon he had been in the gallery, watching Villefort's face." It fits the prose budget (about 95 words now).

**N4. p39, want-from.** Benedetto never asks who sold him to the police. He is clever enough to wonder whether it was "my lord". One exchange would give the cell scene a second edge. For example: "Was it you who wrote to the police, my lord?" / "Does it matter, if I give you something better?"

**N5. p28.** p27 is the first breach of the Count's belief that nothing would happen in that house without his seeing it. On p28 his only staged action is rising from the desk. One staging note (the poised right hand closing when Maximilien says "she should be dead") would register the hit without softening the appetite on p29.

**N6. p53.** The Count says "I promised you Valentine had nothing to fear from me" and moves straight to "She is alive". He does not acknowledge that his bottle nearly killed her twice and has just killed Noirtier's grandson. On p13 Noirtier told him not to stop. One question with real heat would make this scene a reckoning rather than a report, for example: "You told me not to stop. Do you wish now that I had?" with Noirtier's answer, whatever the writer decides it is.

**N7. p38.** "The draught should have worn off by morning" can be misread as the morning after she swallowed it. Clarify: "should have worn off the morning after he carried her out."

**N8. p6.** "We met at the Morcerfs'." could carry heat as well as orientation, for example: "We met at the Count de Morcerf's — before his unhappy end." That is Villefort mentioning Fernand's death, without knowing it, to the man who caused it.

**N9. p52 → p53.** One staging line or short caption on p53, such as "Then he went back into the house," would bridge the street to Noirtier's room.

**Architecture and balance.** Every page has exactly one mode, one turn, one dominant panel and at most two locations. No page has three or more locations, and none has four or more equal panels with nothing in charge. The split is 37 / 11 / 7, or 67 / 20 / 13, which meets the mandate's 18% prose floor. The cast is eight; the boy has no balloons and the grandmother no lines. There is no functional crowd and no character who earns no keep. Danglars is mentioned once. No chronicle pages; the mandate opts out.
