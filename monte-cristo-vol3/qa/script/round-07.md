VERDICT: REVISE

## Cold read

**The story as I followed it.** Twenty-three years after the three men sent Edmond Dantès to If, with Fernand already dead, the Count takes Haydée into a garden at Auteuil. He shows her where Villefort buried a newborn he thought was dead, and smiles. The baby lived. He is Benedetto, twenty-one, a thief who has escaped from the galleys and killed a guard. The Count gives him a purse, a ring and the name Prince Andrea Cavalcanti, and promises him dinner with the King's Attorney. He watches Villefort in court. Then he stops the runaway carriage (his own half-broken horses, sent down his own hill). He brings the boy back with one red drop and hands the vial to Madame de Villefort, knowing what he is reading in her face. Haydée catches him at it, and he does not deny it.

Inside Villefort's house:
- Valentine stands by the window and nobody looks at her.
- Noirtier speaks with his eyes. The Count tells him the truth about the letter, and Noirtier says "No" to stopping.
- The Count gives his word that Valentine has nothing to fear from him.
- At the Morrels', the red purse sits under glass. Maximilien loves Valentine, and the pleasure goes out of the Count's face.
- Haydée tells him to take the bottle back. He refuses ("I want to hear it fall") and promises Valentine will live. She answers with the boy in the carriage.

Then the dinner, the toast "To fathers," the ghost story under the tree, and the empty hole at two in the morning. The grandmother dies of warm milk. Villefort knows the face and stops his father's eyes ("Don't tell me"). Héloïse all but names Valentine to the Count's face. Haydée confronts him, and he bets Valentine's life that the poisoner will wait. His letter puts Benedetto in irons, and Villefort will prosecute his own son.

The chocolate. Noirtier's medicine saved her. The door through the wall, the red drops in the glass, the white pastille, the funeral, the tomb, the sleeper who does not wake. The cell and the date. The ultimatum on the stair. The trial ("Monsieur, my father is you." / "You didn't look."). The carriage race, the vial falling down the white steps, the hussar outside the closed door, "I am Edmond Dantès," "He's dead, Villefort," the digging. Then the snow: he cannot say the next word. Noirtier wishes he had stopped him. The pistol case and one month. A hand closes.

I could paraphrase every page after one read. That is the first thing to say about this script.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p24.** "Do you know who?" / Yes. *How?* He is paralysed in a ground-floor room at the back. The old lady died in a guest room. I flagged it and went on. It came back on p28 (his terror at the chocolate is the terror of a man who knows), on p30 (he has dosed Valentine since the grandmother died, so he knew both the poisoner and the next target), and on p36 (he spells her name). The book never answers it. This is Finding 1.
- **p25.** "Unless something happened to her." I had to work out for myself that Valentine's money would pass to the boy. That was quick, but it was a step (Observation 1).
- **p29.** How did Maximilien hear within hours, when the romance is secret and conducted through bars? (Observation 2.)
- **p31.** "He waited three nights. … She had not waited a week." For a moment I read the second sentence as being about the night visit, not the chocolate (Observation 4).
- **p9.** "Her husband owes me his door." I paused on the figure; p10 resolves it at once. Not a finding.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a cruel, funny, propulsive book, and the Count is hungry on nearly every page he is on until the book deliberately takes the hunger away. I was reading for the next page, not auditing.

**Movement I (pp 1–8).** The best opening in the series that I have read. "And you're smiling. Who was he?" is Haydée in five words. The cellar has real wants on both sides. Benedetto's "You do have a sense of humour" is a voice you could pick out with the names stripped. p8 is the engine in a single panel: her eyes on the vial, his on her, "You may need it one day."

- Heat on p8: he is deciding to arm her, and she is deciding to want it.
- Appetite: total.

**Movement II (pp 9–18).** Haydée is the conscience who never scolds, and both her scenes have real heat: she wants him to say it plainly, and he wants not to. "That is Villefort's problem" is a door shutting. Noirtier's "No" to stopping makes him an accomplice, which pays off on p54. The Morrel pages are the book's only warmth. "He'd want something better done with it than that" is subtext at full strength: the Count is talking about himself, to the man who doesn't know it. On p16 the pleasure drains out of his face, and on p17 he chooses the fall anyway. The appetite bends and does not break, as the mandate asks.

**Movement III (pp 19–27).** Delight, then the first bill.
- p19, Valentine and Maximilien at the gate: "pretending to look at snails" / "Always. That's the trouble with you." They are alive and funny, and the reader wants them together. That is the stake for Movement IV.
- p20, "Then your father was a fool." / "To fathers.": dramatic irony that pays.
- p22: the empty hole and the face at the window.
- p24: Villefort holding up his hand against his father's eyes is the best characterisation of him in the book.
- p25: Héloïse saying the worst thing most gently, while the boy presses the hussar into the Count's hand.
- p26: Haydée's "Did you see the milk?" lands. His answer deflects onto Villefort, and that deflection is the subtext.

**Movement IV (pp 28–39).** Dread, done well.
- p28 is terrifying: a statue whose eyes are screaming.
- p30's "I know because I gave it to her. And I will still have your son." keeps the appetite alive inside the rescue.
- p33–34: "She will be kind to you every night until you are dead" and "Your father will bury you, the way he buries everything" are the Count enjoying himself while he saves a life. That is exactly right.
- "He will have to look at me then" pays off p10.
- p37's "You promised me" hurts.

The softest stretch is p31: necessary mechanics, carried in narration, as they should be.

**Movement V (pp 40–47).** The trial plays as a set piece with a ratchet: name, age, date, place, father. "You didn't look" closes the circle opened by "He didn't stop to listen" on p2. p41's "You have your poison. Use it," followed by the kiss on the boy's hair, is the moment Villefort becomes frightening and pitiable at once.

**Movement VI (pp 48–56).** Horror, earned.
- "I couldn't leave him behind. Not in a house with your name on it."
- The hussar outside the door.
- "You dug under my tree at two in the morning … and never once asked who I was."
- The vial held up empty.
- "I'll dig this one up too."
- p54's second "Yes" is the volume's moral fork, spoken by the one man who told him not to stop.

The book earns its ending.

**Is it any good?** Yes. One causal gap keeps it from approval, and it sits under the book's cleverest character.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p23 / p24 (load-bearing on pp 28, 30, 36). Noirtier knows who the poisoner is, but the reader never sees him find out.**

- **The failure.** On p24, Villefort asks "Someone in this house did it. Do you know who?" and Noirtier answers yes. Everything that follows rests on that knowledge:
  - his terror as Valentine drinks Héloïse's chocolate (p28);
  - his decision to dose Valentine with his medicine "every morning since Grandmother died" (p30), which means he knew both the poisoner and her next target;
  - his spelling of HÉLOÏSE (p36).

  The only plant is "he hears everything" (p11). p23 puts Héloïse in the guest-room doorway, and Noirtier is not in that scene. The grandmother died upstairs. The man who knows is paralysed in a room at the back of the ground floor.
- **What the reader must repair.** At p24 a first-time reader asks "how does he know?" and has to supply an answer: he overheard, he guessed, he is just clever. The book sells Noirtier as the cleverest person in the house. Here he works like an oracle instead of a witness. That is "a character acts on information the reader never saw them acquire," and it falls on the pages the Noirtier thread depends on. A reader who has had to guess here will not trust Noirtier's later signals in the same way.
- **The fix.** Show him acquire it, in story, before p24.
  - *Best:* one or two sentences in p23's prose, which has room within the prose budget. For example: "Madame de Villefort carried the cup up herself. On the way she stopped in Noirtier's room — where nobody ever looked — and tipped something red into the milk, in front of the one man in the house who could not tell." That one stroke also explains why he recognises the substance on p30 (red drops, the same bitterness as his own medicine). It makes Héloïse's contempt for the old man visible, and it turns "he hears everything" into dramatic irony.
  - *Alternative:* a short dramatic beat on p24 before Villefort's question, in which Noirtier's eyes go to a cup or to the reticule. Only use this if the image can carry it without depending on a tiny prop.

  Do not solve it with a caption that merely asserts he knows.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p25, the inheritance chain.** "Unless something happened to her, of course" assumes the reader works out that Valentine's money goes to her father and then to the boy. It is quick, but it is a step. The mandate allows inheritance to be said once, as a want. Héloïse can finish the thought in her own voice: "…unless something happened to her, of course. Then it would all come home to her papa — and one day to my poor boy." The p23 clause "now belonged to Valentine" stays as it is.
2. **p29, how Maximilien knows.** The romance runs through bars, yet he has the doctor's verdict within hours. One clause would close it: "Her maid ran down to the gate to tell me."
3. **Text load on pp 26 and 30.**
   - p30 carries about 102 words in 8 balloons across 5 panels.
   - p26 carries about 104 words in 6 balloons.

   Both are inside the ~105 ceiling with protected lanes and need nothing cut. The packets must reserve balloon lanes, and nobody should trim causal language to fit.
4. **p31, last line.** "He had bet that she would wait. She had not waited a week." follows "He waited three nights," so it can briefly read as being about the night visit. Move it to the head of that paragraph: "He had bet that she would wait; she had not waited a week. She would not try the chocolate twice…"
5. **p2 caption.** "Only Haydée lived in the Count's house with him" reads literally as though he has no servants, and p31 has workmen. "Of everyone in the Count's house, only Haydée knew who he really was" keeps the purpose and drops the oddity.
6. **p50, density.** Héloïse's body, the vial pickup and the volume's identity reveal share one page. The script puts the vial in a 20% opening panel and makes the reveal dominant, which is right. The page packet should keep panel 1 a quiet establishing beat so that "I am Edmond Dantès" owns the page.
7. **Five-word audit.** Every balloon of five words or fewer is either a complete thought or the direct answer to a complete question in the same panel, with one possible referent:
   - pp 2, 3: "What was in the box?", "And the child?", "Villefort's son."
   - p4: "Who's asking?"
   - p6: "No. I don't imagine you have."
   - p9: "To whom?", "That is Villefort's problem."
   - p12: "I'll be just outside, Grandfather.", "I was the sailor."
   - pp 15, 16: "Why hopelessly?", "Who is her father?"
   - p17: "I know where she sleeps."
   - p19: "Forgive me?", "Always. That's the trouble with you."
   - p20: "To fathers.", "Then your father was a fool."
   - p24: "No. Then who —"
   - p33: "Who would do that?"
   - p34: "Maximilien trusts me. Do you?", "He does. So I will."
   - p36: "H.", "Héloïse? My wife?"
   - p37: "You promised me."
   - p40: "Him? The one who buried me?", "My lord, I wouldn't miss it."
   - p43: "Your age?", "Where?"
   - p44: "Monsieur, my father is you."
   - p45: "You buried me alive, Father.", "You didn't look."
   - p48: "Where is my son?"
   - p50: "Who's there?", "I am Edmond Dantès."
   - p55: "One month."
   - p56: "She's holding on."

   Every spoken Noirtier "Yes." and "No." (pp 12, 13, 24, 30, 36, 54) is uniquely determined by the convention established on p11. None of these balloons is merely compressed; several are the strongest lines in the book.
8. **Mode balance.** 38 dramatic, 11 illustrated prose, 7 spectacle: 68 / 20 / 12. That is inside the mandate's lean, and above its 18% prose floor. Every consequential beat has its aftermath, including the Count's (pp 53–54). Haydée's response to the boy's death is a single look at the hussar on p56. That restraint suits her ledger ("she does not scold"), and I would not add to it.
