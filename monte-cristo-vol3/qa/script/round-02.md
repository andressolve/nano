## 1. Cold read

**What happens, as a first-time reader who has read Volumes I and II:**

- **The setup.** Twenty-three years ago three men sent Edmond Dantès to the Château d'If. Fernand is dead. The second man is Villefort, the King's Attorney, the one who knew Edmond was innocent. The Count takes Haydée into the garden of a house he has bought at Auteuil and shows her a patch of bare earth under a plane tree. Twenty-one years ago Villefort buried a newborn there, thinking it was dead. A hidden man dug the box up and found the baby breathing, and years later confessed it to "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count. The child is Villefort's son by another woman: now twenty-one, a thief, and a murderer.
- **The pieces go on the board.** The Count finds Benedetto in a cellar and makes him "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti". He watches Villefort win a case. He stops a runaway carriage carrying Villefort's wife and small son (horses he sold them on purpose), revives the boy with one red drop, and hands the vial to a woman who plainly wants it.
- **The household.** Villefort's house holds a cold father, a sweet stepmother, Valentine (whom nobody looks at), and Noirtier, paralysed, who speaks with his eyes and was the man Edmond's letter was addressed to. The Count tells Noirtier who he is, and gives his word that Valentine has nothing to fear from him. Then he learns that Maximilien Morrel loves Valentine.
- **The poisonings.** The Count holds a dinner where Villefort shakes his son's hand, tells a ghost story under the tree, and watches Villefort dig an empty hole at 2 a.m. The grandmother arrives and dies, and Valentine inherits. Villefort refuses to hear the poisoner's name. The Count tips off the police and Benedetto is arrested.
- **Valentine.** She is poisoned and survives, because Noirtier has been dosing her with his own medicine. The Count cuts a door into her wardrobe, catches the stepmother in the act, and gives Valentine a false death. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE for his son. At the funeral Maximilien says "You promised me." The Count lifts Valentine out of the tomb, and she does not wake.
- **The trial.** The Count gives Benedetto his father's name and a date. Villefort tells his wife to use her bottle, then goes to court. Benedetto names his father and Villefort confesses.
- **The bill.** The wife is dead on the stair and has taken the boy with her. The Count says "I am Edmond Dantès" at the dead child's door. Villefort goes mad and digs in his own garden. The Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine is alive, asks Maximilien for one month, and Valentine's hand closes.

I could tell this story back after one read, which is real praise for a book this tangled.

**Where I stopped, reread, or guessed:**

1. **p17.** The Count decides to let the house burn, and I am *told* it in three sentences of prose. I stopped because this is his biggest choice in the book, and nobody in the room argued with him.
2. **p24.** "If anything happened to her, it would all go to her father, and one day to your son." I reread this. Is he warning her that he knows, or pointing her at Valentine? The page-turn note says "he said nothing", but he plainly said something. I could not tell what the Count wanted on the page where it matters most morally.
3. **p34.** "H É L O Ï S E". I did not know whose name that was until "My wife?" in the next balloon. Nobody in the book has ever called Madame de Villefort Héloïse. Also: "stared at the bell all morning". What bell?
4. **p3.** "A man was hiding behind that wall." Why? This was a small snag, not a full stop.
5. **p39.** "You have your little bottle." How does Villefort know about a bottle? Noirtier spelled a name, not a bottle. This was a small snag.
6. **p49.** "There is nothing left in it." My first thought was that the Count made the stuff, so surely he has more at home. This was a small snag.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes, and mostly very. The Count is hungry from the first page: smiling over a baby's grave, "To fathers", "It was very cheap. Nobody wanted it", "Keep it, madame. You may need it one day." That is the engine, on the page, with relish. I wanted to turn nearly every page.

**Movement I (pp 1–8): alive.**
- The cellar (pp 4–5) is the best scene so far. Benedetto's insolence ("That chair's taken, my lord") and the Count's "I've come to make you some" are two different voices with two real wants.
- The vial hand-off (p8) is chilling, and the Count's appetite is visible in it.
- pp 2–3 lean on Haydée as a question-feeder ("What was in the box?" / "Who was he?" / "And the child?"). The Count's pleasure carries it. Haydée's own want there is thin (nonblocking, N1).

**Movement II (pp 9–17): alive until its last page.**
- p9 has real heat: "There was a child in that carriage." / "And now his mother owes me her son."
- The Noirtier pages (pp 11–13) are superb. A man confessing his identity to the one listener who cannot repeat it, and the old man's eyes saying *don't stop*, is the book's best invention.
- p16 lands: "Of every house in Paris, it had to be that one."
- Then p17 hands the movement's turn to narration. The Count makes his central moral choice alone, in prose, with no one opposing him. That is the one place in the first half where I felt the book *reporting* rather than playing (Finding 1).

**Movement III (pp 18–25): the best movement in the book.**
- The gate scene is alive and funny ("pretending to look at snails"; "I want you").
- "Then your father was a fool." / "To fathers." is perfect dramatic irony.
- The empty hole at 2 a.m. is a great spectacle page.
- Villefort's "Stop. Don't tell me" is the whole character in one gesture.
- p24 has the right idea: the sleeping boy, the soldier given and given back. But it stumbles on whose mouth says the want (Finding 2).

**Movement IV (pp 26–37): alive, tense, well paced.**
- The wardrobe, the red threads in the glass, "She will be kind to you every night until you are dead", "Your father will bury you, the way he buries everything." The Count's appetite survives the danger to Valentine, as the mandate demands: "And I will still have your son."
- p34 is the spelling scene, and it is the one page in this movement that does not work as written (Finding 3).
- The funeral is restrained and hurts.

**Movement V (pp 38–45): excellent.**
- The trial is a true detonation, paced across four pages.
- "You didn't look" pays off p2's "He didn't stop to listen."
- p45's carriage is a model aftermath page.

**Movement VI (pp 46–54): earned.**
- The hussar outside the door, "I did what you told me, monsieur", the vial come home empty, Villefort digging in the wrong garden.
- p51 is the right page in the right mode.
- The ending is quiet and correct.

**Appetite across the whole book:** it holds. I found no stretch of more than a page where the Count reads as serene, until it is supposed to break in Movement VI.

The blockers below are few and cheap. They sit exactly at the volume's two moral hinges (pp 17 and 24) and its key reveal (p34), which is why they block.

## 3. Mandatory findings

### 1. p17: the Count's central choice is narrated, with no opposition; Haydée's want goes unspent

**Failure.** Movement II's turn, per the architecture, is that "the Count learns his poison is in the house of the girl Morrel's son loves — and chooses to guard her rather than stop." On the page this arrives as prose: "He could have stopped it that night… He did not. Villefort's house was going to fall, and the Count wanted to hear it fall."

This is the most consequential choice the protagonist makes in the volume, and it is reported, not chosen. Pacing rule 2 ("Important decisions receive enough panels and dialogue to feel chosen rather than reported") applies, and so does the enacted-not-narrated test.

No one in the room wants anything from him. Haydée is the one character whose ledger want is "to make him say it plainly", and she is in the same house and absent.

**What the reader has to repair.** The reader has to accept that the Count weighed two promises (his word to Noirtier, his love for Maximilien) against his appetite, and chose the appetite, without seeing him pay anything for it. The choice feels decided offstage. Its heat (he could take the bottle back *tonight*) is never between two people. And at p24 the reader has no enacted promise to measure his conduct against.

**Revision.** Add a dramatic page before the present p17 (the book grows to 55; length is never a reason to compress):
- Champs-Élysées, night. Haydée has heard about Valentine and Maximilien, and she wants him to take the bottle back.
- She says it plainly: "Go back tonight and ask her for it. You know where it is."
- He refuses *with appetite*, in his own voice. He wants to hear the house fall.
- Then he makes the promise out loud, in a full sentence: whatever happens in that house, Valentine walks out of it.
- Let her land one blunt line he cannot answer.

Keep the present p17 after it as the illustrated-prose aftermath (his hand on the desk, Danglars planted). That preserves the mode balance at 20% prose and gives Haydée a want-from scene in the middle of the book.

### 2. p24: the Count appears to point the poisoner at Valentine, the opposite of p17's promise; Héloïse's want is never in her own mouth

**Failure.** The inheritance chain is spoken by the Count: "And now Valentine is an heiress. If anything happened to her, it would all go to her father, and one day to your son." It is spoken "pleasantly, as if discussing the weather", to the poisoner, two pages before she poisons Valentine.

The page-turn note says "she has as good as told him who is next, he said nothing". The dialogue contradicts that. *He* told *her*.

**What the reader has to repair.** The reader cannot tell what the Count wants from her in this room:
- If it is a warning ("I see you"), nothing on the page marks it as one.
- If it is a goad, he has just aimed her at the girl he swore to Noirtier, and on p17 to himself, to protect. The book then never acknowledges that act: p28's "It was my bottle" and p51's guilt both name only the bottle.

Either way the reader has to *solve* the protagonist's intent at the moral hinge of the volume.

There is a second cost. Héloïse is "the most loving mother in the book", who "speaks of her son constantly" and "says the worst things most gently". Yet she never once voices her want. The volume mandate asks for exactly this line "in one character's mouth, as a want", and its own example ("Valentine is worth a million now. My son is worth nothing.") is plainly hers.

**Revision.** Give the chain to Héloïse, as a mother's sweet worry, while stroking the sleeping boy. Something like:
- "And now Valentine is an heiress — all of it, hers. My poor boy will have nothing, unless… one mustn't think such things."

The Count says nothing. Stage his silence as the event: a held look, the poised right hand still. That makes the page-turn true ("she has as good as told him who is next, and he said nothing"), and it matches the culpability the book acknowledges later. If the Count needs a line, make it unmistakably double-edged and in his voice, for example "You are careful with it, I hope?" moved after her speech.

Keep panel 4 (the hussar given and given back) exactly as it is. It is the best beat on the page.

### 3. p34: the spelled name is one the reader has never heard; "the bell" has no referent

**Failure.**
- Madame de Villefort is only ever "Madame de Villefort" in everything that renders on pp 7–33. Her first name appears only in non-rendering speaker labels. The first time the reader meets "Héloïse" is as the letters "H É L O Ï S E" on Noirtier's paper. So the page's turn depends on the very next balloon ("Héloïse? My wife?") to decode it. A spelled-out denunciation should land on the letters, not on a gloss.
- Panel 1, "They tell me you have stared at the bell all morning", refers to a bell that is staged nowhere and set up nowhere (see `05-SETTINGS-AND-OBJECTS.md`). A listener asks "What bell?"

**What the reader has to repair.** At the spelling, the reader stares at an unfamiliar word and waits to be told whose it is, so the reveal lands a balloon late. Separately, the reader has to invent the bell's purpose.

**Revision.**
- Let Villefort call his wife by name at least once, early and in passing: p20, "Come, Héloïse, you are cold. Good night, monsieur."; and/or p39, "Héloïse, I know what you did…".
- Optionally, the Count can use it once to her on p24.
- On p34, replace "the bell" with something drawn and already established, such as the door Valentine went out by, or Valentine's empty footstool: "They tell me you have stared at Valentine's stool all morning, Father."

## 4. Nonblocking observations

**N1. pp 2–3, Haydée is a question-feeder.** Four of her six lines are prompts. Give her one line that wants something from him. For example, after "A baby": "And you're smiling." It is in her blunt voice and plants p9.

**N2. p3, the hidden witness has no motive.** "A man was hiding behind that wall." Add one clause so a reader doesn't stop on why: "a man who had come to settle a quarrel with Villefort".

**N3. p39, how Villefort knows about the bottle.** "You have your little bottle." Villefort has never seen it or been told of it. Either "You have your poison. Use it." or one prior beat (he has searched her reticule). Small, and cheap at this gate.

**N4. p49, the "more at home" question.** "There is nothing left in it." Add a clause that closes it: "…and it would be too late if there were. He's dead, Villefort."

**N5. pp 34–39, a three-day gap.** Villefort learns the poisoner's name, then lives three days in the house with her and his son before the ultimatum. That fits his character (he buries). One clause on p39 would make it a choice rather than a gap: "I have waited three days for you to spare me this."

**N6. p26, the caption repeats the image.** "Noirtier could not move. He could not call out. He could only watch." says what the image already shows. Consider cutting it, or replacing it with something the picture cannot say.

**N7. Staging notes contradict each other.** p10 says Villefort's hand on the boy is "the only warm gesture he makes in the whole book"; p39 says his voice is gentle "for the only time in the book". These notes never render, but they will reach the builder. Harmonise them.

**N8. p24 page-turn note.** Once Finding 2 is fixed, check that the note still matches the dialogue.

**N9. Valentine's secondary want.** The ledger says she wants "to be looked at once by her father". It exists only in p10's prose. One beat on p31 would pay it with no cost: "Then I'll tell my father in the morning. He'll have to look at me then."

**N10. Text budget.** pp 3, 31 and 38 each run about 90–95 words in 6–7 balloons. They are within the ~105 ceiling, and each has one clear dominant panel. Builders should reserve balloon lanes first. Every prose page is within 50–125 words.

**N11. Mode balance.** 36 dramatic, 11 prose, 7 spectacle, which is 67 / 20 / 13 and within the mandate's lean. If Finding 1 adds a dramatic page, it becomes 37 / 11 / 7 and prose stays at 20%. No more than five dramatic pages run consecutively.

**N12. Page architecture.** Every page has exactly one mode, one dominant panel, and at most one location (p45 is one carriage). There are no montage pages and no functional crowds. Cast is eight named characters plus the unnamed boy, within the ceiling. Danglars is mentioned once.

Each is a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before it, with one possible referent. Notes on a few:
- **Noirtier's "Yes." / "No."** Every instance follows a complete question in the same panel and is spoken by the asker. The convention is taught on p11 and readable without notes.
- **p32, "And Maximilien?"** The object is slightly open ("will he know?"). It passes because the Count's deflection is the point. Optionally, "And Maximilien? Will he know?"
- **p34, "Héloïse? My wife?"** This is the one that fails, and it is covered by Finding 3.
