VERDICT: REVISE

# Script Gate — Round 01 — *The Count of Monte Cristo, Volume III: The King's Attorney*

Critic: fresh, read-only. I read `08-FULL-SCRIPT.md` cold, start to finish, before opening anything else. After that I read `method/00-CRAFT-MANDATE.md`, `method/02-SCRIPT-LOOP.md`, and the volume's `00`, `01`, `03`, `04` and `05`. There is no page contract yet. I checked the parent Volume II script once, for a single continuity question: where "One." was said, and whether the Count's refusal to eat had been set up. I did not consult any source text.

---

## 1. Cold read

**What I understood, as a reader who has read Volumes I and II:**

The Count has bought a house at Auteuil. Under its plane tree, twenty-one years ago, Villefort buried a newborn he thought was dead. A hidden man dug the child up alive and later confessed it to "Abbé Busoni", who was the Count. That child is Benedetto: a card cheat, and a convict who killed a guard escaping Toulon. The Count gives him a fortune and the name Prince Andrea Cavalcanti, and promises him his father's name later. The Count watches Villefort win in court and meets him there.

Runaway horses come down the hill to the Count's gate. The Count stops them, and one red drop from his vial revives a limp boy. The boy's mother asks what more than one drop would do. "Ten drops would stop a heart." He gives her the vial. Then we learn he sold those horses to her husband on purpose. She is Madame de Villefort.

Inside Villefort's cold house the Count meets three people: Valentine, the daughter nobody looks at; Noirtier, the paralysed grandfather who talks with his eyes; and Héloïse. He tells Noirtier who he is, and that he will take his son apart. Noirtier does not ask him to stop. He only asks about Valentine, and the Count promises she is safe. Then, at the Morrels', Maximilien says he loves Valentine. The Count decides to let the house burn and carry her out of it.

At the Auteuil dinner Villefort shakes his son's hand ("To fathers."). The Count tells a ghost story under the tree, and Villefort comes back at 2 a.m. and digs an empty hole. Valentine's grandmother arrives and dies, and Valentine inherits. Villefort recognises poison, learns from Noirtier that someone in the house did it and that it was not Valentine, and then refuses to hear the name. Héloïse nearly tells the Count what she has done. The Count's anonymous letter gets Benedetto arrested, and Villefort will prosecute him.

Valentine collapses and survives. Noirtier has been dosing her against the poison. The Count cuts a door into her wardrobe from the empty house next door, watches Héloïse pour the drops, and gives Valentine a death-like sleep. She is buried. Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE. Maximilien says "You promised me" at the tomb. The Count lifts her out of the coffin, and she does not wake.

The Count tells Benedetto his father's name in the cell. Villefort tells his wife to use her little bottle before he gets home. In court Benedetto names him, and Villefort confesses. He races home: his wife is dying on the stair, she has taken the boy with her, and the tin hussar lies outside a closed door. The Count says "I am Edmond Dantès." Villefort goes mad and digs in his own garden. The Count cannot say "Two." He tells Noirtier that Valentine is alive, asks Maximilien for a month, and Valentine's hand closes.

**Where I stopped, reread or guessed** (these notes feed the findings below):

1. **p2**, "Haydée, whose father Fernand had betrayed and sold." On first parse I read "her father, Fernand" and had to reread.
2. **p2–3.** Why would a man bury his own newborn? Whose baby was it? The script never says. I guessed a scandal.
3. **p7–8.** Who is this woman with the boy? Nothing that renders tells me until the last balloon of p9 ("That is Villefort's problem"), and only p10's prose confirms it. The poison handoff on p8, which is the volume's key image, played for me as "a stranger's mother wants poison." The irony arrived a page and a half late.
4. **p13.** Why does a father want his son "taken apart"? I had to reach back to Volume I's politics.
5. **p16**, "Of every house in Paris." I had to finish the idiom myself.
6. **p23**, panel 5, "No. Don't tell me." The book has just taught me that a spoken "No." is Noirtier's answer, and for a moment I read this one as his.
7. **p24.** Why does Valentine's fortune matter to Héloïse? I had to build the inheritance chain myself: grandmother, then Valentine, then Villefort, then the boy.
8. **p28**, "You've been giving her some of it every day." He can't move. How? And is the lemon-water on his table the poison that killed the grandmother? It's the same drink.
9. **p29–32.** He has a secret door into her room. Why not just carry her out through it? And why keep her in the dark on p28 when she's lying right there?
10. **p34**, panel 4. Villefort's "Yes." has no question in front of it and no blink beside it.
11. **p41.** How does Benedetto know he was born at midnight on 27 September 1817?
12. **p44 onward.** What happens to Benedetto? He vanishes from the book.
13. **p46 → p49.** Héloïse drops the empty vial on the stair. Then the Count walks out of the boy's room holding "his own cut-crystal vial, the gold stopper still in." He gave his vial away on p8. Are there two? Did he try it? Is the boy dead? Nothing says so in words until p52.
14. **p51**, "Outside Fernand's house… he had looked up at this very window." I had to remember Volume II's last page exactly to work out how Fernand's house and Villefort's window are the same place.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes, mostly, and in places it is very good. This is a real script with a real appetite in it, and I wanted to turn most of its pages. It is not approvable yet. The failures cluster in two places: the mechanics of the poison plot (Movement IV), and the boy, whose death is the whole bill and who never becomes a person.

**Movement I (pp 1–8): alive.** The Count enjoying himself under the tree ("He is enjoying this") sets the register straight away. In the cellar Benedetto is a delight: "That chair's taken, my lord. Unless you've come down here to lose money too." "A prosecutor? You do have a sense of humour." His voice survives the attribution-strip test easily. The court page is cold and precise: "No. I don't imagine you have." is subtext doing exactly what it should. The horses and the vial make a great set piece with a great last exchange: "And if someone gave more than one drop?" / "Ten drops would stop a heart…" Heat: a woman is asking a stranger for a murder weapon, and he is reading her and handing it over. The one hole is that I didn't know who she was (Finding 3). Haydée on p2–3 is mostly a questioner ("What was in the box?" "Who was he?" "And the child?"). The scene survives on the Count's relish, but she is close to a feed (observation N1).

**Movement II (pp 9–17): the best movement.** Haydée's "There was a child in that carriage." against "And now his mother owes me her son, and her husband owes me his door" has real heat: she is testing whether he still has a line, and he shows her he doesn't. The Noirtier pages are the book's discovery. The eye convention is taught cleanly, "He doesn't say yes to many people" is charming, and "I was the sailor" lands. Want-from is excellent: the Count wants a witness, Noirtier wants Valentine safe. Maximilien is warm and his voice is distinct ("I'll give him my life. I mean it."). "He'd want something better done with it than that" is the Count speaking about himself without saying so, which is first-rate subtext. p16, where the pleasure goes out of the Count's face, is the right hinge, but its line is under-built (Finding 6). p17 narrates the Count's decision rather than enacting it. Prose may carry a private belief, and here it earns that.

**Movement III (pp 18–25): alive, with a thin love story.** "Then your father was a fool." / "To fathers." is the best two-line exchange in the book. Villefort with the spade in the dark is a great silent image. Villefort's "No. Don't tell me." on p23 is the volume's finest characterisation of him: a man choosing to bury the truth, enacted in front of us. Héloïse on p24, "And my son will have nothing at all, unless God is kind to him. Isn't that strange?" / "Very strange, madame.", is chilling and has the right subtext, but the money logic is missing underneath it (Finding 9). The gate scene (p18) is the only time the lovers share a page, and it is mostly news: grandmother, Tuesday, a husband. I don't come away rooting for them (Finding 7). And Villefort walks into the very house where he buried his child without a flicker (Finding 2).

**Movement IV (pp 26–37): the set pieces work, but the Count goes serene and the plan goes illegible.** The chocolate, "Bitter, like your medicine —", and Noirtier frozen watching her fall make a strong page. The wardrobe crack, "She will be kind to you every night until you are dead.", and "Die." are all great. But from p27 to p36 the Count is a calm, competent rescuer. He diagnoses, buys a house, waits, saves, carries. For ten pages there is no appetite and no visible guilt. This is exactly the "merely clever, or merely just" failure the mandate names as a blocker. His one appetite beat is narrated after the fact on p37 ("smiling"). Meanwhile the mechanics a ten-year-old will interrogate are left for the reader to solve: how a paralysed man doses anyone, why the secret door isn't the exit, and why Valentine is kept ignorant (Findings 10 and 11). p34's spelling scene and p35's "You promised me." are strong.

**Movement V (pp 38–45): excellent.** This is the book's engine at full power. "Him? The one who buried me?" / "My lord, I wouldn't miss it." "You have your little bottle. Use it." The cross-examination is cut perfectly: name, age, birthplace, father. "You buried me alive, Father." / "It wasn't breathing. I thought it was dead —" / "You didn't look." calls back to p2's "He didn't stop to listen" and is the best use of a five-word balloon in the script. The carriage prose is properly terrifying. One defect: Benedetto's knowledge of his birth date comes from nowhere, and he is dropped the moment his line is spoken (Finding 12).

**Movement VI (pp 46–54): powerful, but the bill is paid in the abstract.** "I couldn't leave him behind. Not in a house with your name on it." is devastating. The hussar outside the door is the right image, and the Count's reveal over a kneeling Villefort is monstrous in the right way. But I felt the boy's death less than I should have, and the cause is upstream. He has been a prop: an unnamed child holding a toy, met only in description and one line of prose. His father's love for him is a single silent panel (p39). The volume's moral weight rests on a person the reader never met (Finding 4). Then p49, the moment the Count sees the bill, is muddied by a second vial and carried by one line (Finding 13). His crisis is narrated on p51, which the architecture intends and which is legitimate as aftermath, but only if p49 has already put it on his face. p52–54 land well: "She's holding on." is a fine double meaning to close on.

**Is this any good?** Yes. Movements I, II and V would hold up next to Volume II. But a book whose bill is "a boy who had done nothing" has to make me know the boy. A thriller whose middle is mechanics has to make those mechanics legible. A volume whose named failure mode is the serene avenger cannot spend ten pages with the Count as a calm doctor. Fix those and this is premium.

## 3. Mandatory findings

### Finding 1 — p2, caption: Haydée's re-introduction misparses
- **The failure:** "Only one person lived in the Count's house with him: Haydée, whose father Fernand had betrayed and sold."
- **Reader harm:** Read naturally, this can parse as "her father, Fernand." That makes Fernand Haydée's father, which inverts Volume II. The sentence also says Fernand "sold" her father, not her. She is a returning face, and this is her only re-introduction.
- **Revise to:** "Only one person lived in the Count's house with him: Haydée. Fernand had betrayed her father to his death and sold her as a slave. The Count had bought her freedom, and he told her everything."

### Finding 2 — p2–3, with p19–20 and p43: the burial has no motive, and Villefort shows no reaction to the house
- **The failure:** Nothing in rendered text says why Villefort buried a newborn, or whose child it was. The nearest is the Count's "Someone with a great deal to lose, I imagine" (p20).
- **Reader harm:** A ten-year-old's first question about "a man buried a baby" is *why?*, and the reader has to invent a scandal. Separately, on p19 Villefort arrives at the very house where he did it, and the reader knows that from p2. He shows nothing until the ghost story. That reads either as a gap (he doesn't recognise the house?) or as a missing beat.
- **Revise:**
  - **p3:** after "Villefort's son.", add a Count line: "Born to a lady who was not his wife. A child like that would have ended the career of a rising young King's Attorney, so he got rid of it." The mother stays unnamed, which keeps the cast ceiling intact.
  - **p19, panel 1:** give Villefort one stiff line as he enters, for example: "I did not know you had bought this house, monsieur." The Count answers: "It was very cheap. Nobody wanted it." The ghost story then lands on a man who has already turned pale.

### Finding 3 — p7–9: Madame de Villefort is unidentified when she receives the poison
- **The failure:** p7 is silent. On p8 she is "madame". p9 refers to "her husband" and ends with "That is Villefort's problem." p10's prose is the first rendered text that names her.
- **Reader harm:** The brief promises the reader will know "from page 8 that the vial in Madame de Villefort's hand came from the Count." As written, the reader doesn't know whose hand it is. The book's central act plays as a stranger scene and has to be re-read after p10. This is "recoverable after looking back", which is a revision flag.
- **Revise:**
  - **p7:** add a caption to panel 1: "Down the hill from the Count's gate, out of control, came the King's Attorney's wife and his little son."
  - **p8:** have the Count name her: "Hold his head, Madame de Villefort. Gently."
  - **p9:** this is then free to carry its own subtext.

### Finding 4 — p8, p10, p24, p39 → p46–51: the boy is never met, and the volume's cost lands in the abstract
- **The failure:** The book ends on "It cost a boy who had done nothing." The boy is unnamed and has no balloons; within the mandate's rules that is fine. But he is never *met* either:
  - p8: he wakes holding a toy.
  - p10: his showing the hussar to the Count is summarised in prose ("The boy showed him the tin hussar…").
  - p24: he is asleep.
  - p39: Villefort's kiss is one 15% silent panel.
  - The ledger's "adoring of his small son" is never enacted before the ultimatum.
- **Reader harm:** The reader is told a child died and is meant to feel it as the Count's bill. Without one enacted moment where this boy is a person to the Count, and is loved by his cold father, p47's hussar is a sad symbol rather than a loss. That is a heat and appetite failure at the book's moral climax.
- **Revise (no names, no balloons, as the mandate requires):**
  - **p10:** stage the hussar moment as an image instead of a prose clause. The boy holds the hussar up to the Count; the Count, a beat late, crouches to look at it. Villefort's hand rests on the boy's curls, the only warm gesture he makes in the book, while he never looks at Valentine. Trim the matching prose clause so the picture carries it.
  - **p24:** have the boy wake and press the hussar into the Count's hand while Héloïse talks of fortunes. The Count must hand it back.
  - **p39:** make the kiss a real beat. Give Villefort a soft line to the boy, the only one in the book: "Be good for your mother. I'll be home tonight." That turns p45's "his son was in the house with her" into a knife.

### Finding 5 — p13: Noirtier's want against his own son is unmotivated
- **The failure:** "I am going to take your son apart… Do you want me to stop?" / [quick blinks] / "No." Noirtier's eyes show "a fierce, burning interest." No rendered text says why a father would choose this.
- **Reader harm:** A seven-year-old asks: his own father says don't stop? The reader has to rebuild Volume I's politics and guess that Noirtier may only now be learning about the burned letter.
- **Revise:** give the Count one line before the question, reading the old man's eyes: "You didn't know he burned it. He told you your letter was lost, and then he put you in this room and kept you here." Or have Valentine plant it on p11: "Papa and Grandfather haven't agreed about anything since before I was born."

### Finding 6 — p16, panel 3: the volume's hinge line is under-built
- **The failure:** "Of every house in Paris." This is a five-word clipped idiom, and the panel calls it "the first time in the book the pleasure goes out of" his face.
- **Reader harm:** A listener has to finish the thought ("…what about every house in Paris?"). This is compressed, not forceful, and it carries the Count's first crack, which is a necessary line.
- **Revise to:** "Of every house in Paris, it had to be that one." Optionally add a beat for Maximilien to misread: "You look as if I'd told you she was dead."

### Finding 7 — p18 (and p22): the love story is told, not lived, and a thread dangles
- **The failure:** The lovers' only scene together is news delivery: "my mother's mother arrives on Tuesday. She's bringing a husband for me." Valentine never says what she feels. "Let me talk to Grandfather first" never pays off. The grandmother's "list of young men" disappears with her, and nobody tells the reader the arranged marriage died too.
- **Reader harm:** The brief promises "a love story worth rooting for." Maximilien's grief at the tomb and his pistol case on p53 depend on a love the reader saw for six balloons. Want-from on p18 is mostly "to inform."
- **Revise:**
  - **p18:** open with the two of them being in love before the news: a joke through the bars, his fingers caught, her dry humour. For example: "You're late. I've been standing in the ivy for an hour pretending to look at snails." Have Valentine say what she wants: "I don't want a husband from a list. I want you."
  - **p22:** add one prose clause closing the thread: "…and the young men on her list went home."

### Finding 8 — p13, p23, p34, p52: the Noirtier convention is broken by its own users
- **The failure:** The mandate says "every page with Noirtier makes the method readable without notes." Four places break it:
  - **p23, panel 5:** "No. Don't tell me." This comes straight after panel 4's relayed "No." The reader reads it as Noirtier's answer.
  - **p34, panel 4:** "Yes. I think I have known it…" There is no question before it and no blink drawn, so the reader can't tell whether Noirtier confirmed something.
  - **p13, panel 3–4:** "Your granddaughter?" gets no drawn answer before the Count's promise.
  - **p52, panel 3:** a long blink with no question and no spoken answer.
- **Reader harm:** The book teaches a clean rule (asker says the answer aloud) and then uses "Yes."/"No." as the asker's own words. Each case makes the reader stop and assign the line.
- **Revise:**
  - **p23:** "Stop. Don't tell me."
  - **p34:** make it a question with a drawn blink: "Héloïse? My wife?" / [Noirtier's long blink] / "Yes." Then: "I think I have known it since the night her grandmother died."
  - **p13:** add the long blink and the Count's "Yes." before his promise.
  - **p52:** have the Count ask "Do you understand me? She's alive." / "Yes."

### Finding 9 — p24 (planted from p22): Héloïse's motive chain is missing
- **The failure:** "And now Valentine is a great heiress." / "…my son will have nothing at all, unless God is kind to him." Nothing that renders says the fortune passes to Villefort, and through him to the boy, if Valentine dies.
- **Reader harm:** The reader has to work out the inheritance chain to understand why Valentine is next, and why the grandmother was killed first. The volume mandate itself requires the inheritance to be said "once, in one character's mouth, as a want."
- **Revise:** keep her sweetness and let the Count say the chain, so it is legible and cruel at once:
  - **COUNT:** "And if anything happened to Valentine, it would all go to her father, and one day to your son."
  - **HÉLOÏSE:** "What a dreadful thing to say, Count."

  Alternatively, use the mandate's own model line in her mouth.

### Finding 10 — p28: a paralysed man "gives" her medicine, and the lemon-water confuses
- **The failure:** "You've been giving her some of it every day since her grandmother died." Noirtier cannot move. Panel 2 also stages "the carafe of lemon-water by Noirtier's hand," and p22 says the grandmother died of a glass of lemon-water.
- **Reader harm:** The reader must invent how he did it (he asked her with his eyes to drink from his glass?). They may also conclude Noirtier's lemon-water is the poison. And they must guess that his "medicine" and the stepmother's red drops are the same substance, which is the whole point of "It won't save her twice."
- **Revise:**
  - Make the mechanism explicit: "Every morning since her grandmother died, you've asked her with your eyes to drink from your glass. Your medicine." / [long blink] / "Yes."
  - Link the substances: "It's made from the same thing that's in her stepmother's bottle. A little every day, so that a big dose can't kill her."
  - Change p22's lemon-water to "a cup of warm milk" so the two drinks never collide.

### Finding 11 — p29–32: the Count's plan is illegible
- **The failure:** He has cut a door into Valentine's wardrobe. He wakes her and says "Die… They'll bury you. I'll bring you out." Nobody says why he doesn't just take her through the door tonight. On p28 he also hides the danger from her ("so Valentine cannot hear"), and no reason is given.
- **Reader harm:** "Why doesn't he just carry her out?" is the first thing a ten-year-old will ask. Mandate §4.7, "make plans legible," is unmet, and the reader has to invent the logic of the false death.
- **Revise:**
  - **p32, after "Then what can I do?":** "If you vanish, your father will turn Paris over to find you and bring you home to her. Nobody hunts for a girl who is dead. Die."
  - **p29 prose:** add one sentence explaining the secrecy: "He did not warn her. Valentine would never believe it of her stepmother until she saw it with her own eyes."

### Finding 12 — pp 27–36: the Count's appetite is written out for ten pages
- **The failure:** From Maximilien's news (p27) to the tomb (p36), the Count is calm, wise, and efficient: he diagnoses (p28), buys and waits (p29), rescues (p31–32), says nothing (p35), carries (p36). Two things never appear on the page:
  - the guilt of "his own poison is killing" her (the p27 page-turn line);
  - his hunger for Villefort, which has to be running alongside the rescue.

  The single appetite beat ("smiling", p37) is narrated after the fact.
- **Reader harm:** This is the volume mandate's named blocker, "merely clever, or merely just, for more than a page at a stretch." The reader stops seeing the dangerous man they are reading for.
- **Revise (two enacted beats):**
  - **p28, panel 4:** Noirtier's eyes go to the Count with an accusation the Count answers aloud: "Yes. It was my bottle. I gave it to her." Then: "And I'll still have your son."
  - **p31–32:** let him enjoy the wardrobe a little ("I was standing in your wardrobe, and I watched her do it.") Have the false-death plan visibly serve both wants. After the Finding 11 line, add: "And your father will bury you, the way he buries everything." This also makes the false death of Valentine the engine at work, not a detour from it.

### Finding 13 — p38 / p41 / p44–45: Benedetto knows what he wasn't told, and gets no aftermath
- **The failure:**
  - On p41 Benedetto gives "midnight on the twenty-seventh of September, 1817." The reader never saw him learn a date; the Count told him only the garden and the box on p4, and the name on p38.
  - After "You didn't look." on p43, Benedetto vanishes. His want ("the most famous man in France") is met and never shown landing. The ledger counts him as the Count's mirror, a buried man who came back.
- **Reader harm:** The reader has to supply off-page coaching, and a major character's triumph has no consequence. Mandate §4.3 applies: stay for the consequence.
- **Revise:**
  - **p38:** have the Count supply the date: "Midnight, the twenty-seventh of September, 1817. Learn it. A good story needs a date."
  - **p44, panel 1** (the total-silence panel), or **p45's prose:** give Benedetto his last beat. For example, a prose sentence: "Behind him the gendarmes were taking the prisoner down. Benedetto was grinning up at the gallery, at the one man who had not turned to look at Villefort."

### Finding 14 — p46 → p49: two vials, an unstated death, and the Count's turn carried by one line
- **The failure:**
  - p46: "the empty cut-crystal vial slipping from her other hand."
  - p49: "In his hand is his own cut-crystal vial, the gold stopper still in." He gave his only vial away on p8. `05-SETTINGS` itself says "exactly one vial on any page."
  - "There is nothing in this that can help him now." The reader can't tell which vial this is, whether he tried it, or (in words) that the boy is dead. Nothing says so until p52.
  - The dominant panel is about the vial, not about the man seeing his bill.
- **Reader harm:** At the volume's moral climax the reader is solving prop continuity. The protagonist's single most important reaction is an ambiguous line about a bottle.
- **Revise:**
  - **p46–48:** stage the Count arriving on the stair past Héloïse and picking up her empty vial. It is his own, come back to him.
  - **p49:** make the dominant panel the Count's face in the doorway, the empty vial in his hand. Line: "One drop brought him back on the hill. There is nothing left in it. He's dead, Villefort."
  - Optionally add a small panel of the Count picking up the tin hussar from the floor. That enacts his turn before p51's prose narrates it.

### Finding 15 — p51, prose: the callback reads as a contradiction
- **The failure:** "Outside Fernand's house, a month ago, he had looked up at this very window and counted: 'One.'" Read cold, Fernand's house and Villefort's window are in conflict. Also, the time elapsed in this volume (dinner next week, Tuesday, three nights, three days, three nights, funeral, three nights) already approaches a month on its own, after an unstated gap since Volume II.
- **Reader harm:** The line that sets up "Two" makes the reader stop to reconstruct Volume II's last page.
- **Revise to:** "On the night Fernand died, he had stood outside Fernand's house, looked across the roofs at this very window, and counted: 'One.'" This drops "a month ago."

## 5. Nonblocking observations

These are kept separate from the findings above.

- **N1. p2–3, Haydée's want-from.** Her balloons are mostly prompts. Her p9 pushback rescues her, but one line of her own stake on p3 would help, for example: "After Fernand, I thought you'd be done. You look hungrier." Her secret feeling for the Count, which the brief says is "planted", appears nowhere in the script. The ledger's "hand on his sleeve" was never written.
