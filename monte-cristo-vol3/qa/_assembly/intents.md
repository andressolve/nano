# Page intents

Reader-facing goals, shown to both builder and critic.

## PAGE 1

Illustrated prose. The book opens at night outside the high garden wall of a dark, shuttered house at Auteuil. The Count of Monte Cristo stands alone at the iron gate, lantern held low, looking up at one huge old plane tree as if it were a face he knew. The title is printed over the night sky; one prose field over the dark of the lane tells the reader who he is, what was done to him, and that the second man on his list is Villefort, the King's Attorney, the one who knew Edmond was innocent and buried him anyway. The dominant turn: the Count has come back to his list, and the second name is Villefort. The reader turns because he has come to this dark house for that man. The house details, the gate's ironwork and the lane stay subordinate to the lone figure and the tree.

## PAGE 2

Dramatic. In the overgrown garden at Auteuil, under the great plane tree, the Count holds his lantern low over a circle of bare earth while Haydée, arms folded against the cold, watches him rather than the ground. A caption tells the reader who Haydée is and why she alone knows his secret. She asks plain questions; he answers that twenty-one years ago a man buried a box here with a newborn baby in it, and the Count's face, lit from below, begins to smile before he names the man: Gérard de Villefort. The dominant turn: the Count shows Haydée where Villefort buried a newborn, and smiles. The reader turns because a baby went into this ground and the Count is enjoying it. The garden walls, the house and the lantern's make stay subordinate to the bare ground and his face.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. Continuous in the night garden. Haydée turns on him: how can he know? The Count explains that a hidden enemy of Villefort dug the box up alive, and later confessed it to a priest who was the Count in disguise. Then, wide and small under the vast tree with the black house behind and the glow of Paris far off over the wall, he tells her the child lived, is twenty-one, and is a thief, a liar and a murderer; Villefort's son by a woman not his wife. Villefort does not know he has a son, and the Count means to give him back. The dominant turn: the buried baby lived, and the Count means to return him to Villefort. The reader turns because that gift is a murderer. The distant city glow stays a small light, never a scene of its own.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. A low, smoky cellar tavern in Paris. Benedetto, young, insolent, in a stained mustard-brown jacket, lays down a winning hand while two rough card players glare; the Count sits down across from him, black and still. Benedetto mocks him; the Count names him, then names his crimes: the escape from the galleys at Toulon and the guard who did not fall by himself. Benedetto's grin stays but his hand goes for the knife in his boot. Leaning into the candlelight, the Count says he knows where Benedetto was born, in a garden, in a box, and that his story has a rich father in it. The dominant turn: the Count finds a murderer born in a box and dangles a rich father. The reader turns because Benedetto now wants the name. The card players and the cellar's bottles stay background.

## PAGE 5

Dramatic. The cellar, continuous. Benedetto, interested at last, demands his father's name; the Count refuses and offers him a better name of his own. On the stained table the Count's gloved hand sets down a fat purse, a gold signet ring and an engraved white visiting card that reads Prince Andrea Cavalcanti: from tonight the convict is an Italian prince. Benedetto slides the ring on, delighted, and asks the price; the Count says the prince will dine where he is told, next week with the King's Attorney, and Benedetto laughs at the joke. The dominant turn: Benedetto becomes Prince Andrea Cavalcanti, bound for the King's Attorney's table. The reader turns because only the Count knows the prince is Villefort's son. The purse and ring stay secondary to the card.

## PAGE 6

Dramatic. The Assize court in grey winter light. Villefort, King's Attorney, in his black robe with white bands, demands twenty years in the galleys for a thin, hungry prisoner while every face is on him; a caption says he has not lost a case in eleven years. High in the gallery the Count watches. Later, the court empty, the Count comes down to Villefort's desk; Villefort greets him with cold courtesy, and the two men stand face to face across the desk exchanging polite lines that mean two different things. The dominant turn: the Count measures Villefort face to face, and Villefort has no idea who he is. The reader turns because the Count is close enough to touch the man who buried him. The prisoner, the crowd and the papers stay subordinate to Villefort's performance and the two faces.

## PAGE 7

Spectacle. On the steep walled lane at Auteuil two bay horses bolt downhill with no coachman, dragging a light open carriage in which Madame de Villefort screams and clutches her small son. A single caption tells who they are. The Count steps out of his own gateway into the road, cloak flying, seizes the lead horse's bridle with both hands and throws his weight back; the horses rear above him, a wheel smashes into the gatepost, and he does not move. Then sudden stillness: the horses heaving, the carriage tilted against the post, and over its side a small limp hand still holding a tin hussar. The dominant turn: the Count stops the runaway carriage, and the boy is limp. The reader turns because the child is not moving. Splinters and the lane's walls stay subordinate to the man in the road.

## PAGE 8

Dramatic. On the gravel inside the Count's gate, he kneels with the limp boy across his knee while Madame de Villefort, hair fallen, begs. One red drop from a small crystal vial falls on the boy's lips; his eyes fly open and he flings his arms round his mother, tin hussar still in his fist. She asks what the Count gave him. Then, face to face over the boy's head, the vial held up between them, her eyes are on the vial, wanting it, and his are on her, reading her: she asks what more than one drop would do, he tells her ten would stop a heart undetectably, and gives it to her. The dominant turn: the Count revives the boy and hands the vial to a mother he knows will use it. The reader turns because he knew. The gravel and the gate stay background to the two faces and the one vial.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic. Evening in the long dark dining room at Auteuil. The Count stands at the glass doors watching a carriage's lamps go away down the lane; Haydée rises from the table behind him and puts it to him plainly: the runaway horses were his, sold half broken to Villefort, and the hill road past his gate was his suggestion. He does not deny it. The boy was never in danger, he says, because he was standing in the road, and now the family owes him its door. Then she asks about the little bottle, and he answers that its use is Villefort's problem. The dominant turn: the Count admits he rigged the horses and armed the mother on purpose. The two of them own the page in the dark; the departing lamps stay tiny and far beyond the glass. The reader turns because on Thursday he walks into her house.

## PAGE 10

Illustrated prose. Villefort's cold grey-blue drawing room, frost light, white marble. Villefort stands rigid by the chimneypiece with his hand on his small son's gold curls; Madame de Villefort beams from a sofa; the boy holds his tin hussar up to the Count, who has crouched, a beat late, to look at it properly. By the window, apart from all of them, stands Valentine, the daughter, and nobody is looking at her. The prose tells who she is and that her father never looks at her; a small second panel shows Villefort's curt gesture toward a door, and the Count asks to meet the father Villefort would rather hide. The dominant turn: the Count enters Villefort's house and sees a cold father, a loved son, and an unseen daughter. The family tableau owns the page; Valentine stays small and apart, but plainly there.

## PAGE 11

Dramatic. Noirtier's dark green room, kept as it was in 1815. In his high-backed wheeled armchair sits Villefort's father, white-maned, utterly still; only his black eyes live, fixed on the Count in the doorway. A caption tells who he is and that young Edmond's letter was addressed to him. Valentine kneels at his chair, her hand on his, and introduces the Count, then explains to him, cheerfully, how her grandfather talks: one slow close for yes, many quick blinks for no. She asks whether he is glad the Count has come, and his eyes close, slowly, once. She speaks the meaning. The dominant turn: Noirtier, who speaks only with his eyes, is glad to see the Count. Noirtier never speaks; every word is Valentine's or the caption's. The reader turns because the Count has come to tell the old man something.

## PAGE 12

Dramatic. Noirtier's room. The Count asks Valentine to leave them alone, and she goes out promising to wait outside. He draws a chair knee to knee with the old man and, voice low, tells him about a sailor who carried a letter from Elba addressed to him in 1815, a letter that never came. Noirtier's eyes close once: he remembers. The Count speaks that yes himself. Then, closer still, the left corner of his mouth lifting, he says it: Villefort read the letter, burned it, and buried the sailor in the Château d'If for fourteen years. He was the sailor. The dominant turn: the Count tells Noirtier who burned the letter and that he was the sailor. The confession in the last panel owns the page; Valentine's exit stays small at the top. The reader turns because the one man who cannot repeat it now knows exactly who he is.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Noirtier's room. The old man's eyes burn at the Count, not with horror but with fierce interest. The Count tells him his son let him think the letter was lost, and that he means to take that son apart, piece by piece; does he want him to stop? Many quick blinks: no. The Count speaks the no. Then Noirtier's eyes move deliberately to the door Valentine went out by and stay there, and the Count understands the question and asks it aloud; one long slow close: yes. The Count's poised hand goes still, and he gives his word that she has nothing to fear from him. The dominant turn: Noirtier does not want him to stop, and the Count gives his word Valentine is safe. Noirtier's eyes own the page, and every word is the Count's. The reader turns knowing what is in Madame de Villefort's reticule.

## PAGE 14

Illustrated prose. The Morrel house in the rue Meslay, the one warm room in the book: a fire, books, a worn rug. Maximilien Morrel, a cavalry captain home from Africa, laughs as he takes both the Count's hands in his. Behind them on a side table, under a glass dome, sits a small red silk purse. The prose tells that nine years ago a stranger saved Maximilien's father from ruin and vanished, that the stranger was Edmond Dantès, and that the Morrels never learned it and keep his purse under glass. The dominant turn: the Count is in the one house where he is loved, under his own red purse. The warm greeting owns the page; the purse is small but plainly visible behind them. The reader turns because nobody in this house knows why they love him.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. The Morrel house. Maximilien lifts the glass dome off the red purse as carefully as if it were a crown, the Count beside him looking down at his own purse. Maximilien says his father believed God sent that man, and swears that if he ever finds him he will give him his life. The Count, very still, says the man would want something better done with it. Maximilien sets the dome down, suddenly shy, grins, and confesses he is hopelessly in love; the Count asks why hopelessly. The dominant turn: Maximilien offers his life to his unknown benefactor, standing beside him, and confesses he is in love. The lifted dome and the purse own the page; the Count's stillness is the undercurrent. The reader turns because Maximilien is about to say who she is.

## PAGE 16

Dramatic. The Morrel house, by the fire. Maximilien paces: her father would sooner see her dead than married to a shipowner's son; they meet at an iron gate at the bottom of his garden and talk through the bars. The Count asks who her father is. Maximilien turns, glowing, and gives the name like a gift: the King's Attorney's daughter, Valentine de Villefort. Then the Count's face, and for the first time in the book the pleasure goes out of it. Of every house in Paris, that one. Maximilien sees it and asks whether he has met her; once, the Count says, standing by a window, and nobody was looking at her. The dominant turn: the girl Maximilien loves is Valentine de Villefort, and the pleasure leaves the Count's face. Maximilien's glowing name owns the page; the Count's changed face answers it. The reader turns because she lives where the Count left a poison.

## PAGE 17

Dramatic. The Count's study on the Champs-Élysées at night, black and gold, one candle. The Count stands at the tall window over the lamps of Paris with his gloves still on; Haydée, in crimson and gold, comes in behind him and says it bluntly: the girl Maximilien loves sleeps in the same house as his little bottle. She tells him to go back tonight and ask the mother for it. He refuses: he wants to hear Villefort's house fall, every floor. He turns from the window and promises he will see everything, and that Valentine walks out alive. Haydée answers that he said that about the boy in the carriage. The dominant turn: Haydée asks him to take the bottle back; he refuses and promises Valentine will live. Her last line is the page's knife, and he has no answer for it.

## PAGE 18

Illustrated prose. The Count's study, late at night, black and gold, one candle. The Count sits alone at his desk, writing; three plain cream invitation cards lie in a row before him, seen edge-on; through the tall window, the lamps of Paris. The prose carries his private belief: that God kept Villefort's buried child breathing for him; that the boy in the carriage lived because he stood in the road, and that he will stand in every road of Villefort's house. Then he writes three invitations to Auteuil, for Villefort, his wife, and Prince Andrea Cavalcanti, and Danglars can wait. The dominant turn: the Count believes he will see everything, and invites Villefort and his son to Auteuil. His solitary figure at the desk owns the page; the cards stay small and wordless. The reader turns because Villefort is coming to dinner where he buried a child.

## PAGE 19

A dramatic page at the iron gate at the bottom of Villefort's garden, at dusk. Valentine, inside, and Maximilien, outside in the lane, meet with their hands through the bars, and for one panel they are simply two people in love and laughing. The dominant turn: Valentine and Maximilien are in love, and her grandmother is coming with husbands. Her face falls as she gives the news; he offers to ask her father; she grips his fingers and says she wants him, not a husband from a list. The page belongs to the two of them and the bars between them. The reader turns because an old lady is coming to stay in the house where the poison is.

## PAGE 20

A dramatic page in the candlelit dining room at Auteuil, the dark garden and the pale plane tree beyond the glass doors. The Count, delighted host, presents "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti" — Benedetto in green velvet — and the young man bows over Villefort's hand, two long narrow faces close together, one young and one old. The dominant turn: Villefort shakes his own son's hand and calls his father a fool. Benedetto's charm, Villefort's cold courtesy, and the Count's toast "To fathers" carry the irony the reader holds and the guests do not. Madame de Villefort is radiant and subordinate. The reader turns because the King's Attorney has just insulted the man who buried his son: himself.

## PAGE 21

A dramatic page in the garden at Auteuil after dinner, guests with candles under the one old plane tree. The Count stops on the bare ground and tells a ghost story about a child born and buried here one night long ago; Madame de Villefort is thrilled. The dominant turn: the Count's ghost story under the tree sends Villefort away white. The tableau owns the page — Villefort white as paper gripping the trunk, his wife delighted, and Benedetto, his grin gone, looking at the ground under his own feet and then up at the Count. Villefort turns stiffly for the house with a curt good night. The reader turns because the King's Attorney is fleeing a tree, and the false prince has understood whose garden this is.

## PAGE 22

A spectacle page, near-silent, in the garden at Auteuil in the dead of the night. Under the plane tree Villefort kneels alone in his shirtsleeves at the edge of a deep raw hole he has dug by lantern light, coat flung on the ground, spade beside him, staring into it. It is empty. The dominant turn: Villefort digs at night and finds the hole empty while the Count watches. One caption tells it plainly; the second panel looks up at the dark house, where the Count's pale face watches from an upstairs window. The empty hole owns the page; the watcher is small and certain. The reader turns because Villefort now knows someone dug the box up, and the Count saw him learn it.

## PAGE 23

An illustrated-prose page in a dim guest room of Villefort's house, one candle. Under the white covers of a curtained bed lies a small still shape with white hair on the pillow, her face never shown. Valentine kneels at the foot of the bed with her face in the coverlet; in the doorway stands Madame de Villefort in violet, perfectly composed. The dominant turn: Héloïse poisons the grandmother in front of Noirtier, and Valentine inherits. The prose tells the arrival, the cup of warm milk, the red drops tipped in before the one man who could not tell, and the fortune; the picture holds grief and composure in one room. The reader turns because Valentine is suddenly rich, and the reader knows what was in the milk.

## PAGE 24

A dramatic page in Noirtier's dark green room. Villefort shuts the door and questions his father, who can answer only with his eyes: one long blink for yes, many quick blinks for no, and Villefort says each answer aloud himself. Yes, someone in the house did it; no, it is not Valentine. As the old man's eyes begin to name the poisoner, Villefort straightens and holds up one hand between himself and those eyes to stop them. The dominant turn: Villefort knows there is a poisoner and chooses not to know who. The raised hand and the furious eyes behind it own the page. Noirtier never speaks. The reader turns because the King's Attorney has chosen not to know.

## PAGE 25

A dramatic page in Villefort's cold grey-blue drawing room. Madame de Villefort sits on the sofa in violet, her son asleep in her lap with his tin hussar, stroking his curls, while the Count stands before her in black. Sweetly she thanks him for his "little medicine" and talks of Valentine's million and her poor boy's nothing — unless something happened to Valentine. The dominant turn: Héloïse as good as tells the Count Valentine is next, and he does not stop her; his still hand and his pleasant question are the whole of his answer. Then the boy, half awake, presses his tin soldier into the Count's gloved hand, and the Count gives it back. The reader turns because the Count has been told who is next and let it stand.

## PAGE 26

A dramatic page in the Count's black-and-gold study at night, one candle. Haydée stands across the desk, hands flat on the wood, and lays the dead grandmother in front of him: an old woman drank his bottle in a cup of milk; did he see the milk? The Count keeps writing and answers with Villefort, who knew the face and chose not to ask. The dominant turn: Haydée confronts him with the dead woman; he bets the poisoner will wait and writes to the police. Haydée's straight question owns the page; his cold reasons and the folded letter are his defence. The reader turns because he is wagering Valentine's life on a murderer's patience, and the letter is for the police.

## PAGE 27

An illustrated-prose page on the great lit steps of the Opéra at night. A fashionable crowd in furs and greys draws back as two gendarmes in blue hold Benedetto by both arms; he is in his bottle-green velvet coat with the diamond pin, and he is smiling. The dominant turn: Benedetto is arrested, and Villefort will prosecute his own son. The prose tells the unsigned letter the Count wrote, the arrest, and Villefort, who shook that man's hand and dug under the Count's tree and still asks nothing, announcing he will prosecute the false prince himself and ask for his head. The smiling prisoner owns the image. The reader turns because the father will try his son, and Valentine is still in the poisoner's house.

## PAGE 28

A dramatic page in Noirtier's green room in the morning. Valentine sits on a footstool at her grandfather's chair, chatting about how kind her stepmother has been and sipping the chocolate her stepmother sent down. She notices it tastes bitter, and then she falls. The page belongs to the fall and to the old man above it: Valentine sliding off the stool, the cup breaking, the book dropping open, while Noirtier, who cannot move a muscle or make a sound, stares at her in terror. The dominant turn: Valentine drinks the poisoned chocolate and falls while Noirtier can only watch. The reader turns because the poison has reached the girl the Count's plan was supposed to keep safe. Subordinate: the furniture of the room, the book, the medicine glass on his table.

## PAGE 29

A dramatic page in the Count's black-and-gold study. Maximilien bursts in soaked from the rain with the news that Valentine fell this morning and should be dead. The Count's poised hand closes when he hears it. Maximilien seizes him and demands to know what is happening in that house; the Count tells him plainly that someone there is a poisoner, then stops him from running to the police, because the police answer to Valentine's father, and gives his word that she will not die. The page belongs to Maximilien's desperation meeting the Count's stillness, and the turn is the promise. Dominant turn: Maximilien begs for help, and the Count promises Valentine will not die. The reader turns knowing he has promised to save the girl his own poison is killing. Subordinate: the study's furniture, the rain, the crushed hat.

## PAGE 30

A dramatic page in Noirtier's room in the evening. Valentine, pale on a daybed, says her grandfather made her sip his medicine every morning. The Count smells the old man's glass and understands: Noirtier has been dosing her against the poison, and his one long blink confirms it. Then, close and low so Valentine cannot hear, the Count tells Noirtier that the next dose will be bigger, and when the old man's eyes demand to know how he knows, the Count confesses the bottle was his, and that he will still have Noirtier's son. Dominant turn: Noirtier's medicine saved her once, and the Count confesses the vial was his. The old man owns the page with nothing but his eyes; the confession is pulled out of the Count. Subordinate: the daybed, the room, the glass once it has been smelled.

## PAGE 31

An illustrated-prose page in a bare, dusty upper room of the empty house next door to Villefort's, at night. The Count, alone, holds a lantern beside a narrow doorway newly cut through the wall; through it, past the back boards of a tall wardrobe left open a crack, lies a sliver of a white room. The prose tells how he bought the house, had the door cut by workmen paid to keep quiet, put out his lantern and sat down in the dark to wait, why he did not warn Valentine, and that he could wait three nights in a wall. Dominant turn: the Count cuts into the wall and waits in the dark. The reader turns because he is inside the wall and someone is coming. Subordinate: the dust, the tools, the room's emptiness.

## PAGE 32

A silent spectacle page in Valentine's room at night. We look out from inside the dark wardrobe, through the crack of its door: a white moonlit room, Valentine asleep in her narrow bed, and her stepmother in a violet dressing-gown, a shaded candle in one hand, tipping red drops from the small crystal vial into the glass of water by the bed. Below, in the black of the wardrobe, the Count's eye at the crack and his hand flat on the door. Dominant turn: the Count watches Héloïse pour the drops into Valentine's glass. The page belongs to the act seen through the gap; the reader turns because he has caught the poisoner in the act, with his own vial. Subordinate: the room's furniture, the moonlight, the candle.

## PAGE 33

A dramatic page in Valentine's room minutes later. The Count steps out of the wardrobe and quiets her with a hand over her mouth, then holds her glass up to the candle so she can see the red threads drifting in it. He tells her who poured them; she cannot believe it of her stepmother, then lifts her chin and says she will tell her father, and the Count answers that two people have been poisoned in her father's house and he has not called the police. Dominant turn: the Count shows Valentine the poisoned glass and that her father will not save her. The glass held to the candle owns the page; the reader turns because Valentine now knows, and even her father will not save her. Subordinate: the bed, the wardrobe, the room.

## PAGE 34

A dramatic page in Valentine's room by candlelight, minutes after she has seen the poisoned glass. She asks to be taken away through the wardrobe; the Count refuses, because a missing girl is hunted and a dead one is not. The dominant turn: Valentine chooses to swallow the Count's draught and die. The page belongs to the Count's open palm, a small gold box holding one white pastille, with his face above it and the corner of his mouth lifting very slightly, because he is enjoying the plan. Then the choice passes to her: she asks about Maximilien, he asks whether she trusts him, and she takes the pastille with her eyes on his. The reader turns because she has swallowed it and in the morning she will be dead. The wardrobe and the room stay subordinate.

## PAGE 35

An illustrated-prose page in grey morning light. The dominant turn: the house believes Valentine is dead. She lies white and still on her bed, hands folded, a white sheet drawn up; her father stands rigid at the foot with a hand over his eyes and does not weep; her stepmother bows her head prettily in the doorway; and wheeled to the bedside, her grandfather fixes his wide wet eyes on her face. The page belongs to the stillness of the bed and to Noirtier's eyes, which are the only living thing in the room and are saying something nobody there can read. The prose tells what the house believed; the picture lets the reader see the three different faces of grief. The reader turns to learn what the old man's eyes are saying. The furniture stays subordinate.

## PAGE 36

A dramatic page in Noirtier's green room, the afternoon after. Villefort, exhausted, comes to his silent father, who has stared at Valentine's empty stool since he was wheeled back. The dominant turn: Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE, and Villefort can no longer not know. Unwilling, Villefort recites the alphabet with a pencil while his father's blazing eyes close once at a letter; the word grows letter by letter on a scrap of paper; and when Villefort, grey, asks whether it means his wife, the old man's eyes close slowly, once, and Villefort himself says the answer aloud. Noirtier never speaks; every word on the page is his son's. The reader turns because the King's Attorney now knows the poisoner is his wife. The furniture and the clock stay subordinate.

## PAGE 37

A dramatic page at Père-Lachaise in the rain, the day of Valentine's funeral. The dominant turn: Maximilien accuses the Count at the tomb, and the Count says nothing of what he could say. The page belongs to the wide wet scene of the vault door closing on her, the mourners under umbrellas, Villefort at the front, Maximilien bareheaded in his blue uniform at the very door, and the Count at the back of the crowd. Then Maximilien turns away from the tomb and faces the Count: you gave me your word. The Count, rain on his face, tells him to come away because the house is watching, and Maximilien says it again: you promised me. The reader turns because the Count is letting the man he loves believe she is dead. The other mourners stay subordinate.

## PAGE 38

A silent spectacle page at night in the cemetery; the rain has stopped. The dominant turn: the Count lifts Valentine out of the tomb. The iron door of the family vault stands open, one lantern burns on the stone inside, and the Count, alone, lifts Valentine, white and limp in a white dress, out of her open coffin in his arms. Below, a shallow strip: he carries her along the wet path between black trees toward the small warm lamp of a waiting carriage. There are no words on the page. The reader turns because he has her, but she has not woken. The tomb's architecture and the carriage stay subordinate to the lift.

## PAGE 39

An illustrated-prose page by candlelight in an upstairs room of the Count's house. The dominant turn: Valentine does not wake, and the Count smiles at the trial to come. Valentine sleeps very pale in a white bed; Haydée, in crimson and gold with her sleeves pushed back and a cloth in her hand, has nursed her day and night. The Count stands at the dark window, smiling about tomorrow, and Haydée lays her hand on his sleeve, a touch that is tenderness and also a check on him; he does not move it away. The prose tells why the draught has not worn off, why he will not tell Maximilien, and that the trial is in the morning. The reader turns because the girl may never wake and the Count is looking forward to the courtroom. The room stays subordinate.

## PAGE 40

Dramatic. The night before the trial, the Count visits Benedetto in his prison cell and keeps the promise he made in the cellar: he gives the young man his father's name, Gérard de Villefort, the King's Attorney who will ask for his head tomorrow, and a birth date to learn by heart. The page's one dominant turn: the Count gives Benedetto his father's name and his birth date. The Count owns the giving; Benedetto owns the reaction, a grin that spreads slowly into delight as he understands the man who buried him is the man who will prosecute him. The reader turns because tomorrow the son will name the father in front of everyone. The cell, the lantern and the bars stay plain and subordinate; this is two faces in a small stone room.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Morning, at the foot of the white stair in Villefort's house. Villefort, dressed for court, tells his wife he knows she poisoned Valentine and her grandmother, and orders her to use her poison on herself before he comes home tonight. Then, gently, he kisses his small son's curls and leaves for court. The page's one dominant turn: Villefort orders his wife to poison herself and kisses his son goodbye. Villefort owns the page: his cold face, close, giving the sentence. The last beat belongs to Madame de Villefort on the stair with her hand on the boy's head, asking what will become of him. The reader turns because Villefort has told his wife to die and left the boy alone with her. The hall's marble and the door stay subordinate.

## PAGE 42

Spectacle. The Assize court, packed to the walls: the public gallery crammed, judges in red on the high bench, gendarmes in blue, Benedetto in the dock in his grey smock grinning at the crowd, Villefort rising at the prosecutor's desk in his black robe, and, high in the gallery, the Count watching. The page's one dominant turn: the packed court watches Villefort rise to question the false prince. One great image owns the page; one short caption anchors it. The reader turns because Villefort is about to question the son he does not know is his. The crowd is texture and scale; no single spectator competes with Villefort, Benedetto, or the small dark figure of the Count above them.

## PAGE 43

Dramatic. Villefort questions the prisoner coldly, from a paper, barely looking at him: his real name, his age. Benedetto answers with insolent ease and gives the exact birth date the Count taught him. Then Villefort asks where, and Benedetto says Auteuil, a house with a garden and a very big tree. The page's one dominant turn: Benedetto names Auteuil and the garden, and Villefort's pen stops. Villefort owns the dominant panel: his pen still, his face lifting slowly toward the dock. Benedetto is the voice that does it. The reader turns because Villefort knows that garden. The courtroom, the judges and the crowd stay subordinate behind the two of them.

## PAGE 44

Dramatic. Villefort, on his feet and white, tries to call the prisoner a convict and a liar. Dramatic page. Villefort bursts into his silent hall shouting for his wife and his son, and finds Madame de Villefort at the top of the white marble stair in her violet silk, swaying, the empty crystal vial slipping from her hand and falling down the steps. She tells him, gently, that she did what he told her. Then she sinks onto the top stair, eyes closing, and answers the question he cannot finish asking: she could not leave the boy behind. The dominant turn: Héloïse has taken the poison and the boy with her. The dominant image is her on the stair with the vial falling; Villefort's entry and her sinking are subordinate. Dramatic page. We stay in the corridor: the Count has gone through the doorway, out of sight, and Villefort slumps against the wall beside it, staring at the tin hussar on the floor. ## PAGE 52

Dramatic page. At dusk, in the small walled garden behind his own house, Villefort in shirtsleeves, his robe flung on the gravel, collar torn open, hair fallen across his brow, digs furiously in the frozen flower beds with a spade while every window of the house above him is lit. The Count at the garden door tells him, quietly, that this is his own garden and the tree is at Auteuil; Villefort answers that he will dig up every garden in Paris, and keeps digging. The dominant turn: Villefort digs in the wrong garden while the Count watches. The digging madman owns the page; the Count is a still witness, not an actor. This is not the Auteuil garden: no plane tree, no hole from earlier, no box.

The prose tells what he cannot say: two men are finished, and he cannot say the next word, "Two"; his hand is closed round a tin soldier in his pocket; he keeps seeing a red bottle in a woman's hand where he put it himself; for the first time since the Château d'If he does not know whether God is on his side.
