## PAGE 48

Dramatic page. Villefort bursts into his silent hall shouting for his wife and his son, and finds Madame de Villefort at the top of the white marble stair in her violet silk, swaying, the empty crystal vial slipping from her hand and falling down the steps. She tells him, gently, that she did what he told her. Then she sinks onto the top stair, eyes closing, and answers the question he cannot finish asking: she could not leave the boy behind. The dominant turn: Héloïse has taken the poison and the boy with her. The dominant image is her on the stair with the vial falling; Villefort's entry and her sinking are subordinate. The reader turns to find the boy. She is not drawn dying in agony; the falling vial and her quiet sentence carry the horror, not any struggle.

## PAGE 49

Spectacle page, wordless. A long cold grey corridor; at its far end a closed white door; on the floor before it, alone, the boy's tin hussar on its rearing horse, red and gold. Villefort, small in the distance in his black robe, runs toward it. A close beat follows: his hand on the white door handle. The dominant turn: the tin hussar lies outside a closed door. The corridor and the toy own the page; the reader understands before Villefort does. The reader turns because the door is about to open. The boy is never seen, nor anything behind the door; Villefort's face is not the subject here, the distance and the small bright toy are.

## PAGE 50

Dramatic page. A quiet establishing beat: Madame de Villefort lies fallen with dignity on the top step, eyes closed, while the Count, coming up the stair, stoops and lifts his own empty vial from where it fell, his eyes on her face. Down the corridor Villefort kneels in his son's open doorway and asks who is there. The Count answers that he has come to tell him who he is, and then, face full in the frame with every mask off, says it: Remember the Château d'If; I am Edmond Dantès. Villefort rises, seizes him by the coat and drags him toward the door to see what he has done. The dominant turn: over Héloïse's body and at his son's door, the Count tells Villefort he is Edmond Dantès. That face and that sentence own the page. The boy and the room beyond the door stay unseen.

## PAGE 51

Dramatic page. We stay in the corridor: the Count has gone through the doorway, out of sight, and Villefort slumps against the wall beside it, staring at the tin hussar on the floor. The Count comes slowly back out, his face emptied, the empty vial held up in his open hand: one drop brought the boy back once; full, it would still be too late; the boy is dead. Villefort laughs a terrible small laugh and says he will dig this son up too. Last, the Count's gloved hand picks the hussar off the floor. The dominant turn: the Count comes out with the empty vial, the boy is dead, and Villefort's mind breaks. The Count in the doorway owns the page. The boy is never drawn; the room stays out of sight.

## PAGE 52

Dramatic page. At dusk, in the small walled garden behind his own house, Villefort in shirtsleeves, his robe flung on the gravel, collar torn open, hair fallen across his brow, digs furiously in the frozen flower beds with a spade while every window of the house above him is lit. The Count at the garden door tells him, quietly, that this is his own garden and the tree is at Auteuil; Villefort answers that he will dig up every garden in Paris, and keeps digging. The dominant turn: Villefort digs in the wrong garden while the Count watches. The digging madman owns the page; the Count is a still witness, not an actor. This is not the Auteuil garden: no plane tree, no hole from earlier, no box.

## PAGE 53

Illustrated-prose page. The Count stands alone in a black street under a gas lamp as snow begins, beneath Villefort's lit windows, the window he once counted from across the roofs on the night he said "One." The prose tells what he cannot say: two men are finished, and he cannot say the next word, "Two"; his hand is closed round a tin soldier in his pocket; he keeps seeing a red bottle in a woman's hand where he put it himself; for the first time since the Château d'If he does not know whether God is on his side. The dominant turn: the Count cannot say "Two." The lone figure in snow owns the image; the street, the lamp and the house stay quiet and subordinate.

## PAGE 54

Dramatic page. The Count goes back into the house and kneels by Noirtier's chair in the dark green room by one candle. He tells the old man his son is digging in the garden and his wife and the boy are dead; then, close and low, that Valentine is alive in the Count's house. A tear runs down Noirtier's still face; his eyes close once, slowly, and the Count speaks the answer: yes. The Count asks him to keep this secret as he kept his name, then asks whether Noirtier now wishes he had stopped. The eyes close once more, slowly: yes. The dominant turn: the Count tells Noirtier Valentine lives, and Noirtier wishes he had stopped him. Noirtier never speaks; his eyes answer and the Count says the word. The candle and the room stay subordinate.

## PAGE 55

Dramatic page. In the Count's study at night, Maximilien, very calm in a plain brown coat, comes to say goodbye, his father's dark pistol case under his arm: his father nearly used it once, at eleven in the morning. The Count rises and says a stranger came at five minutes to eleven; Maximilien asks how he could know that. The Count lays his hand flat on the pistol case between them, faces close: give me one month; if you still want to die then, I'll load it myself. Maximilien agrees. The dominant turn: Maximilien agrees to wait one month. The hand on the case and the two faces own the page; the study furniture stays dark and subordinate. The case stays shut.

## PAGE 56

Spectacle page, nearly silent, the last page of the volume. By candlelight Valentine sleeps in a white bed in the Count's house; Haydée in crimson and gold sits beside her holding her hand; the Count stands in the doorway in black, not coming in, the boy's tin hussar in his gloved hand. Haydée looks at the hussar, then up at him, and does not let go of Valentine. Close: Valentine's pale hand closes around Haydée's fingers, and Haydée says she's holding on. The dominant turn: Valentine's hand closes. The wide candlelit room with the three of them owns the page; the closing hand is the quiet answer. Nobody embraces; the Count stays in the doorway.
