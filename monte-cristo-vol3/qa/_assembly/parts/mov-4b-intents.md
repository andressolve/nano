## PAGE 34

A dramatic page in Valentine's room by candlelight, minutes after she has seen the poisoned glass. She asks to be taken away through the wardrobe; the Count refuses, because a missing girl is hunted and a dead one is not. The dominant turn: Valentine chooses to swallow the Count's draught and die. The page belongs to the Count's open palm, a small gold box holding one white pastille, with his face above it and the corner of his mouth lifting very slightly, because he is enjoying the plan. Then the choice passes to her: she asks about Maximilien, he asks whether she trusts him, and she takes the pastille with her eyes on his. The reader turns because she has swallowed it and in the morning she will be dead. The wardrobe and the room stay subordinate.

## PAGE 35

An illustrated-prose page in grey morning light. The dominant turn: the house believes Valentine is dead. She lies white and still on her bed, hands folded, a white sheet drawn up; her father stands rigid at the foot with a hand over his eyes and does not weep; her stepmother bows her head prettily in the doorway; and wheeled to the bedside, her grandfather fixes his wide wet eyes on her face. The page belongs to the stillness of the bed and to Noirtier's eyes, which are the only living thing in the room and are saying something nobody there can read. The prose tells what the house believed; the picture lets the reader see the three different faces of grief. The reader turns to learn what the old man's eyes are saying. The furniture stays subordinate.

## PAGE 36

A dramatic page in Noirtier's green room, the afternoon after. Villefort, exhausted, comes to his silent father, who has stared at Valentine's empty stool since he was wheeled back. The dominant turn: Noirtier spells HÉLOÏSE, and Villefort can no longer not know. Unwilling, Villefort recites the alphabet with a pencil while his father's blazing eyes close once at a letter; the word grows letter by letter on a scrap of paper; and when Villefort, grey, asks whether it means his wife, the old man's eyes close slowly, once, and Villefort himself says the answer aloud. Noirtier never speaks; every word on the page is his son's. The reader turns because the King's Attorney now knows the poisoner is his wife. The furniture and the clock stay subordinate.

## PAGE 37

A dramatic page at Père-Lachaise in the rain, the day of Valentine's funeral. The dominant turn: Maximilien accuses the Count at the tomb, and the Count says nothing of what he could say. The page belongs to the wide wet scene of the vault door closing on her, the mourners under umbrellas, Villefort at the front, Maximilien bareheaded in his blue uniform at the very door, and the Count at the back of the crowd. Then Maximilien turns away from the tomb and faces the Count: you gave me your word. The Count, rain on his face, tells him to come away because the house is watching, and Maximilien says it again: you promised me. The reader turns because the Count is letting the man he loves believe she is dead. The other mourners stay subordinate.

## PAGE 38

A silent spectacle page at night in the cemetery; the rain has stopped. The dominant turn: the Count lifts Valentine out of the tomb. The iron door of the family vault stands open, one lantern burns on the stone inside, and the Count, alone, lifts Valentine, white and limp in a white dress, out of her open coffin in his arms. Below, a shallow strip: he carries her along the wet path between black trees toward the small warm lamp of a waiting carriage. There are no words on the page. The reader turns because he has her, but she has not woken. The tomb's architecture and the carriage stay subordinate to the lift.

## PAGE 39

An illustrated-prose page by candlelight in an upstairs room of the Count's house. The dominant turn: Valentine does not wake, and the Count smiles at the trial to come. Valentine sleeps very pale in a white bed; Haydée, in crimson and gold with her sleeves pushed back and a cloth in her hand, has nursed her day and night. The Count stands at the dark window, smiling about tomorrow, and Haydée lays her hand on his sleeve, a touch that is tenderness and also a check on him; he does not move it away. The prose tells why the draught has not worn off, why he will not tell Maximilien, and that the trial is in the morning. The reader turns because the girl may never wake and the Count is looking forward to the courtroom. The room stays subordinate.
