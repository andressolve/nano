The night before the trial, the Count visits Benedetto in his prison cell and keeps the promise he made in the cellar: he gives the young man his father's name, Gérard de Villefort, the King's Attorney who will ask for his head tomorrow, and a birth date to learn by heart. The page's one dominant turn: the Count gives Benedetto his father's name and his birth date. The Count owns the giving; Benedetto owns the reaction, a grin that spreads slowly into delight as he understands the man who buried him is the man who will prosecute him. The reader turns because tomorrow the son will name the father in front of everyone. The cell, the lantern and the bars stay plain and subordinate; this is two faces in a small stone room.

Dramatic. Morning, at the foot of the white stair in Villefort's house. Villefort, dressed for court, tells his wife he knows she poisoned Valentine and her grandmother, and orders her to use her poison on herself before he comes home tonight. Then, gently, he kisses his small son's curls and leaves for court. The page's one dominant turn: Villefort orders his wife to poison herself and kisses his son goodbye. Villefort owns the page: his cold face, close, giving the sentence. The last beat belongs to Madame de Villefort on the stair with her hand on the boy's head, asking what will become of him. The reader turns because Villefort has told his wife to die and left the boy alone with her. The hall's marble and the door stay subordinate.

Spectacle. The Assize court, packed to the walls: the public gallery crammed, judges in red on the high bench, gendarmes in blue, Benedetto in the dock in his grey smock grinning at the crowd, Villefort rising at the prosecutor's desk in his black robe, and, high in the gallery, the Count watching. The page's one dominant turn: the packed court watches Villefort rise to question the false prince. One great image owns the page; one short caption anchors it. The reader turns because Villefort is about to question the son he does not know is his. The crowd is texture and scale; no single spectator competes with Villefort, Benedetto, or the small dark figure of the Count above them.

Dramatic. Villefort questions the prisoner coldly, from a paper, barely looking at him: his real name, his age. Benedetto answers with insolent ease and gives the exact birth date the Count taught him. Then Villefort asks where, and Benedetto says Auteuil, a house with a garden and a very big tree. The page's one dominant turn: Benedetto names Auteuil and the garden, and Villefort's pen stops. Villefort owns the dominant panel: his pen still, his face lifting slowly toward the dock. Benedetto is the voice that does it. The reader turns because Villefort knows that garden. The courtroom, the judges and the crowd stay subordinate behind the two of them.

Dramatic. Villefort, steadying himself on his desk, asks the prisoner for his father's name, half daring him. Benedetto, delighted, leans on the dock's rail: his father is the King's Attorney. Then he points straight across the court, and the whole court turns. The page's one dominant turn: Benedetto names the King's Attorney as his father. The dominant image belongs to the pointing arm and to what it does to the room: every face turning toward Villefort, alone at his desk in black. The reader turns because the whole court is now looking at Villefort. Judges, gendarmes and spectators are a single turning mass, never individuals competing for the eye.

## PAGE 45

Dramatic. Villefort, on his feet and white, tries to call the prisoner a convict and a liar. Benedetto, his grin gone hard and quiet, tells the court about the night in the garden: the box, the tree, the man who watched and dug him up. Then Villefort's own words escape him, that it wasn't breathing, that he thought it was dead, and Benedetto answers that he didn't look. The page's one dominant turn: Villefort's own words confess it; he thought it was dead; he didn't look. Benedetto owns the dominant panel, quiet and terrible. The reader turns because Villefort has just confessed without meaning to. The court stays a dim background.

## PAGE 46

Dramatic. Total silence in the court; every face is on Villefort except Benedetto's, grinning up at the gallery between his gendarmes. Villefort, his cap fallen and his robe slipping from one shoulder, looks up past all of them to the gallery and tells the court not to look for proof: it is true. High in the gallery, the Count looks down, and the corner of his mouth lifts. The page's one dominant turn: Villefort admits it is true, and the Count has the face he waited for. Villefort standing alone owns the page; the Count's small, private satisfaction is the last beat. The reader turns because the King's Attorney has confessed in open court and the Count has what he came for. The crowd stays a silent mass.

Illustrated prose. Villefort alone in a closed carriage racing through Paris in the rain, still in his robe, his face in his hands. The prose tells how he walked out of the court, how Benedetto was taken down still grinning, and how, in the carriage, he remembered what he had told his wife on the stair, and that his son was in the house with her; he shouts to go faster. Through the little back window, the lamps of a second black carriage follow. The page's one dominant turn: in the carriage Villefort remembers his son is in the house with her. Villefort owns the page; the following carriage is small and quiet. The reader turns because the boy is with her and the Count is following.
