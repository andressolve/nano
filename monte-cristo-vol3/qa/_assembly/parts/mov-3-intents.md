## PAGE 19

A dramatic page at the iron gate at the bottom of Villefort's garden, at dusk. Valentine, inside, and Maximilien, outside in the lane, meet with their hands through the bars, and for one panel they are simply two people in love and laughing. The dominant turn: Valentine and Maximilien are in love, and her grandmother is coming with husbands. Her face falls as she gives the news; he offers to ask her father; she grips his fingers and says she wants him, not a husband from a list. The page belongs to the two of them and the bars between them. The reader turns because an old lady is coming to stay in the house where the poison is.

## PAGE 20

A dramatic page in the candlelit dining room at Auteuil, the dark garden and the pale plane tree beyond the glass doors. The Count, delighted host, presents "Prince Andrea Cavalcanti" — Benedetto in green velvet — and the young man bows over Villefort's hand, two long narrow faces close together, one young and one old. The dominant turn: Villefort shakes his own son's hand and calls his father a fool. Benedetto's charm, Villefort's cold courtesy, and the Count's toast "To fathers" carry the irony the reader holds and the guests do not. Madame de Villefort is radiant and subordinate. The reader turns because the King's Attorney has just insulted the man who buried his son: himself.

## PAGE 21

A dramatic page in the garden at Auteuil after dinner, guests with candles under the one old plane tree. The Count stops on the bare ground and tells a ghost story about a child born and buried here one night long ago; Madame de Villefort is thrilled. The dominant turn: the Count's ghost story under the tree sends Villefort away white. The tableau owns the page — Villefort white as paper gripping the trunk, his wife delighted, and Benedetto, his grin gone, looking at the ground under his own feet and then up at the Count. Villefort turns stiffly for the house with a curt good night. The reader turns because the King's Attorney is fleeing a tree, and the false prince has understood whose garden this is.

## PAGE 22

A spectacle page, near-silent, in the garden at Auteuil in the dead of the night. Under the plane tree Villefort kneels alone in his shirtsleeves at the edge of a deep raw hole he has dug by lantern light, coat flung on the ground, spade beside him, staring into it. It is empty. The dominant turn: Villefort digs at night and finds the hole empty while the Count watches. One caption tells it plainly; the second panel looks up at the dark house, where the Count's pale face watches from an upstairs window. The empty hole owns the page; the watcher is small and certain. The reader turns because Villefort now knows someone dug the box up, and the Count saw him learn it.

## PAGE 23

An illustrated-prose page in a dim guest room of Villefort's house, one candle. Under the white covers of a curtained bed lies a small still shape with white hair on the pillow, her face never shown. Valentine kneels at the foot of the bed with her face in the coverlet; in the doorway stands Madame de Villefort in violet, perfectly composed. The dominant turn: Héloïse poisons the grandmother in front of Noirtier, and Valentine inherits. The prose tells the arrival, the cup of warm milk, the red drops tipped in before the one man who could not tell, and the fortune; the picture holds grief and composure in one room. The reader turns because Valentine is suddenly rich, and the reader knows what was in the milk.

## PAGE 24

A dramatic page in Noirtier's dark green room. Villefort shuts the door and questions his father, who can answer only with his eyes: one long blink for yes, many quick blinks for no, and Villefort says each answer aloud himself. Yes, someone in the house did it; no, it is not Valentine. As the old man's eyes begin to name the poisoner, Villefort straightens and holds up one hand between himself and those eyes to stop them. The dominant turn: Villefort knows there is a poisoner and chooses not to know who. The raised hand and the furious eyes behind it own the page. Noirtier never speaks. The reader turns because the King's Attorney has chosen not to know.

## PAGE 25

A dramatic page in Villefort's cold grey-blue drawing room. Madame de Villefort sits on the sofa in violet, her son asleep in her lap with his tin hussar, stroking his curls, while the Count stands before her in black. Sweetly she thanks him for his "little medicine" and talks of Valentine's million and her poor boy's nothing — unless something happened to Valentine. The dominant turn: Héloïse as good as tells the Count Valentine is next, and he does not stop her; his still hand and his pleasant question are the whole of his answer. Then the boy, half awake, presses his tin soldier into the Count's gloved hand, and the Count gives it back. The reader turns because the Count has been told who is next and let it stand.

## PAGE 26

A dramatic page in the Count's black-and-gold study at night, one candle. Haydée stands across the desk, hands flat on the wood, and lays the dead grandmother in front of him: an old woman drank his bottle in a cup of milk; did he see the milk? The Count keeps writing and answers with Villefort, who knew the face and chose not to ask. The dominant turn: Haydée confronts him with the dead woman; he bets the poisoner will wait and writes to the police. Haydée's straight question owns the page; his cold reasons and the folded letter are his defence. The reader turns because he is wagering Valentine's life on a murderer's patience, and the letter is for the police.

## PAGE 27

An illustrated-prose page on the great lit steps of the Opéra at night. A fashionable crowd in furs and greys draws back as two gendarmes in blue hold Benedetto by both arms; he is in his bottle-green velvet coat with the diamond pin, and he is smiling. The dominant turn: Benedetto is arrested, and Villefort will prosecute his own son. The prose tells the unsigned letter the Count wrote, the arrest, and Villefort, who shook that man's hand and dug under the Count's tree and still asks nothing, announcing he will prosecute the false prince himself and ask for his head. The smiling prisoner owns the image. The reader turns because the father will try his son, and Valentine is still in the poisoner's house.
