## Page intent

## PAGE 7

Spectacle. On the steep walled lane at Auteuil two bay horses bolt downhill with no coachman, dragging a light open carriage in which Madame de Villefort screams and clutches her small son. A single caption tells who they are. The Count steps out of his own gateway into the road, cloak flying, seizes the lead horse's bridle with both hands and throws his weight back; the horses rear above him, a wheel smashes into the gatepost, and he does not move. Then sudden stillness: the horses heaving, the carriage tilted against the post, and over its side a small limp hand still holding a tin hussar. The dominant turn: the Count stops the runaway carriage, and the boy is limp. The reader turns because the child is not moving. Splinters and the lane's walls stay subordinate to the man in the road.

## Exact owner-controlled script

## PAGE 7 · *spectacle*

**Location:** the steep lane outside the Count's gate at Auteuil, afternoon.

**Panel 1 — 25%.** Top band: two wild-eyed bay horses bolting down the steep
walled lane, no coachman on the box, a light open carriage slewing behind
them. In it MADAME DE VILLEFORT — honey-gold hair in high coils coming loose,
amethyst-violet silk — clutches her small SON, about eight, gold curls, violet
velvet suit, a toy soldier in his fist. Her mouth is open in a scream.

> **CAPTION** *(upper left)*
> "Down the hill toward the Count's gate, out of control, came the King's Attorney's wife and his little son."

**Panel 2 — dominant, 60%.** The Count steps out of his own gateway into the
road, cloak flying, and seizes the lead horse's bridle with both hands, his
whole weight thrown back; the horses rear above him, hooves in the air; a
carriage wheel strikes the stone gatepost in a burst of splinters. The Count
does not move. He is a black post in the road.

**Panel 3 — 15%.** Sudden stillness: the horses standing, heaving, steam off
their backs; the carriage tilted against the gatepost; over its side hangs a
small limp hand still holding a tin hussar on a rearing horse, red and gold.

**Why you turn the page:** the horses have stopped, and the boy is not moving.

## Materiality threshold

The critic is a release gate, not a defect collector. A visible issue is
blocking only when it materially harms the reader event, exact text or
attribution, named or focal identity, consequential continuity or object state,
focal generation integrity, or the dominant dramatic relationship. Every
correction replaces the complete page. Minor background, cosmetic, tiny-prop,
geometry, colour, texture, and prompt-fidelity observations are nonblocking.
Omit them. Numeric panel shares and lettering sizes are builder construction
guidance, never critic measurements.

Each numbered criterion is also its stable retry signature. Cite it only when
the reader harm and redraw justification are concrete.

A lettered speaker name, label, or tag anywhere on the page is extra text and
blocks under the transcription criterion, whatever else reads correctly:
finished pages carry dialogue, captions, prose, and sound cues only.
A sound cue whose letters and order match the script reads correctly; the run
length of a repeated letter in it is nonblocking and never counted.

## Numbered blocking criteria

## Page 7 — card

### C1 — Transcription fails
The one caption reads exactly from the 600 × 900 proof, once, with no other text: no screams lettered, no sound cues. A string that does not read is blocking; a string that reads is not to be measured. Nonblocking: spacing around a dash, curly against straight quotes, a hyphen for a dash, a comma present or missing inside a line, a regional spelling variant of the same word, and the run length of a repeated letter in a sound cue; the words and their order are what must match. A caption is owned by placement; a line owned by placement (directly under its speaker's previous balloon, or naming its addressee) is owned even if its tail wanders.

### C2 — The stop does not land
One image owns the page: the Count in the road holding the rearing horses by the bridle, immovable. If he reads as thrown, trampled, or a bystander, or the page reads as dialogue rather than spectacle, it is blocking.

### C3 — The limp hand lost
The last image must show the stillness and a small limp hand over the carriage side holding the tin hussar. If the hand or the toy is missing, so the reader cannot tell the boy is not moving, it is blocking. The boy shows no blood or wound; one would be blocking.

### C4 — Identity
The woman in the carriage with honey-gold coils and violet silk; the boy small with gold curls and violet velvet; the Count pale in a black cloak. A coachman on the box, or a misread identity, is blocking.
