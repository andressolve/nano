# Batch gate — pages 11–20

## Verdict

APPROVED

## Uninterrupted read

The Count enters Noirtier's room as Valentine explains that her grandfather can answer only with his eyes. Once Valentine leaves, the Count tells Noirtier that Villefort intercepted and burned the letter meant for him, sent its young carrier to the Château d'If, and that the carrier was the Count himself. Noirtier does not ask him to stop taking Villefort apart; he asks only for Valentine, and the Count gives his word that she will not be harmed.

The story then moves into the one Paris home where the Count wants nothing. Maximilien Morrel reveres the unknown man who saved his father, unaware that the man stands beside him. He confides that he loves Valentine de Villefort, turning the Count's abstract promise to Noirtier into an immediate personal obligation. Haydée names the danger plainly: Valentine sleeps in the same house as the poison. The Count refuses to abandon his revenge but promises to watch every path through the house and bring Valentine out alive. Alone afterward, he convinces himself that vigilance saved the boy in the carriage and will save Valentine too, then invites Villefort, Madame de Villefort, and Andrea Cavalcanti to Auteuil.

At the garden gate, Valentine and Maximilien reaffirm their love as an approaching family visit threatens to force her into another marriage. At Auteuil, Villefort meets Andrea without knowing that he is meeting his own abandoned son. The Count lets Villefort call Andrea's father a fool, then closes the sequence with the pointed toast, "To fathers."

## Gate judgment

The causal chain is clear and emotionally cumulative: the promise to Noirtier leads into Maximilien's disclosure, Haydée's challenge, the Count's decision to guard Valentine, and the dinner designed to bring Villefort together with Andrea. Page 19 gives the threatened lovers room on the page before page 20 converts the Count's plan into dramatic irony.

Visible text and attribution match the batch script across the transitions. Noirtier's eye-language remains intelligible; Valentine, Maximilien, Haydée, the Count, Villefort, and Andrea are distinct at each return. The red purse and bell jar persist through the Morrel sequence, the clock and green room hold Noirtier's setting together, the Count's study carries from Haydée's warning into his solitary aftermath, and the iron gate and Auteuil dining room clearly establish the next movement.

Pacing varies effectively between dramatic pages and illustrated prose. Pages 11–13 deliberately sustain the pressure of one confrontation; page 14 opens into warm retrospective prose, pages 15–17 tighten the personal stakes, and page 18 supplies consequential interior aftermath before the gate scene and dinner. Neither prose passage reads as compressed summary, and no consequential beat is left without a readable emotional or causal response.

No material sequence, continuity, focal, transcription, attribution, identity, pacing, or page-turn failure warrants a complete-page redraw.
