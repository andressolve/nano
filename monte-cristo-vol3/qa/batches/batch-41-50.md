# Sequence gate — canonical pages 41–50

## Uninterrupted visual read

Villefort confronts Héloïse at home with Édouard beside her. He says he knows she poisoned Valentine and her mother, gives her until his return that night to use her own poison, and warns that otherwise he will arrest her and send her to the scaffold. He leaves after telling Édouard to be good for his mother; Héloïse asks what will become of their son.

At the packed trial of the “false prince,” Villefort questions Benedetto himself. Benedetto gives his name, his birth date, and Auteuil as his birthplace, then identifies his father first as the King's Attorney and finally points directly at Villefort. Villefort calls him a liar. Benedetto answers that Villefort put him in a box and buried him alive beneath the tree, mistaking his stillness for death. Villefort breaks before the court and admits that everything Benedetto has said is true. A dark-haired observer watches the collapse from the gallery, having waited twenty-three years to see it.

Villefort leaves the court while Benedetto is led away. In his carriage, he remembers the ultimatum he gave Héloïse and then remembers that Édouard was in the house with her. He orders the driver to hurry, unaware that a black carriage follows him.

At home, Villefort calls for Héloïse and the boy. Héloïse appears on the stairs and drops the poison bottle. She says she obeyed him and could not leave their son behind in a house bearing Villefort's name. A toy soldier lies in the corridor as Villefort runs to a closed door and takes the knob.

A dark-haired man has reached the collapsed Héloïse on the stairs. Villefort encounters him in the corridor. The stranger recalls being dug up from beneath the Auteuil tree, names the Château d'If and the sailor Edmond Dantès who once carried a letter for Villefort's father, and reveals that he is Dantès. Villefort seizes him and tells him to come see what he has done.

## Script comparison and sequence judgment

The visible sequence preserves the script's material story without omission or contradiction. The dialogue, prose, captions, and their attributions are exact across the batch. Benedetto's identity and relationship to Villefort unfold in the intended order; the gallery observer remains visually distinct until he names himself as Edmond Dantès. Héloïse, Villefort, Édouard, Benedetto, and the Count retain their story roles and focal relationships across every turn.

The consequential objects and settings carry cleanly: Édouard's red-and-gold tin hussar moves from his hand on page 41 to the floor outside his closed door on page 49 and remains visible at the page-50 confrontation; the cut-crystal poison vial falls on the white stair on page 48 and is recovered by the Count there on page 50; the Auteuil tree named in court connects Benedetto's accusation to Dantès's identification; and the house's white stair, iron rail, upstairs corridor, and boy's door make the return home spatially legible. The court-to-carriage-to-house transitions are causal and immediate, and the black carriage on page 47 prepares the Count's arrival rather than making it arbitrary.

The focal line stays with Villefort's collapse while allowing the Count's watchful presence to become explicit at the proper moments. Each page turn pays off the preceding hook: the child left with Héloïse leads into the trial under a sustained threat; Benedetto's biographical answers tighten into accusation and confession; the confession receives a full aftermath in the carriage; Héloïse's admission leads through the silent toy-and-door beat; and Dantès's revelation ends on the consequential demand to witness the result.

The pacing is forceful but not compressed summary. Pages 43–46 sustain one courtroom confrontation, yet each page performs a distinct escalation—recognition, identification, accusation, confession—and page 47 supplies its emotional and causal aftermath. The batch varies its page mode through the full-court spectacle on page 42, the intimate questioning pages, the illustrated-prose carriage passage on page 47, the silent corridor spectacle on page 49, and the direct confrontation on page 50. No consequential beat inside the batch lacks an aftermath, and no page-turn failure creates material reader confusion.
