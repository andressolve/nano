# Batch 31–40 sequence gate

## Uninterrupted visible sequence (recorded before script comparison)

The Count uses the empty house beside Villefort’s to enter through a newly cut opening and wait inside Valentine’s wardrobe. He watches Valentine’s stepmother pour red drops into the water by Valentine’s bed, then stops Valentine from crying out, identifies himself, shows her the poisoned glass, identifies her stepmother as the killer, and warns that the house will accept Valentine’s death. He offers a white pill and asks Valentine to trust him as Maximilien does; she takes it.

The next morning Valentine appears dead. Villefort stands at the bed without weeping, Madame de Villefort weeps in the doorway, and Noirtier stays beside his granddaughter’s body. Later Villefort asks Noirtier what he has to say about Valentine’s empty chair. Through the alphabet method, Noirtier spells “HÉLOÏSE,” identifying Villefort’s wife as the poisoner.

At Valentine’s rain-soaked tomb, Maximilien mourns and accuses the Count of breaking his promise that Valentine would not die. The Count urges him away because the household is watching. That night the Count carries the apparently dead Valentine out of the opened tomb and into a waiting carriage.

The draught should have worn off by the next morning, but Valentine remains unconscious after her body has already endured another poison that month. Haydée stays beside her while the Count waits through three nights, refuses to tell Maximilien because Valentine may never wake, and faces the approaching trial in fear.

On the night before the trial, the Count visits the condemned young man who expects execution. He keeps his promise to reveal the prisoner’s father: Gérard de Villefort, the King’s Attorney, who will be prosecuting him. He supplies the prisoner’s birth date as proof and directs him to name Villefort in court, turning the coming trial toward public exposure.

## Script comparison and verdict

The pages preserve the scripted story without a material omission or contradiction. Page 31 establishes the Count’s covert access and wait; page 32 pays that turn with the poisoning witnessed; pages 33–34 convert the discovery into Valentine’s informed choice; page 35 shows the household accepting her apparent death; page 36 converts Noirtier’s vigil into his identification of Héloïse; pages 37–38 carry Maximilien’s grief into the Count’s secret recovery of Valentine; page 39 supplies the necessary complication and emotional aftermath when she does not wake; and page 40 turns the approaching trial into the Count’s next move through Benedetto.

All scripted dialogue, prose, printed text, dates, and named identities are present and attributed intelligibly. Valentine, the Count, Madame de Villefort/Héloïse, Villefort, Noirtier, Maximilien, Haydée, and Benedetto remain distinguishable across their transitions. The poisoned bedside glass, white pastille, apparent death, tomb and coffin, waiting carriage, Valentine’s continued unconsciousness, and the impending trial retain their consequence from page to page. Changes of setting are legible and causally motivated.

Focal integrity holds throughout: each page concentrates on the beat that drives its turn, and the concealment from Maximilien remains emotionally clear rather than confusing. The batch alternates illustrated prose, spectacle, and dramatic pages sufficiently to avoid a wall of one mode. The two-page bedroom dialogue earns its space; the run does not read as compressed summary. Major actions receive aftermath: the poisoning leads to Valentine’s decision, the feigned death to household and Maximilien grief, the rescue to the failed awakening, and the trial setup to Benedetto’s prepared revelation.

No material sequence, text, identity, continuity, focal, pacing, or page-turn failure requires a complete-page redraw.
