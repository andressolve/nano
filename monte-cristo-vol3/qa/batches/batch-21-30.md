# Sequence gate — canonical pages 21–30

## Blind sequence read

At Auteuil, the Count tells the story of the child buried beneath the tree and watches the suggestion strike both Villefort and the false prince. Villefort returns alone after midnight, digs beneath the tree, and finds the grave empty while the Count watches from the house.

In Villefort's home, Héloïse poisons Valentine's grandmother with the Count's medicine. The death makes Valentine rich and removes her suitors. Villefort recognizes poison and confirms through Noirtier's eyes that someone in the house is responsible, but refuses to learn the name rather than expose his family. Héloïse then all but tells the Count that Valentine is next in the line of succession; the innocent gesture of her son offering the Count his soldier makes his silence land as a moral choice.

Haydée confronts the Count over the dead woman and the danger to Valentine. He answers by exposing Benedetto, and Villefort undertakes to prosecute the false prince without realizing that he is demanding his own son's death. Héloïse then sends Valentine poisoned chocolate. Valentine collapses before the helpless Noirtier, but unexpectedly survives. Maximilien brings the crisis to the Count, who prevents him from going to police controlled by Villefort and promises that Valentine will live. At her bedside, the Count discovers that Noirtier has protected her by giving her small daily doses of the same substance. That defense will not withstand another attempt, and the Count admits to Noirtier that he supplied Héloïse while declaring that his vengeance against Villefort will continue.

The emotional movement is continuous: insinuation becomes terror, terror becomes concealed murder, concealment becomes complicity, and complicity puts Valentine in immediate danger. Her collapse converts the Count's detached manipulation into a promise he must personally keep; the final exchange offers a narrow reprieve rather than relief.

## Script comparison and gate judgment

The pages preserve the material story and the complete causal chain. Dialogue, captions, prose, and attribution match the batch script across the transitions. Villefort, Héloïse, Benedetto, the Count, Noirtier, Valentine, Haydée, and Maximilien remain legible and consistent as the focal characters change. The consequential objects and settings carry correctly: the Auteuil tree and empty grave; the red poison and warm milk; Héloïse's violet dress; Benedetto's green coat; the son's tin hussar; the anonymous letter; Valentine's chocolate cup; and Noirtier's protective medicine.

The page turns function as intended. Each threat receives a consequential payoff or aftermath within the batch: the garden story leads to the empty grave; the grandmother's death leads to Villefort's suppression and Héloïse's inheritance logic; Héloïse's warning leads to Haydée's challenge and Benedetto's arrest; Valentine's poisoning leads to Maximilien's appeal and the explanation of her survival. Villefort's decision to prosecute Benedetto remains active pressure rather than an abandoned beat.

Pacing is controlled rather than compressed. Pages 23 and 27 use illustrated prose for elapsed events, while pages 22 and 28 supply strong visual set pieces and the remaining dramatic pages change scale, setting, and emotional mode. The run therefore does not read as either summary or an undifferentiated wall of confrontations. The focal line stays with the consequences of the Count's intervention throughout.

No material sequence, continuity, text, attribution, identity, focal, pacing, aftermath, or page-turn failure justifies a complete-page redraw.
