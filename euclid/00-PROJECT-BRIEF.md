## One-sentence window

*When kings could redraw borders and scattered knowledge could vanish with one damaged scroll, an undocumented Greek teacher in the new city of Alexandria tried to build one thing power could not revise: a road of reasons beginning with almost nothing and strong enough to outlive him.*

## Human spine

This book is about Euclid, not about a list of theorems.

He begins as a man who believes a proof can be purified of every human weakness. He is right about the proof and wrong about the people. Building the *Elements* forces him to confront three things:

1. Knowledge has many makers, including people whose names history did not preserve.
2. A truth that cannot be taught, copied, or used will not survive.
3. His own name may disappear, while the chain he builds continues.

His emotional movement is from **control** to **custodianship**. He does not end by owning geometry. He ends by accepting responsibility for carrying it forward.

## Story status and invention policy

Almost nothing dependable is known about Euclid's personal life. The opening page says so plainly. This is **a novel of Euclid**, not a recovered documentary biography.

Historically anchored:

- Euclid worked in Alexandria around 300 BCE.
- He is credited with the thirteen-book *Elements*.
- The work organizes earlier mathematics into a rigorous deductive sequence.
- The royal-road and profit anecdotes survive only in late tradition.
- No original manuscript or trustworthy portrait survives.

Invented:

- Euclid's childhood and motive.
- Nefru, Philippos, teachers, copyists, rivals, and most scenes.
- The manuscript crises, deadlines, arguments, and personal relationships.
- All dialogue except the traditional royal-road sentence, which is itself labeled as a later story.

The full ledger is `../euclid-research/RESEARCH.md`.

## Curricular connection

The active Lyceum curriculum explicitly begins with Euclidean geometry: angles, parallel lines, congruence, triangles, circles, and the Pythagorean theorem. This volume is therefore the origin story of work the readers are already doing.

The reader can reenact the central construction with paper, compass, and straightedge:

1. Draw a segment.
2. Draw a circle centered at each endpoint, using the segment as the radius.
3. Join either circle intersection to the endpoints.
4. Explain why all three sides must be equal.

## Pacing commitment

The book is deliberately not compressed into a conventional 18–20 page biography. The historical gap creates a risk of montage and abstraction; 28 pages give the invented human scenes enough cause-and-effect to read cleanly.

| Movement | Pages | Job |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| The undocumented man | 1–4 | Declare the invention policy, establish Euclid's fear of loss, and bring him to Alexandria. |
| Teacher and city | 5–9 | Show the scattered inheritance, his teaching failure, and the people around the work. |
| Building from almost nothing | 10–17 | Begin the *Elements*, make Proposition 1 doable, dramatize pride, profit, and practical measurement. |
| Power, credit, and rigor | 18–23 | Ptolemy, the royal road, authorship conflict, and Euclid's refusal to weaken the chain. |
| Completion and disappearance | 24–28 | The later books, completion, first transmission, unknown death, and the proof-shaped legacy. |

## Page-format vocabulary

- Cinematic single-image pages carry the human story.
- One two-zone memory page shows the invented formative boundary-stone event.
- One annotated-breakthrough page teaches Proposition 1.
- One quote-as-artifact page stages the royal-road tradition.
- One failure-and-study page turns the coin anecdote against Euclid's own pride.
- One partnership page shows Euclid and Nefru rebuilding the work together.
- The finale is constructed from the two intersecting circles of Proposition 1, with the transmission history inside the lens.

## Tone

- Serious, lucid, warm, and dramatically confident.
- No lone-genius worship.
- No misty “mysteries of geometry” language.
- No terse captions that make the reader infer the missing cause.
- Technical terms receive plain-language help on first use.
- The invented supporting characters are full people, not devices for asking obvious questions.

## Visual motif

**The line and the two circles.**

- A line first appears as a moved boundary: power changes where it lies.
- Euclid later draws a line as a stated starting object: power cannot change what follows from it.
- Two circles meet above the line in Proposition 1.
- Their lens shape recurs in lamps, arches, harbor ropes, and the reader progress strip.
- The final page uses the construction itself as the architecture of Euclid's legacy.
