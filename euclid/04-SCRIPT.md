## PAGE 1 — The Empty Space · `pages/page-01.png`

**Prototype 1: cinematic hook and uncertainty contract.**

**Scene:** Master Euclid alone in the workroom before dawn. A long blank papyrus sheet stretches toward the viewer; one small finished diagram sits at the far end. Through the columns, young Alexandria is waking. Medium-wide camera low across the table, making the blank papyrus feel like an unknown life waiting to be crossed.

**LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:**

- Top band: `Around 300 BCE, a mathematician named Euclid worked in Alexandria, Egypt. He wrote or assembled a work called the Elements. It became one of the most influential books ever made.`
- Upper-right box: `History preserved the book and almost nothing about the man. We do not know where he was born, who raised him, whom he loved, or how he died. No trustworthy portrait survives.`
- Bottom band: `So this is not a recovered life. It is a novel built inside the empty space: a story about the kind of person who might try to make knowledge stronger than memory, fire, pride, and kings.`

**Density:** T4, about 104 words. **Refs:** master Euclid, workroom, Alexandria.

**LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:**

## PAGE 9 — The Work He Chose · `pages/page-09.png`

**Scene:** Night workroom. Younger Euclid has arranged dozens of scroll fragments and wax tablets into long rows across tables and floor. At center is a fresh papyrus roll. Nefru watches from the doorway holding an oil lamp. High three-quarter camera shows the scale of the sorting.

**LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:**

- Top band: `Euclid did not decide to write down everything mathematicians knew. That would have produced a warehouse of results, impressive and impossible to trust.`
- Upper-right box: `He chose a harder task: select a small set of starting points, arrange earlier discoveries in a dependable order, repair weak demonstrations, and make each proposition rely only on what the reader had already accepted or proved.`
- Nefru, right balloon: `How long will that take?`
- Euclid, left balloon: `Until the last page owes nothing to a missing page.`
- Bottom band: `He called the work Stoicheia — Elements: the basic pieces from which a larger structure can be built.`

## PAGE 10 — Say What You Mean · `pages/page-10.png`

**Template:** failure-and-study page.

**Scene:** Younger Euclid at the sand table before students. He has drawn a dot, a line, and a circle. Philippos points to the dot with the stylus, puzzled. Close working composition; large simple marks visible without modern labels.

**LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:**

- Top band: `The Elements does not begin with a dazzling theorem. It begins by defining its words. A point has no part. A line has length without breadth. A circle is bounded by one line whose points are equally distant from its center.`

**Density:** T4, about 112 words. **Refs:** younger Euclid, Philippos, workroom/tools.

## PAGE 13 — The First Construction · `pages/page-13.png`

**Prototype 2: annotated breakthrough and primary-source artifact.**

**Scene:** Warm papyrus poster treated as a real sheet lying on a worktable. At center: the correct Proposition 1 construction—one horizontal segment, two equal-radius circles centered at its endpoints, intersecting above and below, with the upper intersection joined to both endpoints. Around it, four large papyrus explanation boxes connected by thin bronze leader lines. At the bottom edge, Euclid's and Philippos's hands hold compass and straightedge; no faces needed.

**LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:**

- Title at top: `BOOK I · PROPOSITION 1`
- Subtitle directly beneath: `Begin with any finite straight line. Construct an equilateral triangle upon it.`
- Upper-left callout: `1. Put the compass point on the left end. Open it to the right end. Draw a circle.`
- Upper-right callout: `2. Without changing the opening, put the compass point on the right end. Draw a second circle.`
- Lower-left callout: `3. Join either meeting point of the circles to both ends of the original line.`
- Lower-right callout: `4. Each new side is a radius of one circle. Both radii equal the original line. Therefore all three sides are equal.`
- Small bottom ribbon: `What was required has been done.`

**Density:** T5, about 106 words in 7 elements. **Refs:** workroom/tools. **Hard constraint:** the construction must be geometrically correct and all text large.

- Top band: `The Elements did not stop with shapes. Books Seven through Nine studied whole numbers. One proof showed that prime numbers — numbers divisible only by one and themselves — can never run out.`

## PAGE 25 — What Cannot Share a Measure · `pages/page-25.png`

**Scene:** Night workroom. Master Euclid and Nefru study a square drawn on papyrus and compare its side to its diagonal using dividers. A pile of unsuccessful repeated subdivisions sits as marks on wax tablets. Close composition on hands and two faces.

**LETTERING — verbatim, render exactly:**

## What history knows—and what this novel invented

Euclid was a Greek mathematician active in Alexandria around 300 BCE and is credited with the thirteen-book *Elements*. The book gathers and rigorously organizes mathematical work created across generations. Nearly every personal scene you have just read—including Euclid's childhood, Nefru, Philippos, and the making of the manuscript—is invented.

The “royal road” and coin stories are ancient traditions preserved long after Euclid's lifetime, not eyewitness reports. We used them because, like the famous Gauss classroom story, they express a lasting idea even when their literal history is uncertain. The difference is made visible here: the novel tells them confidently, and this note tells you what kind of evidence stands behind them.

What does not need invention is the central achievement. Begin with definitions and a few permitted moves. Prove one result. Use it to prove the next. Build a structure a reader can question from the first line to the last. That is why the *Elements* survived the man.
