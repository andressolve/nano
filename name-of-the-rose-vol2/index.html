End of Book Two

The Name of the Rose

The abbey is gone. The library is gone. William and Adso have ridden down the mountain with no book saved, no order discovered, and only memory left to carry the dead. Decades later, old Adso returns to the ruins, gathers a few unreadable scraps of vellum, and writes what he can before the names disappear.

The rose remains only as a name.

Five questions on what you have read →

Test Your Understanding

1. Why is the debate over whether Christ owned anything so dangerous on the fifth day?
Yes. The question sounds abstract, but it decides whether the Church can own kingdoms and still claim to imitate Christ. The Franciscans say Christ owned nothing; Pope John XXII says Christ owned what he used. In 1327 that disagreement can become a death sentence.
Not quite. The danger is not grammar or William's ambition. It is power: the Franciscan doctrine of poverty threatens a wealthy Church, and the Pope's side can call the opposing answer heresy.
2. Why does Remigio's confession not solve the murders?
Right. Gui's goal is not to find the poisoner; it is to produce heresy and guilt. Remigio confesses because the irons are in front of him. The next death proves the confession false: Malachi falls with blackened fingertips while Remigio is in the cellar.
No. Remigio does confess to the murders, but the confession is coerced. The story shows why that matters: the same poison kills again when Remigio could not possibly have done it.
3. What makes the abbey library a labyrinth on purpose?
Exactly. The library is not merely big. It is organized as a secret world-map, with regions such as ANGLIA, AEGYPTUS, LEONES, and AFRICA. The confusing rooms and mirror-door protect the forbidden books, especially the finis Africae.
Not quite. The labyrinth is deliberate, not random, and soldiers are not the real guard. The architecture itself guards the books: geography, wrong turns, mirrors, and secret knowledge held by the librarian.
4. Why does Jorge fear Aristotle's lost book on comedy and laughter?
Yes. The book is dangerous to Jorge because it gives laughter intellectual authority. If monks learn to laugh at sacred things, he believes fear will vanish, and with fear gone, the world he has served will collapse.
No. The book is not evidence of theft and not a map. It is dangerous because of what it says about comedy: laughter can be examined by reason, not only condemned as disorder.
5. What does William mean when he tells Adso there is no order in the universe?
Correct. William is not giving up on reason. He is giving up on the fantasy that the world must fit a perfect pattern. He found the book by guessing, being wrong, and then paying attention to the error.
Not quite. William still believes in thinking. He rejects certainty, not reason. The lesson is that a wrong theory can help if you are willing to notice where it breaks.
