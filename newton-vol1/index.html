Test Your Understanding

1. When Isaac Newton was born at Woolsthorpe in the winter of 1642, what was true of him?
Exactly. His father was dead before he was born, and the newborn was so small the household women did not expect him to survive the winter. The man who would later explain the motion of planets began as a fragile baby nobody thought would live a week.
Not quite. His father had already died, and Isaac was born so small that no one thought he would live. That fragile beginning is where the story starts — not in greatness, but in survival.
2. In 1665, plague closed Cambridge and sent Newton home to Woolsthorpe. What did he do during those quiet plague years?
Yes. Alone in his mother's farmhouse for two years, Newton invented a new kind of mathematics (fluxions — what we now call calculus), let a beam through a shutter and discovered that white light is made of all the colors, and began to wonder whether the same force that pulls an apple also pulls the moon. He later said he was “in the prime of my age for invention.”
Not quite. Shut up at Woolsthorpe during the plague, Newton did the most extraordinary scientific work any young person has ever done: he invented calculus, proved white light is made of colors, and first asked whether the same force pulls both the apple and the moon. These quiet years were the engine of the rest of his life.
3. Newton let a narrow beam of sunlight pass through a glass prism and threw a rainbow onto the wall. Then he did something that proved the colors were not being made by the prism. What was it?
Right. That is the second-prism experiment. If the prism were truly creating colors, a pure red beam put through a second prism would have split again. It didn't — red stayed red, blue stayed blue. That proved the colors were already inside white light. The prism only pulls them apart.
Not quite. He took one color from the first rainbow and put it through a second prism. It did not split again. That proved the prism wasn't making the colors — they were already hidden inside white light. This was called the experimentum crucis, the crucial experiment.
4. Newton's great insight about the moon was not that it is pulled toward Earth, but something stranger. What was it?
Exactly. The moon is falling — all the time. But it is also moving sideways so fast that by the time it has fallen a little, the Earth has curved away beneath it. That is what an orbit is: a perpetual, sideways fall. The same law that drops an apple holds the moon in the sky.
Not quite. The moon is falling, constantly, toward the Earth. But it is also flying sideways fast enough that the Earth's curve falls away beneath it just as fast. An orbit is a fall that never lands. One law, two cases: the apple and the moon.
5. For nearly twenty years Newton kept his deepest work to himself. What finally made him write the Principia, the book that gave the world one law of motion for all matter?
Yes! Edmond Halley came to Cambridge in 1684 and asked what curve a planet would trace around the sun. Newton answered, calmly, “An ellipse. I have calculated it.” Halley saw at once that Newton had done, privately, the single greatest piece of science in Europe. Halley pushed him to write it down and then paid out of his own pocket to publish the Principia. Without Halley, there might have been no book.
Not quite. It was Edmond Halley — the young astronomer later famous for the comet — who came to Cambridge in 1684, asked what curve planets trace, and discovered Newton had already answered it years earlier. Halley coaxed the book out of him and paid to print it himself. The Principia exists because of that one visit.
