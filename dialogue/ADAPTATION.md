# Research and Adaptation Standard

This is the canonical upstream procedure for every new Dialogue Studio work.
It ends before reference generation or story-page production. `PLAYBOOK.md`
governs only after this phase passes. `AUDIENCE.md` supplies the default reader
profile unless the owner explicitly overrides it for a project.

## 1. Governing outcome

The studio makes ultra-premium stories for a named audience. It does not make
academic editions, illustrated plot summaries, completeness exercises, or
historical reconstructions whose drama is secondary to fidelity.

Research creates understanding, not obligations. An adaptation owes its source
intelligent engagement, not obedience. It may cut, combine, reorder, simplify,
expand, change viewpoint, or invent connective material whenever that creates
stronger storytelling for the intended readers. Historical and cultural
research protects the credibility of the world and any owner-designated claims;
it does not dictate the scene list. No character, episode, explanation, proper
noun, or famous incident survives merely because it appears in the source.

The test is not whether the adaptation preserves the most material. The test is
whether the intended readers understand, feel, anticipate, and urgently want
the next scene.

## 2. Upstream authority

During research and adaptation, authority descends in this order:

1. the owner's audience, purpose, boundaries, and explicit decisions;
2. the owner-approved audience promise and adaptation brief;
3. the owner-approved architecture, visual direction, script, and page
   contract;
4. protected historical, cultural, ethical, or franchise claims explicitly
   named in the adaptation brief;
5. research dossiers and source maps as evidence;
6. source texts, scholarship, and prior adaptations as inputs rather than
   narrative masters.

Accuracy disputes matter when they affect a protected claim, cultural
credibility, ethical treatment, or the story the project promises to tell.
Mere departure from a source is never a defect.

## 3. Bounded research runs

Research begins with questions, not indiscriminate reading. Split large works
into bounded runs by source, episode cluster, historical question, audience
risk, or visual world. A fresh researcher receives the research brief and only
the sources needed for its question. It saves evidence to disk and returns a
compact handoff; the adaptation orchestrator does not retain raw transcripts.

Every source-map entry is tagged as one of:

- `PROTECTED`: a claim the owner may require the adaptation to preserve;
- `STORY_OPTION`: material available for transformation or omission;
- `VARIANT`: conflicting tellings or uncertain evidence;
- `SENSITIVE`: material requiring deliberate audience treatment;
- `BACKGROUND_ONLY`: useful maker knowledge that should not burden the story.

The researcher records provenance, confidence, disagreement, and dramatic
possibility. It does not write the adaptation, score fidelity, or turn every
finding into required exposition. Research closes with a synthesized handoff,
not a pile of context for the next agent.

## 4. Audience promise and adaptation design

Before a full script, lock an audience promise that names:

- the actual intended readers and what they already enjoy;
- the emotional promise and central dramatic question;
- the repeatable pleasures of this particular work;
- the complexity budget: names, factions, time jumps, exposition, and assumed
  knowledge;
- tonal and content boundaries;
- what this project deliberately refuses to become.

The adaptation brief then defines:

- protagonist or ensemble spine, desire, opposition, stakes, and change;
- viewpoint, agency, narrative voice, and the emotional owner of each movement;
- material to cut, combine, reorder, simplify, expand, or invent;
- protected claims and the reason each is protected;
- the volume promise, ending payoff, and surrounding world worth revealing;
- explicit exclusions, including beloved source material that weakens this
  telling.

Architecture is built as drama, not coverage. Every retained scene needs a
desire, pressure, turn, consequence, and reason to continue. Complexity arrives
when the reader has a reason to care. Page count follows emotional duration and
comfortable reading; it is not a compression target. Add scenes, reactions,
silence, panels, or pages when they make causality and feeling land.

## 5. Ultra-premium story test

A proposal is not ready merely because it is coherent. It must show:

- immediate human access to the protagonist, problem, or wonder;
- meaningful agency rather than a passive tour through famous incidents;
- escalating desire, opposition, consequence, and discovery;
- dialogue that changes relationships or decisions rather than transporting
  notes;
- exposition dramatized, delayed, visualized, or moved to optional end matter;
- enough quiet and reaction for important events to acquire weight;
- distinct set pieces serving one emotional spine instead of episodic novelty;
- a satisfying volume-level transformation and payoff;
- a density a normal reader can enjoy once, without study or rereading;
- genuine humor, terror, wonder, tenderness, strategy, or surprise appropriate
  to the work—not prestige solemnity by default.

Any scene retained mainly for completeness, prestige, pedagogy, or source
reverence fails this test.

## 6. Graphical direction

Graphical style is story design, not decoration added after the script. Before
greenlight, define:

- visual register and why it fits the audience and emotional promise;
- historical/cultural anchors and where stylization is welcome;
- character separation, silhouettes, ages, bodies, costume logic, and faction
  readability;
- environments, geography, technology, recurring objects, and visual motifs;
- palette and lighting progression tied to story movement;
- panel grammar, action clarity, quiet-page rhythm, and page-turn strategy;
- native lettering approach and dialogue-density expectations;
- visual cliches, anachronisms, generic genre defaults, and living-artist
  imitation to avoid;
- the later reference plan: style anchors, identities, settings, and only the
  consequential objects production will need.

Research and adaptation may inspect lawful visual references and save source
links or notes. They do not generate prototype story pages. Actual references
and style anchors are a separately approved preparation step after greenlight.

## 7. Two-stage audience critic

Use a fresh critic with no research or adaptation history.

**Stage 1: story-only read.** Give the critic the audience promise, proposed
story, architecture, and script or representative scripted sequence. Hide the
source map, original work, fidelity notes, and maker rationale. The critic
reports what the story is, why the protagonist acts, where attention rises or
drops, what is confusing or overcompressed, what feels like explanation rather
than drama, and whether the promised readers would want the next scene.

**Stage 2: protected-claim check.** Only after recording the story read, reveal
the adaptation brief's protected claims, sensitive-material decisions, and
liberties ledger. Check contradictions, cultural or ethical harm, and promises
the adaptation itself claims to preserve. Do not report unprotected departures,
omissions, combinations, invention, chronology changes, or lack of
completeness.

An approval says the proposed telling works as a story for its audience and
honors its declared constraints. It never certifies academic fidelity.

## 8. Owner-facing greenlight proposal

The adaptation run ends by saving and presenting `adaptation/GREENLIGHT.md`.
It is a decision document, not a research dump. It includes:

1. one-paragraph audience promise and story hook;
2. the proposed complete story and volume ending;
3. how the story will be told: viewpoint, voice, structure, scene rhythm,
   dialogue approach, density, and page-count rationale;
4. principal character arcs and relationships;
5. major set pieces and emotional progression;
6. what the adaptation cuts, combines, reorders, simplifies, expands, or
   invents—and why;
7. protected claims, sensitivities, and deliberate departures;
8. graphical direction, including style register, palette, character/world
   design, panel grammar, lettering, motifs, and reference plan;
9. audience-critic result, unresolved risks, and exact owner decisions needed;
10. the proposed next production-preparation step, with no image generation
    silently authorized.

The orchestrator must also summarize this proposal directly to the owner. A
file silently saved to disk is not presentation. Production remains closed
until the owner records explicit approval in `adaptation/OWNER-APPROVAL.md`.

## 9. Production handoff gate

Before reference generation or page production:

- research files are complete and synthesized;
- audience promise, adaptation brief, story architecture, graphical direction,
  complete script, and page contract are locked to the owner-approved scope;
- the two-stage audience critic says `APPROVED`;
- `GREENLIGHT.md` has been presented;
- `OWNER-APPROVAL.md` records the owner, date, approved scope, and any tolerated
  risk, plus the exact project-relative script and contract paths;
- those exact script and contract documents each say `Status: LOCKED`;
- the deterministic adaptation-readiness check reports clean.

From the project directory run:

```sh
python3 -B ../../tools/check_adaptation.py .
```

Only then may `SESSION-START.md` open the production preparation and
builder-critic workflow.
