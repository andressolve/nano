## Quiz (apply the WHY-not-WHAT rule and the no-length-tell rule)

Test why things happened. Distractors must be substantive — plausible-sounding wrong answers with period detail, similar in length to the correct answer. Correct positions shuffled across the five.

### Question 1 — The 1693 breakdown

**Why did Newton write to Pepys in September 1693 apologising for being "embroiled" and unable to sleep?**

- a) Because the publication of the *Opticks* had been delayed by Robert Hooke and he held Pepys responsible. *(wrong — Opticks was 1704, after Hooke's death)*
- b) **Because after years of intense work on the *Principia* and on alchemical experiments, his mind broke down and he was sleepless for five nights together.** *(correct)*
- c) Because Halifax had withdrawn the offer of the Mint and Newton blamed himself for having insulted him. *(wrong — Halifax made the offer two years later, and Newton accepted)*

(Correct answer: **b**, middle position. All three roughly similar length.)

### Question 2 — Why he chased Chaloner

**Why did Newton, as Warden of the Mint, personally prosecute William Chaloner in 1699?**

- a) Because Chaloner had publicly accused Newton of incompetence and Newton wanted a personal revenge through the courts. *(wrong — Chaloner did write pamphlets, but the prosecution was for counterfeiting, not for the pamphlets)*
- b) Because the Recoinage had failed to stop counterfeiting and Newton needed a public hanging to prove the new coinage was secure. *(wrong — the Recoinage did succeed; Chaloner was caught after it)*
- c) **Because Chaloner was the most prolific counterfeiter in England and Newton had spent two years assembling depositions against him through informants in London coffee-houses.** *(correct)*

(Correct answer: **c**, last position. The two distractors are plausible period-grounded — both wave at real historical detail, both are wrong about the *reason* the prosecution happened.)

### Question 3 — Why he waited to publish the Opticks

**Newton finished the *Opticks* manuscript in the 1670s. Why did he not publish it until 1704?**

- a) **Because he had sworn not to publish it while his rival Robert Hooke was alive, and Hooke did not die until 1703.** *(correct)*
- b) Because the experiments needed in the *Opticks* required precise prisms that English glass-blowers could not manufacture until the turn of the century. *(wrong — period detail-flavored distractor; the prism was not the holdup)*
- c) Because the Royal Society had refused to print it on the grounds that its claims about white light contradicted the Aristotelian curriculum then taught at Oxford and Cambridge. *(wrong — fits the period feel, contains nothing true)*

(Correct answer: **a**, first position. All three similar length.)

### Question 4 — Why he chaired the Leibniz committee in secret

**The Royal Society's *Commercium Epistolicum* of 1712 was supposed to be an impartial committee report deciding who had invented calculus first. Why was it not actually impartial?**

- a) Because Leibniz refused to send the committee any of his own letters, and the committee was forced to rule on incomplete evidence weighted toward Newton's English papers. *(wrong — Leibniz was never asked)*
- b) **Because Newton had chosen the committee members himself, none of them Continental mathematicians, and had secretly drafted the verdict in his own hand before publication.** *(correct)*
- c) Because the Royal Society had only English Fellows in 1712 and the rules of the Society did not allow a foreign mathematician's claim to be heard in any official committee. *(wrong — sounds plausible but invented)*

(Correct answer: **b**, middle position. The distractors are period-flavored and substantive.)

### Question 5 — The South Sea Bubble quote

**After losing twenty thousand pounds in the South Sea Bubble of 1720, Newton said he could "calculate the motion of the heavenly bodies but not the madness of people." Why did the line stick?**

- a) Because it was the first thing Newton ever published in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society, and the Society reprinted it for years afterward. *(wrong — he never wrote it down for publication; the line survives because Stukeley recorded it in conversation)*
- b) Because Newton said it on the floor of Parliament during the inquiry into the South Sea collapse, and it was entered into the official record. *(wrong — there was a parliamentary inquiry, but Newton did not testify with this line)*
- c) **Because it admitted, from the man who had calculated the motion of the heavens, that there were forces in human behavior he could not predict — and it was recorded by William Stukeley, who wrote the first memoir of Newton's life.** *(correct)*

(Correct answer: **c**, last position. Distractors are substantive, period-grounded, and plausible.)

**Correct-answer positions across the quiz:** b, c, a, b, c. None are "always c"; none are "always last"; distractors are similar in length to the correct answers.
