The Invisible Forces — Isaac Newton

Isaac Newton (1642–1727) was buried in Westminster Abbey on 28 March 1727. In his private papers, kept by Catherine Barton's husband John Conduitt and passed down to the Portsmouth family, were a million words on alchemy and another million on the prophecies of Daniel. They were sold at Sotheby's in 1936. The economist John Maynard Keynes bought them and called the man who wrote them “the last of the magicians.”

Five questions on what you have just read →

Test Your Understanding

1. Why did Newton write to Pepys in September 1693 apologising for being “embroiled” and unable to sleep?
Yes. In September 1693 Newton wrote to Pepys saying he was “extremely troubled by the embroilment” he was in, had neither eaten nor slept well in twelve months, and had lost his former consistency of mind. He was 50 years old and had spent the previous six years on the Principia and on intense alchemical experiments. Something inside him gave way. He recovered, but the man who stood up from the desk was not the same.
Not quite. The Opticks was published in 1704, after Hooke's death — nothing to do with 1693. And Halifax's Mint offer came two years after the breakdown, in 1695, and Newton accepted it. The real reason was simpler and more frightening: years of overwork on the Principia and on his secret alchemical experiments cracked his mind, and for several months he could not sleep or trust his own letters.
2. Why did Newton, as Warden of the Mint, personally prosecute William Chaloner in 1699?
Right. Chaloner cast shillings that passed at every market, printed his own bank-notes, and was even talking himself into selling the Mint advice on how to catch the counterfeiters — meaning him. No previous Warden had ever really tried to prosecute counterfeiters. Newton tried. He walked into London taverns in a plain coat, paid informants, took depositions in coffee-houses, and in two years built more than a hundred cases. Chaloner was the largest of them.
Not quite. Chaloner did write pamphlets attacking the Mint, but Newton prosecuted him for counterfeiting, not for the pamphlets. And the Recoinage actually succeeded — Chaloner was caught precisely because the new milled coin made his trade harder. The real reason for the prosecution was that Chaloner was the most prolific counterfeiter in England, and Newton had quietly built the case against him over two years of coffee-house depositions.
3. Newton finished the Opticks manuscript in the 1670s. Why did he not publish it until 1704?
Yes. Newton and Robert Hooke had quarreled over light and color since the 1670s. Newton had sworn he would not publish the Opticks while Hooke lived. He kept it in a drawer for thirty years. Hooke died on 3 March 1703. Within weeks Newton went to his desk, opened the drawer, took out the manuscript, and prepared it for the press. It came out in 1704, his first major book since the Principia seventeen years earlier.
Not quite. The prism was never the holdup — Newton had been doing the experiments with English-made prisms since the 1660s. And the Royal Society never refused the book; in fact Newton became its President shortly before the Opticks appeared. The real reason was personal: he had sworn he would not publish the book while Robert Hooke lived. Hooke died in 1703, and the Opticks came out the next year.
4. The Royal Society's Commercium Epistolicum of 1712 was supposed to be an impartial committee report deciding who had invented calculus first. Why was it not actually impartial?
Exactly. As President of the Royal Society, Newton chose the committee. He chose its members. He wrote its report. He printed it in Latin so all Europe could read it, then again in English. The Commercium Epistolicum’s verdict, predictably, was that he had invented calculus first. He never put his name on the report. Everyone in Europe knew he had written every word.
Not quite. Leibniz was never even asked — the committee did not invite him to submit anything. And the Royal Society did have foreign Fellows and had heard foreign claims before. The real reason the report was not impartial was that Newton, as President of the Society, hand-picked every member of the committee and secretly drafted the verdict in his own hand before it was printed.
5. After losing twenty thousand pounds in the South Sea Bubble of 1720, Newton said he could “calculate the motion of the heavenly bodies but not the madness of people.” Why did the line stick?
Yes. Newton sold his South Sea shares once at a profit, then watched the price keep climbing and bought back in at the peak. The bubble burst that summer. He lost twenty thousand pounds, which was a fortune. The line stuck because the man who had calculated the motion of the planets was admitting he could not calculate the behavior of a crowd. It was preserved because William Stukeley, his friend and biographer, wrote it down in conversation. Newton would not allow the South Sea Company to be spoken of in his house.
Not quite. Newton never published the line; it survives because William Stukeley, his friend who wrote the first memoir of Newton's life, recorded it in conversation. There was a parliamentary inquiry into the South Sea collapse, but Newton did not testify with this line. The line stuck because of what it admitted: that the man who had calculated the motion of the heavens could not calculate the behavior of a crowd.
