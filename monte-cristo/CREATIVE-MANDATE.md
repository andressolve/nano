# The Count of Monte Cristo — Creative Mandate

## Status

**Locked by Andres on 2026-07-25.**

This mandate governs every later architecture, script, reference, prototype,
page, reader, and editorial decision. If another planning note points toward a
historical survey, narrative treatise, illustrated synopsis, or explanatory
adaptation, this mandate takes precedence.

## The Story Promise

> A sweeping, dialogue-driven adventure about a young sailor who loses
> everything, remakes himself into a figure of almost supernatural power, and
> discovers that revenge cannot obey him.

This is a **story**, not a historical or narrative treatise. It should astonish
the reader through character, suspense, reversals, spectacle, discovery, and
emotion.

## Governing Rules

1. **Drama carries information.** Political, financial, legal, and historical
   facts should become understandable because characters want something, fear
   something, conceal something, or act upon something.
2. **Dialogue is the primary engine.** Most pages should be live scenes with
   distinct voices, conflicting objectives, shifting power, and a meaningful
   turn.
3. **Captions are connective tissue, not lectures.** Use them sparingly for
   time, place, compression, atmosphere, or an essential transition.
4. **History supports the illusion.** Research should make costumes, ships,
   rooms, institutions, travel, and consequences convincing without becoming
   the foregrounded content.
5. **No reader homework.** A first-time reader should understand what changed,
   why it matters, and what each character now wants without filling narrative
   gaps.
6. **Explanation must remain dramatic.** Faria's teaching, Villefort's
   politics, the tunnel plan, banking, telegraphy, poison, and the family
   networks must be staged as discovery, danger, persuasion, conflict, or
   choice—not as detached diagrams or essays.
7. **Every page earns its place.** A page should deliver at least one of:
   desire, opposition, discovery, reversal, decision, consequence, intimacy,
   wonder, or spectacle.
8. **The Count is fascinating, then frightening.** The story should let readers
   thrill to Edmond's intelligence and mastery before they feel the cost of his
   certainty.
9. **Mercy is dramatic action.** The moral landing must arise from consequences
   the reader has lived through, not from a late explanatory lesson.
10. **Architecture serves scenes.** Volume counts, chapter maps, and historical
    scaffolding may guide production, but they must never flatten the novel into
    coverage.

## Visual and Format Commitments

- Selected graphic direction: **Velvet Cinema**.
- **Locked page format: 3:2 landscape, 1536 × 1024.** Do not generate,
  crop, redesign, or approve portrait or square story pages.
- Reference sheets may use internal multi-view layouts, but their canvas must
  also remain 3:2 landscape so the production pipeline never drifts.
- Dialogue and captions must be baked into finished page images.
- The reader provides navigation and the quiz; it is not a lettering or
  exposition layer.
- Historical detail must increase immersion while leaving visual priority to
  faces, gestures, danger, and emotional turns.

## The Standard for “Wow”

The adaptation should make scenes such as the betrothal arrest, Villefort
burning the letter, the first sound behind the prison wall, Faria's underground
school, the burial-sack escape, the treasure cave, the restored *Pharaon*,
Mercédès recognizing Edmond, Haydée's testimony, and the Count's moral collapse
feel inevitable in retrospect and astonishing in the moment.

The production test is not “Did we include the information?” It is:

> Did the reader feel compelled to turn the page?
