End of Book One

The Name of the Rose

It is the evening of the fourth day. Bernard Gui has opened his court in the chapter house. The village girl and Salvatore are in chains. Adelmo, Venantius, and Berengar are dead — all touched, on their fingers or tongue, by the same black stain. William of Baskerville has not yet named the book or the killer, but he has named the shape: every dead monk read one common volume, and the library that holds it is forbidden.

Three more days remain. The fire that ends them is not yet lit.

Five questions on what you have read →

Test Your Understanding

1. Why does William of Baskerville say, on the third night, that he does not seek the murderer — he seeks the book?
Yes. On the third midday, walking with Adso in the cloister, William says: “Three men have died, and one thing is true of all three: each of them touched a certain book in the forbidden library before they died. Find what they read, and we find why they died.” This is his master's method — the rule he learned from Roger Bacon and from his friend William of Ockham: do not invent angels and demons to explain what a poisoned book can explain. Six hundred years later, men would teach it in universities and call it science.
Not quite. The Abbot has not forbidden William's inquiry — he has actually requested it. And William does not think Salvatore is the killer at this point in the story: Salvatore is a former Dolcinian heretic, but heresy is not poisoning. The real answer is the one William gives Adso in the cloister: every dead monk touched the same book in the forbidden library. Find the book, find why they died.
2. When Bernard Gui's delegation arrives at the abbey on the fourth day, why does he seize the village girl — not Salvatore alone, and not any monk of the abbey?
Right. Bernard Gui did not ride up the mountain to solve a poisoning. He came for the papal delegation, which is debating whether Christ owned his own clothes — the Franciscan poverty question. A peasant girl in the kitchen at night, with a former Dolcinian by her side, is not a thief in his hands. She is a witch. And a witch tried inside the abbey lets him put the whole abbey on trial by association. The murders are William's problem. The heresy charge is Gui's prize.
Not quite. Gui does not need the Abbot's permission to arrest anyone — he is the Inquisitor of Toulouse and his warrant runs above the abbey's. And no one in the story claims monks were smuggling food to mountain heretics. What Gui actually wants is a heretic to convict. A peasant girl paired with a former Dolcinian gives him exactly that, and lets him put the whole abbey on trial by association.
3. On the second morning, Severinus the herbalist examines the dead Venantius in the pig's-blood vat and finds black staining on his tongue and on the fingertips of one hand. What does this tell William?
Yes. Severinus shows William the old herbal: a poison from the East, colorless, tasteless, that stains the skin and the tongue dark wherever it touches. A small drop on a finger is enough to kill. A man wetting his finger to turn a page would die before dawn. The black on one hand and the tongue is the signature of a poisoned book — and the same stain will appear on Berengar that same night.
Not quite. Venantius was not strangled — there are no thumb marks on his throat, only the black stain. And the Matins-chalice idea is invented; nothing in the story points there. What Severinus actually finds, with his old herbal in front of him, is the trace of a poison that stains the skin black wherever it touches — absorbed through a finger wetted to turn a page. A poisoned book.
4. William of Baskerville and his student Adso of Melk are Franciscans — brown wool habit, knotted rope belt at the waist. Why does it matter, in 1327, that they are Franciscans and not Benedictines or Dominicans?
Exactly. The meeting at the abbey is between two delegations: the Pope's, riding up with Bernard Gui, and the Franciscans', riding up with William. The question on the table is whether Christ owned the clothes he wore. The Franciscans say no — that absolute poverty is the rule of Christ. Pope John XXII has declared the doctrine of apostolic poverty itself a heresy. William is not at the abbey by accident. He is one of the delegates — and the murders begin under him.
Not quite. There was no “silver inquisitorial seal” reserved to Franciscans — that is invented. And Franciscans were not forbidden from Benedictine houses; orders crossed each other's thresholds regularly under guest-right. The real answer is political: the whole meeting at the abbey is between the Pope's delegation and the Franciscan order, over whether Christ owned property. William is a Franciscan delegate — and the Pope's side has come to convict his order of heresy.
5. William wears riveted leather-and-glass spectacles — a brand-new technology in 1327, only forty years old, invented in Pisa around 1284. On the third night Adso finds them placed beneath the dead Berengar's body in the bathtub. Why is this the moment William says, “Three dead. One book they had all read. And my master could now see again”?
Yes. William's spectacles — the riveted leather-and-glass kind, the new technology of his lifetime — had been stolen from him in the dark of the forbidden library on the night Venantius died. He had been reading half-blind ever since. When they turn up under Berengar's body, three things land at once: Berengar was the thief, so Berengar was in the library that night; Berengar therefore touched the same book the others touched; and William, with his glasses back, can finally read it. “Three dead. One book they had all read. And my master could now see again.”
Not quite. Berengar was not strangled with the spectacles — the black stain on his finger says he was poisoned the same way as Venantius. And Adso never thought William was blind. The real answer is that William had lost his spectacles two days earlier in the dark of the forbidden library, on the night Venantius died. Finding them on Berengar proves Berengar was in the library that night — ties him to the book — and gives William back the eyes he needs to read it.
