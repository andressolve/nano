# The Name of the Rose — Research Dossier

**Purpose:** Source material and analysis for a graphic-novel adaptation of Umberto Eco's *Il nome della rosa* (1980) for Francisco (9) and Sebastian (7). Goal per Andres: give the kids a window into a *different worldview* — a world where knowledge was scarce, carefully preserved, and carefully controlled. Not "dark ages" condescension; the medieval mind on its own terms. Clear, not dumbed down.

This dossier is written before any planning doc commits. Read in full before drafting `00-PROJECT-BRIEF.md`.

---

## 1. The book in one paragraph

A Franciscan friar named **William of Baskerville** and his teenage Benedictine novice **Adso of Melk** arrive at a wealthy Benedictine abbey in northern Italy in late November **1327**. They have come for a theological summit — Franciscans (who believe Christ and the apostles owned nothing) are about to meet a papal delegation (sent by the wealthy Pope John XXII in Avignon) on what is supposed to be neutral ground. The abbot asks William, an ex-inquisitor with a Sherlock-Holmesian mind, to quietly investigate the recent death of a young illuminator named **Adelmo**, who fell from the abbey's great tower. Over **seven days**, six more monks die. William slowly realizes the deaths all trace back to the abbey's vast labyrinthine **library** — the largest in Christendom — and specifically to a single book hidden in a sealed room called the *finis Africae*. The book turns out to be the lost **second volume of Aristotle's *Poetics***, which argues that laughter and comedy are legitimate subjects of philosophy. The blind, aged librarian **Jorge of Burgos** believes that if monks learn to laugh at sacred things, faith collapses, and so he has been quietly poisoning the pages so that anyone who turns them dies. In the final confrontation, Jorge eats the poisoned pages himself, knocks over a lamp, and the entire library — irreplaceable, the memory of a civilization — burns to the ground. William and Adso escape. William dies later of plague. Old Adso, decades later, returns to the ruins, gathers charred fragments, and writes the book we are reading.

## 2. Frame and structure

- **Frame narrator** ("Naturally, a manuscript"): a modern editor claims to have found a 19th-c. French translation of a 17th-c. Latin edition of Adso's lost 14th-c. memoir. Layered, deliberately destabilized.
- **Inner narrator:** old Adso of Melk, writing in his Austrian monastery at the end of his life, recounting events from 60+ years earlier.
- **Time:** 7 days, **late November 1327** (snowy, cold, short days).
- **Each day** is subdivided by the **eight canonical hours** of the Benedictine Rule. These are the heartbeat of the abbey's life. They are: **Matins** (~2:30 AM), **Lauds** (~5 AM, dawn), **Prime** (~6 AM, sunrise), **Terce** (~9 AM), **Sext** (~12 noon), **None** (~3 PM), **Vespers** (~sunset), **Compline** (~bedtime). The clock is the church bell, not a number.

## 3. Setting

- **An unnamed Benedictine abbey** somewhere in the Apennines or alpine foothills of northern Italy. Eco modeled it on multiple real abbeys (San Michele della Chiusa, Sacra di San Michele).
- The abbey owns vast lands, has a wealthy abbot, employs lay workers, runs a herbarium, infirmary, kitchens, refectory, cloister, chapter house, dormitory, stables, pigsties.
- **The Aedificium** — a massive fortified octagonal-cornered tower that dominates the abbey. Three floors:
  - **Ground floor:** kitchens and refectory.
  - **First floor:** the **scriptorium**, where monks copy and illuminate manuscripts (the abbey's main intellectual product).
  - **Top floor:** the **library** — a labyrinth of 56 rooms whose layout secretly maps the known world. The books are shelved by region. Only the librarian and his assistant know how to navigate it. Visitors are forbidden.
- **The finis Africae** ("end of Africa"): a hidden room in the south tower of the library, sealed behind a mirror-door, containing the most dangerous books. Named because adjacent rooms hold African authors.

## 4. The day-by-day skeleton (from LitCharts breakdown — verified)

**Prologue.** Frame narrator + old Adso introduces William and the journey.

**Day 1.** Arrive. William deduces a runaway horse named *Brunellus* from snow tracks (Sherlock-opening). Abbot Abo confides about Adelmo's death. Meet most of the named monks. Long debate with Jorge of Burgos about whether laughter is permissible.

**Day 2.** **Venantius** (translator) found upended in a barrel of pig's blood. Fingertips and tongue blackened. William can't see clearly — someone has stolen his reading glasses. Found a coded manuscript on Venantius's desk. First failed labyrinth attempt.

**Day 3.** **Berengar** (assistant librarian) missing. Salvatore, a polyglot half-mad monk, tells Adso about his past as a follower of the heretic **Fra Dolcino**. Adso meets a starving peasant girl in the kitchen who has been trading sex to the cellarer Remigio for food; she and Adso have a brief encounter. Berengar's body found drowned in a kitchen tub, fingers and tongue blackened. Glasses recovered on his body.

**Day 4.** Severinus (herbalist) confirms blackened tongues = poison. Salvatore admits he procures women for Remigio. William starts deciphering the Greek on Venantius's parchment. The papal/Franciscan delegations arrive — including the inquisitor **Bernard Gui** (real historical Dominican, c. 1261–1331, author of the actual *Practica Inquisitionis*). Salvatore and the village girl are arrested as witches by Gui.

**Day 5.** Theological disputation: did Christ own property? William speaks for the Franciscan position. **Severinus** is murdered after telling William he has found a strange Arabic-bound book. Bernard Gui interrogates Remigio; under torture-threat Remigio falsely confesses to everything, including the murders. Gui condemns him, Salvatore, and the village girl to be burned.

**Day 6.** **Malachi** the librarian collapses dead at morning prayers, fingers blackened — proving Remigio innocent of the murders. Adso, exhausted, sleeps in church and dreams of the *Coena Cypriani* (a real medieval Latin parody of the Last Supper as a wedding feast — a "blasphemous" comic text). The dream cracks William's case. He examines the library catalogue handwriting and deduces a *secret*, unrecorded librarian who has guided every official librarian for decades. Abbot Abo, panicking, tells William to stop. They defy him. They find the secret door to the *finis Africae*.

**Day 7.** William and Adso enter the *finis Africae*. **Jorge of Burgos** is waiting in the dark. He explains everything. He has been the secret librarian for decades. The book is the **lost second book of Aristotle's *Poetics***, the only surviving copy, a treatise arguing comedy is a legitimate art. Jorge believes laughter dissolves the fear of God; if monks laugh at the devil, they will laugh at God; if they laugh at God, the order of the world ends. He poisoned the pages' corners with a contact poison so anyone wetting their finger to turn pages would die. He invites William to read. William refuses to touch it bare-handed (he uses a glove). Jorge then eats the pages himself, knocks over Adso's lantern, and the library catches fire. William tries to save books; Adso drags him out. The fire spreads to the whole abbey. Days later, only ash. The Abbot is also dead (trapped in a passage by Jorge). Adso and William ride away. The book is gone forever.

**Last page.** Old Adso, decades later, has returned to the ruins, sifted ash, recovered scattered scraps. He cannot tell what they are anymore. He is dying. The book ends with the line *"Stat rosa pristina nomine, nomina nuda tenemus"* — "The rose of old remains only in its name; we hold only naked names." A medieval Latin tag about how meaning slips away even when words remain.

## 5. Characters — who they are and what they're for

**Protagonists**
- **William of Baskerville** — English Franciscan, ~50, tall, gaunt, sharp-eyed, used to be an inquisitor but quit when he saw what it did to people. A composite of:
  - **Sherlock Holmes** (the name *Baskerville* is Conan Doyle's; the deductive method is Holmes's)
  - **William of Ockham** (the actual 14th-c. Franciscan philosopher, "Ockham's razor" — simplest explanation wins)
  - **Roger Bacon** (13th-c. Franciscan proto-scientist who insisted on observation and experiment; William's "mentor" in the book).
  - Wears spectacles — a brand-new invention in 1327, which makes him look like a magician.
- **Adso of Melk** — Benedictine novice, ~18, German, wide-eyed, intellectually hungry, occasionally lustful, our narrator. Modeled on Watson but more confused. *He is the kid surrogate.*

**The abbey's monks**
- **Abbot Abo of Fossanova** — proud, political, protects the abbey's secrets above its souls. Killed by Jorge on Day 7 (trapped in a passage).
- **Jorge of Burgos** — blind, ancient, Spanish, the most venerated monk and the secret villain. Named after Jorge Luis Borges (who wrote "The Library of Babel" and was blind). The dogmatic mind in its purest form.
- **Malachi of Hildesheim** — the official librarian. Bitter, suspicious, controlled by Jorge. Murdered on Day 6.
- **Berengar of Arundel** — assistant librarian, English, vain, attracted to Adelmo. Second victim.
- **Adelmo of Otranto** — young illuminator. Loved beauty. Slept with Berengar in exchange for a glimpse of the forbidden book. Killed himself in despair (first body — not murdered).
- **Venantius of Salvemec** — Greek translator. Found the forbidden book. Read it. Died. First poisoning victim found.
- **Severinus of Sankt Wendel** — German, the herbalist, runs the infirmary. William's natural ally. Murdered Day 5.
- **Benno of Upsala** — Scandinavian, rhetorician, ambitious, willing to trade morals for library access.
- **Remigio of Varagine** — the cellarer (kitchen + supplies). Former Dolcinian heretic in hiding. Trades food for sex. Falsely confesses under Bernard Gui's pressure.
- **Salvatore of Montferrat** — half-mad polyglot beggar-monk, also former Dolcinian. Speaks a half-comic stew of every European language. Arrested with Remigio.
- **Ubertino of Casale** — real historical figure (c. 1259–1329). Aging Franciscan spiritualist mystic, hiding at the abbey. Mentor figure to William, but on the verge of fanaticism himself.
- **Alinardo of Grottaferrata** — oldest monk in the abbey, half-senile. Drops the clue (almost by accident) that the murders seem to follow the seven trumpets of Revelation. (They don't — but Jorge plays into the pattern when he realizes William has noticed.)
- **Nicholas of Morimondo** — master glazier. Makes William new lenses.
- **Aymaro of Alessandria** — gossipy Italian monk, resentful of Northern dominance in the abbey.

**The outside world**
- **Bernard Gui** — real Dominican inquisitor (c. 1261–1331). Author of *Practica Inquisitionis* — an actual inquisitor's manual that survives, with five books including detailed templates for interrogating Cathars, Waldensians, Beguines, Pseudo-Apostles, and relapsed Jews. He served as Inquisitor of Toulouse from 1307–1323. In the novel he is cold, intelligent, terrifyingly competent at extracting confessions. Embodies the institutional violence of the Church in this era.
- **Michael of Cesena** — real Franciscan Minister General who led the order's defense of the poverty doctrine and was eventually excommunicated.
- **The papal legates** — the team sent by Pope John XXII of Avignon.
- **The village girl** — unnamed (this is part of Eco's point: the only character Adso loves is the only one whose name we never learn). Peasant, starving, trades sex for food, arrested as a witch, sentenced to burn. Adso never sees her again.

## 6. The historical bones (this is the worldview the kids need to see)

### The Avignon Papacy (1309–1377)
After a French pope (Clement V) refused to live in Rome, the Papacy moved to Avignon in southern France. By 1327 the Pope is **John XXII**, an Avignon pope famously rich, politically aggressive, and at open war with the Holy Roman Emperor **Louis IV of Bavaria**. The novel happens in the same month John formally declared Louis a heretic. The abbey in the novel is meant to be neutral ground for a peace conference between the papal delegation and the Franciscan order. *That conference never happens — the murders consume everything.*

### The Franciscan Poverty Debate
This is the one big idea the kids need. **Did Jesus and the apostles own anything?** The Franciscans (founded by St. Francis of Assisi, 1209) said no — they walked the earth with no money, no possessions, eating what was given. The "Spiritual Franciscans" wanted to live exactly that way. The Pope, sitting in a palace in Avignon, said: actually yes, Christ owned the things he used. To say otherwise is heresy. In **1322** (*Ad conditorem canonum*) and **1323** (*Cum inter nonnullos*) Pope John XXII issued formal decrees making the Franciscan poverty doctrine a heresy. The political subtext is obvious: a Church that says Christ was rich can comfortably be rich itself. *This is why people die in the novel — not really for Aristotle, but for what Aristotle represents in a Church that has lost confidence in itself.*

### The Three Orders (essential teaching for the kids)
- **Benedictines** (founded by St. Benedict, c. 530) — the original Western monks. Live in monasteries. Rule of "ora et labora" (pray and work). Wealthy by 1327: own land, copy and sell books, run schools. **The abbey in the novel is Benedictine.**
- **Franciscans** (founded by St. Francis of Assisi, 1209) — friars, not monks. Don't live in monasteries. Walk and preach. Vow of absolute poverty. **William and Ubertino are Franciscans.**
- **Dominicans** (founded by St. Dominic, 1216) — also friars, but founded specifically to preach against heresy. Highly educated. From the 1230s onward, they run the **Inquisition**. **Bernard Gui is a Dominican.**

### Heresies in the Background
- **Cathars / Albigensians** — crushed by crusade and Inquisition in southern France by the late 13th c. Bernard Gui hunted the last of them.
- **Waldensians** — preached lay poverty without Church permission.
- **Dolcinians** — followers of **Fra Dolcino** (c. 1250–1307). A radical apocalyptic offshoot of the Franciscan poverty movement. Believed the Pope and cardinals were corrupt, that God would soon punish them, that they themselves were the true apostles. Held property in common. They were militarily crushed in 1307, Dolcino captured, tortured, and burned. **Salvatore and Remigio in the novel are ex-Dolcinians hiding their pasts.** This subplot lets Eco teach the brutal lower-class side of the poverty debate.

### Manuscript culture
- A single book might take a year to copy. Vellum (calfskin), iron-gall ink, quill pens, miniatures, gold leaf.
- A library of 56 rooms with thousands of volumes is the largest in Christendom at this time.
- Roughly **150 years before Gutenberg's printing press (1455).** The fire that ends the novel destroys *the only copy* of Aristotle's book on comedy. That is what the loss of a single manuscript meant.

## 7. Themes — what Eco is doing under the murder mystery

1. **Knowledge is power, and dangerous knowledge is forbidden knowledge.** The library is not built to share. It is built to hoard and to filter. Knowledge in a monastery is a treasure *and* a trap.
2. **Laughter undermines authority.** Jorge's deepest fear is that if people laugh at sacred things, they will stop being afraid of them. He is *correct* — that is exactly what happens 200 years later in the Reformation and Renaissance. Jorge is the dogmatic mind defending itself against the future.
3. **The limits of reason.** William is a near-modern rationalist (Holmes + Ockham). He solves the case — but partly by accident. The pattern he thought he saw (seven trumpets of Revelation) was wrong. He says at the end: *"I behaved stubbornly, pursuing a semblance of order, when I should have known well that there is no order in the universe."* This is one of Eco's central points: rational pattern-finding works, but the world's order is not the order we project on it.
4. **The Inquisition.** Bernard Gui shows what happens when institutional fear gets a manual. The village girl is condemned because she is poor and a woman, not because she did anything. The book does not soften this.
5. **Loss.** The library burns. The book is gone. We hold *nomina nuda* — naked names. Knowledge is fragile. Civilization is one fire away from forgetting itself.
6. **The Sherlock-Watson dynamic.** Adso watches William; we watch through Adso. The reader learns alongside the novice. This is *exactly* the structure that works for kids: a smart adult and a kid who is awed and confused.

## 8. The 1986 film (Annaud) — what it did, what we should *not* copy

- Sean Connery as William, Christian Slater as Adso, F. Murray Abraham as Bernard Gui, Ron Perlman as Salvatore.
- Visually magnificent abbey (filmed at Eberbach, Kloster, and a custom-built exterior at Rocca Calascio).
- Strips out almost all theology. Keeps the murder mystery and the laughter argument.
- Cuts most of the named monks (no Benno, no Alinardo, no Aymaro).
- **Slightly happier ending:** the village girl escapes the fire and survives. (In the book she is burned.)
- Abbot's death is dropped.
- Treats Adso's encounter with the girl as a tender first-love scene rather than the strange, half-coerced thing the book describes.

**Our adaptation should be closer to Eco than to Annaud** for Andres's stated goal. The film is a thriller. Eco is a worldview lesson. Keep the worldview.

## 9. Adaptation decisions to flag with the user

### A. Volume count
The book is dense. Three viable shapes:
- **1 volume (~30 pages)** — film-tight. Loses depth, loses theology, becomes a murder mystery in robes. *Not recommended given the stated goal.*
- **2 volumes (~25 pp each)** — Recommended. Vol 1 = Days 1–4 (arrival, world-building, scriptorium and library tours, first murders, Salvatore's Dolcinian backstory, Adso meets the village girl, Bernard Gui arrives — cliffhanger). Vol 2 = Days 5–7 (the disputation, the interrogations, Severinus murdered, Malachi falls, the dream and the breakthrough, the *finis Africae*, the fire, the epilogue). Each volume can carry one or two real "teaching pages" (how a book was made; the three orders; the Avignon papacy).
- **3 volumes (~20 pp each)** — would allow each Day to breathe and teach. Likely overkill unless the kids ask for more after Vol 1.

### B. Faithful to Eco or to Annaud
Recommend faithful to Eco. Restore: the theological disputation (1 page, well-illustrated), Adso's confusion about heresy, the Avignon backstory, Adso's grief over the burned girl, the abbot's death, William's "no order in the universe" closing speech.

### C. The Adso / village girl subplot
This is the only place where the book content needs reframing for 9 and 7 year olds. Eco's version: a starving girl trades sex to the cellarer for food; she and Adso have a one-night encounter in the kitchen; she is later condemned as a witch and burned; Adso never sees her face again.

Two reframing options that preserve the *meaning* without the sex scene:
1. **The encounter is meeting + sharing food.** Adso finds her stealing in the kitchen, sees she is starving, gives her bread. They speak (she has no Latin, only her dialect; he has no Italian; they communicate barely). She runs. He is haunted. She is later arrested by Gui as a witch for the same trade she was making with Remigio. He grieves. William explains to him why she dies: not for what she did but for who she is — poor, female, illiterate, useful to no one with power. *This preserves the emotional and moral weight. Cuts the sex.*
2. **Closer to Annaud's softening** — implied romance, but kept off-page; same arrest and condemnation; same grief. Slightly more conventional.

My recommendation: **Option 1.** It's a stronger lesson and more honest about what the village girl represents in Eco's argument (the Church's victims are not the heretics it claims to fear; they are the poor it does not see).

### D. Visual register
- **Default:** continue the project's locked oil-painting realism (gpt-image-2 standard, 3:2 landscape, T1–T5 density tiers per `bio.md`). Matches Newton / da Vinci / Honda register.
- **Possible variant:** for chapter / canonical-hour breaks, render in the style of a 14th-c. illuminated manuscript margin — gold-leaf border, Gothic miniature in the initial. This is *only* for chapter breaks and would teach the visual vocabulary of the era. The main page art stays oil-painting realism. *Flag for user — this is the kind of stylistic choice the skill says to surface explicitly.*

### E. Ending tone
Eco's ending is bleak. The book burns. William says there is no order. Adso lives, but the world he lived in is gone. *Do not soften it.* The kids will feel something real, and that is the point. The epilogue should be the old Adso scene from the book — kid sees: *what we did here mattered, and most of it was lost; that is what writing it down is for.*

## 10. The line for the kids (in our voice, not Eco's)

The thing Andres wants them to see: **people in 1327 were not stupid.** They were trained in dialectic, logic, three languages, the entire corpus of available philosophy. They built libraries that were the most sophisticated information systems in the world. *And they still lived in a world where one fire could erase Aristotle, where a starving girl could be burned for begging, where the question "did Jesus own his sandals?" was a capital matter.* What we have now — books on every phone, the ability to look up anything — is not a sign that we are smarter than them. It is a sign that a great many people, across a great many centuries, made and remade and protected and shared knowledge until it became *cheap.* The story is about the moment in history when knowledge was still expensive, and what it cost when it was lost.

This is the load-bearing sentence and should sit somewhere in Vol 1 (William's voice) and again in the Vol 2 closing.
