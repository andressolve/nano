The First Crisis Was Over

Foundation — Book One: The Plan

Hari Seldon proved with mathematics that the Galactic Empire had to fall — and that thirty thousand years of darkness could be cut to one thousand, if a colony of scientists was placed on a bare rock at exactly the right moment. The Empire exiled him for saying so, which was exactly what he wanted. Fifty years later his exiles learned the truth from a dead man's hologram: their Encyclopedia was bait, their whole world was a chess move, and every crisis to come would leave them one path — and only one. Salvor Hardin found the first path. One quiz stands between you and the end — it tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why did the Empire put Hari Seldon on trial when all he had done was mathematics?
Right — that is Chen's whole case. "You disturb the peace of the Emperor's realm." The mathematics wasn't the crime; the belief it would spread was. A prediction of collapse, believed widely enough, becomes a push toward collapse.
Not quite. Nobody accused Seldon of hiding weapons or faking numbers — the Commission's own advocate never disputed his math. The danger was the prediction itself: if forty billion people believed the Empire was dying, the panic could hasten the fall.
2. Why did Seldon tell Gaal the trial had gone "exactly as computed"?
Right — "Chen chose the world I needed." Seldon used psychohistory on his own trial. The Commission thought it was banishing a troublemaker; it was actually planting the seed of the next Empire exactly where the Plan needed it planted.
Not quite. The judges were no friends of his, and he never feared execution — he told Chen he would be dead within five years anyway. The trial itself was his move: he computed that Chen would choose exile, and Terminus was exactly the world the Plan required.
3. Why did the envoy's slip — "You still have nuclear power?" — matter so much to Hardin?
Right — one startled question told Hardin everything. Anacreon burned coal and oil inside imperial-looking warships. The kingdoms had the shells of power without the science, so tiny Terminus was both far ahead of them and in terrible danger.
Not quite. Anacreon had no plutonium to sell — that was the whole point — and Rodric served Anacreon, not the Emperor. The slip revealed the kingdoms had lost atomic science while keeping the Empire's ships and uniforms, making atomic Terminus a prize worth taking.
4. Why did Seldon's hologram call the Encyclopedia Foundation "a fraud — and always has been"?
Right — "I never cared whether one volume was ever published." The Encyclopedia was scaffolding. It got a hundred thousand of the right people to the right rock, kept them harmlessly busy for fifty years, and then fell away — leaving the real Foundation: the seed of the Second Empire.
Not quite. The work was real and honest — Hardin even tells Pirenne so. The fraud was the purpose, not the pages: the Encyclopedia was bait to place scientists on Terminus and keep them busy until the first crisis trapped them onto the one path Seldon had computed.
5. Why was Hardin's solution to the first crisis "obvious — obvious as all hell"?
Right — Terminus had no fleet and no rescuer, and that was fine. Its atomic power made it a prize all four kingdoms wanted, and none could let a rival have. Balance, not battle: the first crisis is solved by the neighbors' own jealousy.
Not quite. Terminus had no warships at all, and Hardin's logic analysis proved the Empire's treaty promised exactly nothing. The obvious move used the enemy's rivals: no kingdom could let another own atomic power, so the four would guard Terminus from each other.
