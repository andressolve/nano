The Third Crisis Was Over

Foundation — Book Three: The Merchants

Seventy-five years after Hardin, the religion of science had stopped growing — every border world had banned the missionaries on pain of death. So the Foundation sent a different kind of man. Hober Mallow walked into Korell's trap at the spaceport, kept a Visual Record running, and let ultraviolet light win his trial for him. He followed a stamped sunburst back to a dying Empire whose tech-men could run their machines but never mend them. And when Korell attacked with borrowed dreadnoughts, the new Mayor refused to fight at all: he simply stopped the trade, and let Korell's own dead stoves, dark necklaces and silent factories do the fighting for him. Not one shot was fired. One quiz stands between you and the end — it tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why did the religion of science stop working as the Foundation's tool for growth?
Right — and it was exactly what Seldon's hologram had warned. The religion still defended the Foundation perfectly; but as a tool of growth it had made itself famous. Rulers like the Commdor and the Elders of Askone didn't need to disbelieve it — they only needed to keep it out. A weapon everyone has seen coming stops arriving.
Not quite. The faith held firm inside the Four Kingdoms — no secret got out, and priests were plentiful. The problem was reputation: every ruler on the rim had watched Anacreon become a puppet of Terminus, so they banned the missionaries at the border, on pain of death. The religion could defend; it could no longer spread.
2. Why did Mallow hand the "missionary" back to Korell — with his own crew begging him not to?
Right — Mallow read the geography. Nobody stumbles by accident onto an isolated spaceport with a mob conveniently minutes behind. Sheltering a "priest" meant handing Korell a legal excuse for war and Sutt a dead rival. So Mallow refused the bait — and the Visual Record he kept running turned the trap inside out at his trial, under ultraviolet light.
Not quite. Mallow feared no mob, and no law forced his hand — his crew certainly saw none. What he saw was that the scene could not be real: an empty port far from any city, yet the hunted man and the mob both arrived within minutes. Staged bait. A rescue meant war; so he refused it — and kept a Visual Record running for the day he'd need to prove why.
3. What did Mallow learn inside the Siwenna power plant, and why did it matter?
Right — the plant was the Empire in miniature: colossal, still running, and dead inside. Custodians tended machines they could not mend, in a science that had stopped asking questions generations ago. That told Mallow the future: the Empire could still hand out dreadnoughts, but it could never out-build, out-shrink or out-sell the Foundation. Time itself was on Terminus's side.
Not quite. The plant powered Siwenna, guarded jealously by its tech-men — no plot, no agents, no abandonment. What Mallow found was worse for the Empire than any plot: custodians who could run their generators but not repair them. Imperial science had fossilized into ritual. In a long contest of machines, the side that can still invent must win.
4. Why did Mallow refuse to send a fleet against Korell — even when Terminus called it surrender?
Right — "Seldon crises are not solved by individuals but by historic forces." Mallow had armed his weapon years earlier, gift by gift and gadget by gadget: every Korellian kitchen, factory and palace necklace ran on Foundation atomics that only Foundation traders could keep alive. The embargo turned all of it off. Three cold years later, Korell surrendered to nothing.
Not quite. There was no secret deal and no waiting game — Mallow never intended to fight at all. His reason was Seldon's: crises are won by broad forces, not brilliant heroics. Korell had filled itself with Foundation machines, and machines need spare parts. Stop the trade, and Korell's own dead stoves and silent factories would do the fighting — and they did.
5. By the end of the book, why is trade a stronger weapon than religion?
Right — a missionary can be banned; a bargain bans itself nowhere. The Commdor executed priests and still begged for atomic gadgets, because greed walks through doors that faith cannot. And every sale created dependence — machines that only the Foundation could refuel and repair. Korell learned what that dependence meant. The lamp had become a web; the Empire had finally noticed.
Not quite. Mallow built no war fleet and smuggled nothing — his whole victory was refusing to fight. Trade wins because it needs no belief: religion can be stopped at the border, but self-interest opens every door — even the Commdor who executed priests begged for gadgets. And each gadget quietly made its buyer depend on Terminus, until dependence itself became the weapon.
