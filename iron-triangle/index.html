The Race Goes On

The Iron Triangle — How a Tank Does Three Impossible Things at Once

Hit hard, survive being hit, move fast — three jobs that pull against each other, held in balance inside one machine. You saw how the gun, the armor, and the tracks each pull at a different corner of the triangle, how every sharper shell breeds a cleverer shield, and how a hundred-year-old idea now faces a new hunter: the cheap drone diving on its roof. The tank is not finished — it is still learning to defend itself. One quiz stands between you and the end — it tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why is a tank always a compromise, never perfect at all three jobs at once?
Right — this is the iron triangle. A bigger gun and thicker armor both add weight, and weight steals speed. Push one corner and the others give way. There is no perfect tank, only the balance that fits the job.
Not quite. It is not about crew room, price, or the engine. Firepower and protection both add weight, and weight costs mobility — so improving one corner of the triangle pulls down another. Every tank is that balance.
2. Why do designers SLOPE a tank's armor instead of just bolting on a thicker plate?
Exactly. A shot crossing a tilted plate has to travel through more steel to get out the other side, and a glancing hit can ricochet off. You get more protection for the same weight — a free gift to the mobility corner.
Not quite. Sloping is not about rust, the view, or storage. A tilted plate forces a shot through more steel and can deflect it — more protection without adding the weight a thicker plate would cost.
3. Why does the gun rely on a computer that re-aims more than a hundred times a second?
Right. The shell takes time to fly, and everything keeps moving in that time. The computer measures the exact range, predicts where the target will be, and shifts the aim — over and over — so the tank can fire on the move and still hit.
Not quite. It is about hitting a moving target while you yourself are moving. The computer measures range and leads the shot, redoing the math a hundred-plus times a second so the aim stays true even as the tank pitches.
4. Why does a 60-tonne tank ride on wide tracks instead of huge wheels?
Exactly. Spreading sixty tonnes along two long tracks keeps the pressure on any patch of ground surprisingly gentle — gentler than you would guess. That is why the tank crosses mud, sand, and broken ground where a wheeled truck sinks.
Not quite. It is about ground pressure. Tracks spread the enormous weight over a long contact surface, so the tank presses lightly on each patch of earth and can cross soft mud that would trap a wheeled vehicle.
5. Why can a drone costing a few hundred dollars threaten a tank worth millions?
Right. A tank's heaviest armor faces forward; its roof is thin. A 400-dollar drone can dive straight onto that weak spot and destroy a machine worth millions — the same cost trap from our last book, now aimed at the giant. The answer is layers: radar, jammers, and guns that guard the roof.
Not quite. The gun can elevate, the drone's warhead is small, and modern tanks do sense drones. The real reason is cost and the weak spot: a cheap drone dives onto the thin roof armor, flipping the economics of the whole fight.
