# Leonardo da Vinci — Source Dossier (Book One: 1452–1499)

Verified from Wikipedia and Britannica searches, May 2026. Cross-checked across multiple independent results. Quotes verbatim. **Do not paraphrase quotes; do not invent dates.**

## 1. Birth and parentage (1452)

- **Born:** 15 April 1452, "on a Saturday, at the third hour of the night" — per his paternal grandfather Antonio's diary entry.
- **Place:** Vinci or near Vinci (Tuscan hill town, ~20 miles west of Florence). Traditional account places the birth at the hamlet of Anchiano, which would have offered privacy for an illegitimate birth.
- **Full name:** Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci ("Leonardo, son of ser Piero, of Vinci").
- **Father:** Ser Piero da Vinci (1426–1504), Florentine notary. "Ser" = honorific for a notary.
- **Mother:** Caterina di Meo Lippi (c. 1434–1494), of the lower class (peasant).
- **Illegitimate.** Both parents married other people the year after his birth. Leonardo was raised primarily in his paternal grandfather's household in Vinci. Because of his illegitimacy, he was barred from his father's profession (notary) and from the university route.

## 2. The kite memory (Codex Atlanticus, written later as adult)

Leonardo's earliest recorded memory, in his own hand, on a page of notebook studies of birds:

> "Writing like this so particularly about the kite seems to be my destiny, since the first memory of my childhood is that it seemed to me, when I was in my cradle, that a kite came to me, and opened my mouth with its tail, and struck me several times with its tail inside my lips."

- The bird is a **kite** (a hawk-like raptor common in Tuscany), not a vulture. The "vulture" reading is a known German mistranslation Freud built his 1910 monograph on.
- This is render-as-artifact material for Page 1.

## 3. Florence and Verrocchio (c. 1466 → 1481)

- Sometime around 1466 (age ~14), Ser Piero brought Leonardo to Florence and apprenticed him to **Andrea del Verrocchio**, one of the leading workshops of the city. Verrocchio was sculptor, goldsmith, painter — a polymath workshop master, exactly the right environment.
- Leonardo qualified as a **master in the Guild of Saint Luke** (the painters' guild) in **1472** — but he stayed in Verrocchio's workshop for several more years, working alongside Botticelli, Ghirlandaio, Perugino, and Lorenzo di Credi.
- **The Baptism of Christ** (Verrocchio's workshop, finished c. 1475): Leonardo was assigned to paint an angel in the lower-left. He also painted much of the background landscape (in oil; the rest of the panel is tempera).
- **Vasari's claim** (Lives, 1550): "Lionardo's Angel" was so much better than Andrea's figures that "Andrea never wanted to touch colors again, disdainful that a boy knew more about them than he did." Vasari did not know Leonardo personally; the anecdote may be embellished, but Verrocchio's painting output did effectively cease after this.

## 4. The Adoration of the Magi (1481, abandoned 1482)

- Commissioned by the **Augustinian monks of San Donato in Scopeto** (a Florentine monastery) in 1481.
- Leonardo prepared a detailed underdrawing in lamp black, then a thin white-lead priming to guide the paint. The composition was extraordinarily ambitious — dozens of figures, swirling movement, architectural ruins.
- Leonardo **left for Milan in 1482** without finishing it. Only the underdrawing and partial monochrome modeling exist on the panel.
- The Augustinians waited; eventually they commissioned a different Adoration from **Filippino Lippi** (completed 1496).
- Leonardo's panel is now in the Uffizi.

## 5. The letter to Ludovico Sforza (1482)

- Leonardo wrote a famous self-promoting letter to **Ludovico il Moro** ("Ludovico the Moor"), Duke of Milan, sometime after arriving in Milan c. 1482–83.
- Preserved in the **Codex Atlanticus** at the Biblioteca Ambrosiana, Milan.
- Structure: **ten numbered points**. The first nine are military engineering capabilities (portable bridges, methods to drain moats, methods to destroy fortresses, lightweight cannons, mortars, mining/sapping, naval war machines, covered armored chariots, catapults/mangonels/trabocchi). The **tenth and final point** is, almost as an afterthought: in times of peace he can do architecture, hydraulics, sculpture in marble/bronze/clay, and painting "as well as any other, be he who he may." He closes by noting he can also undertake the bronze horse for the duke's father.
- This is the famous "first résumé." Render-as-artifact material for the primary-source page.

## 6. Milan period — court of Ludovico Sforza (1482–1499)

- Leonardo worked in Milan from 1482 until 1499 — about seventeen years, his longest single residency.
- Roles: **military engineer, festival designer, painter, sculptor, architect, hydraulic engineer.** The court was small and Leonardo was its polymath.
- **Lady with an Ermine** (c. 1489–1491): portrait of **Cecilia Gallerani**, Sforza's teenage mistress (~16 at sitting). The ermine is a triple symbol: Sforza himself was nicknamed "the White Ermine" (Order of the Ermine, 1488); "Galé" puns on her surname (galé = ermine in Greek); and the ermine traditionally symbolizes purity. Considered the **first modern psychological portrait** in Western art.
- **The Virgin of the Rocks** (first version, c. 1483–86): commissioned for a Milanese chapel.

## 7. Notebooks and mirror writing

- Leonardo wrote in **mirror script** (right-to-left, letters reversed), readable only when held to a mirror.
- He was **left-handed**. The mirror writing is most likely a natural left-hander's habit (smudge-avoidance, ease of stroke direction); not, as legend sometimes claims, a code to hide his thoughts. He wrote letters and texts intended for others in conventional left-to-right script.
- The notebooks (Codex Atlanticus, Codex Leicester, Codex on the Flight of Birds, Madrid Codices, Windsor Royal Collection sheets, etc.) cover **anatomy, hydraulics, architecture, optics, geology, botany, mechanics, painting theory, fables, grocery lists**. ~7,000 surviving sheets out of an estimated original 13,000.

## 8. Apprentices and household

- **Salaì** (Gian Giacomo Caprotti da Oreno, 1480–1524): joined Leonardo's household on **St. Mary Magdalen's Day, 22 July 1490**, at age **10**. Stayed for ~25 years.
- Leonardo's note describes him as "a liar, a thief, stubborn, a glutton" — and the next day records that after Leonardo had two shirts, hose, and a jerkin cut for him, Salaì stole **4 lire** from Leonardo's purse.
- Nickname "Salaì" = "the little devil" / "as dangerous as Saladin."
- Salaì was the model for several Leonardo works (St. John the Baptist, the Bacchus, the Angelo Incarnato).

## 9. Vasari's description of Leonardo's appearance

- "Striking and handsome," "magnificent presence," "marvellously endowed by heaven with beauty, grace and talent in such abundance."
- "Leonardo's disposition was so lovable that he commanded everyone's affection."
- Tall for his time, athletic, graceful.
- Hair: at a time when most men wore hair cropped short and beards close, Leonardo wore his hair "down to the middle of his breast, in ringlets and well arranged" (older). His beard flowed over his chest.
- **Vegetarian** (rare for the period). Reputedly bought caged birds in Florentine markets to set them free.

## 10. The Sforza Horse / Gran Cavallo (commissioned 1482, model 1493, destroyed 1499)

- **Commissioned 1482** by Ludovico Sforza as a memorial equestrian monument to his father, Francesco Sforza. Intended to be the largest bronze equestrian statue ever cast — approaching **8 meters / 26 feet** at the shoulder.
- Leonardo spent years on the project: anatomical horse studies, casting calculations.
- **November 1493**: Leonardo unveiled a **24-foot full-scale clay model** in the courtyard of the Castello Sforzesco, on the occasion of the wedding of Ludovico's niece Bianca Maria Sforza to Holy Roman Emperor Maximilian I. The model was acclaimed across Italy.
- **70 tons (some sources: 80 tons) of bronze** had been gathered for the casting.
- **1494**: Ludovico requisitioned the bronze to make **cannons** for the war with France (the Battle of Fornovo, 1495, came out of these tensions).
- **1499**: After Louis XII's invasion, **French archers used the clay model for target practice**, reducing it to ruins.
- The horse was never cast in Leonardo's lifetime. (A bronze horse based on Leonardo's studies was finally cast and installed in Milan in 1999.)

## 11. The Last Supper (1495–1498)

- Painted on the **north wall of the refectory** of the Convent of **Santa Maria delle Grazie**, Milan.
- Painted **c. 1495 – completed 9 February 1498** (date documented by Luca Pacioli).
- Subject: the moment Christ announces "one of you will betray me," capturing the disciples' reactions in clusters of three.
- **Technique:** Leonardo rejected true fresco (which requires fast, irreversible work on wet plaster). Instead he used an **experimental dry technique**: tempera and oil paint applied to **two layers of dry preparatory ground** (a sealing layer plus a white lead priming) on the dry plaster wall. This let him work slowly, glaze, revise — but the paint never bonded chemically into the wall as fresco does.
- **The technique failed.** Within a few years the paint began to flake. The wall was a thin exterior, the convent had been hastily rebuilt under Sforza's order with moisture-retaining rubble fill, and humidity attacked the unbonded paint. Ruined further by 18th-century repaintings, the 1796 French occupation (the refectory was used as a stable), and a 1943 bomb.

## 12. The fall of Milan (1499)

- **August–October 1499**: **King Louis XII of France**, claiming descent from Milan's first duke (his grandmother was a Visconti), invaded Italy. He was supported by Venice and by Milanese resentment of Ludovico's heavy taxation. The duchy fell quickly.
- **Ludovico fled** in October 1499. He attempted a comeback in February 1500, was captured at Novara in April 1500, and died in French captivity in 1508.
- **Leonardo left Milan in December 1499**, traveling with the mathematician **Luca Pacioli** (his close friend; author of *Summa de Arithmetica*; with whom Leonardo collaborated on *De Divina Proportione*, illustrated by Leonardo).
- Their route: Mantua (Leonardo drew the famous portrait of Isabella d'Este), then **Venice** in March 1500 (Leonardo worked briefly as a military engineer designing moveable barriers against an Ottoman threat), then **Florence** in spring 1500.
- This is the natural close of Book One. Book Two would pick up in Florence, leading to the second Florence period, the Mona Lisa, the move to France, and death at Cloux in 1519.

## 13. Anatomy and water studies (Milan period, before 1499)

- Anatomy: Leonardo's pre-1499 anatomical work concentrated on the **skull series (1489)** — sectioned views of the skull rendered with perspective lines, cataloged in the Windsor sheets. Also extensive **horse anatomy** for the Sforza Horse.
- Hydraulics: Leonardo studied **vortices, weirs, river flow, canal engineering** throughout the Milan period, advising the duchy on the Naviglio Grande and similar canal works. Major notebook output on water as a fluid system, often with text written in mirror beside the diagrams.

## 16. Things NOT to render in this volume

- The Mona Lisa — that's after 1500, Book Two.
- Vitruvian Man — drawn c. 1490, but its iconic isolation is a Book Two-era resonance; usable in Book One only as a notebook-page motif.
- Leonardo with white flowing beard — he was 47 in 1499. The beard-down-to-his-chest portrait is the older Leonardo. In Book One peak (Milan), depict him as 30s–40s: dark hair tied back, neat short beard, well-dressed.
- The "homosexuality charges" of 1476 — historically attested (anonymous accusation, dismissed) but not central to the human spine of Book One. Skip.
