**Image text:**

Mid-right caption box:

## PAGE 9 — Lady with an Ermine

**Narrative job:** The portrait. What made Leonardo modern.

**Image:** Leonardo's Milan studio, c. 1490. Leonardo (~38, mid-30s lock) at the easel, brush in hand. On the easel: the *Lady with an Ermine* taking shape — Cecilia Gallerani in three-quarter view, blue-black hair smoothed under a fine net, a large white ermine cradled in her arm. Cecilia, 16, sits across from him on a low chair, holding a real white ermine that fidgets in her arm. North window light from the left. The painting on the easel and the woman in the room are visibly the same composition, but the painting catches something the still-life pose cannot — the moment of the head turning, attention shifting toward something off-frame.

**Image text:**

Top caption band:
"His first masterwork in Milan was a portrait. The sitter was Cecilia Gallerani, sixteen years old, the duke's young mistress, gifted in poetry and Latin. The painting would later be called *Lady with an Ermine*."

Mid-right caption box (under or beside the easel):
"Until then, Italian portraits showed people in formal profile, like coins. Leonardo turned Cecilia in three-quarter view and caught her in the middle of an instant — her head just beginning to move toward something happening outside the frame. The viewer can almost hear it."

Bottom caption band:
"The white ermine in her arm was not a pet. The duke's order of knighthood had given him the title \"the White Ermine.\" Cecilia's surname punned on the Greek word for it. The painting was a code, a courtly compliment, and a portrait — and it was also the first time in Western art a face on a panel looked, unmistakably, like it was thinking."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~155 words). **Lettering placement:** top band, mid-right box, bottom band.

## PAGE 10 — The Notebooks (Annotated page)

**Narrative job:** Notebooks as method. Mirror writing. Observation as discipline.

**Image:** Central anchor image: an open notebook, palm-sized, on a wooden table, filled edge-to-edge with Leonardo's hand — mirror-script in iron-gall ink (right-to-left, letters reversed) and precise small sketches: a wing in three flight phases, a water vortex around a stone, a hinge mechanism, the muscles of an arm. The notebook fills most of the frame. Around it, four small in-image callout boxes anchored by thin painted lines to features on the page.

**Image text:**

Top caption box (centered, off-white parchment):
"Leonardo wrote everything down. Over his life he filled an estimated thirteen thousand notebook pages. About seven thousand survive."

Callout 1 (anchored to mirror-script lines):
"He wrote right-to-left, in mirror script. He was left-handed. The reversed writing kept his hand from smudging the wet ink. It was not, as legend says, a code."

Callout 2 (anchored to bird-wing sketch):
"On bird pages: the geometry of how a wing pushes air down so that the bird, by Newton's law not yet written, is pushed up."

Callout 3 (anchored to water vortex):
"On water pages: the patterns a stream makes around a stone. He drew water as if it were also a body, with muscles and joints."

Callout 4 (anchored to anatomy sketch):
"On the body: tendons drawn the way a sculptor draws ropes — because that, he said, was what they were."

Bottom caption band:
"The notebooks were not for posterity. They were a working tool — a way of slowing his eye down to the speed at which a thing actually happens. The world, he believed, would tell you what it was if you were willing to draw it carefully enough to be wrong about it first."

**Density:** T5+ (top caption + 4 callouts + bottom = 6 elements, ~165 words). **Lettering placement:** central image clear of overlap; small callout boxes around the edges of the notebook page; thin painted leader-lines connecting each callout to its feature.

## PAGE 12 — Wings

**Narrative job:** The flying machines. Pull the kite memory back. Show the method.

**Image:** Leonardo's Milan studio, c. 1493. The walls and the long oak table are covered in bird-wing studies — wings of kites, swallows, falcons, drawn in the three phases of a stroke. On the table: a small wooden model of a flapping wing, its frame of thin wooden ribs covered with stretched waxed linen, articulated by small leather straps. Through the open window: a real Tuscan kite circles outside, distant. Leonardo (mid-40s lock — start showing him at 45) holds the wooden wing model up, pulling a strap to flex it. He is alone, evening, candlelight warming the cream linen wing. A notebook open beside the model with mirror-script.

**Image text:**

Top caption band:
"Leonardo had not forgotten the kite. He drew birds for thirty years, slow and precise, in three flight phases: the down-stroke, the recovery, the glide."

Mid-right caption box:
"He believed a man could fly. Not by magic, not by alchemy, but by understanding the geometry of a wing's pressure on the air — and building a machine that did the same thing, in proportion, with human muscles."

Bottom caption band:
"He never built one that flew. The materials of his time were wrong: linen and ash wood are too heavy for the muscle a man can supply. But the studies he made — the notebooks of wings — are the first serious attempt in Europe to ask what a bird is doing when it stays up. Four hundred years later, in 1903, two American bicycle mechanics named Wright would solve the same problem with engines. The question Leonardo had asked was, by then, no longer his."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~155 words). **Lettering placement:** top band, mid-right box, bottom band.

## PAGE 14 — The Wall

**Narrative job:** The Last Supper begins, 1495. Establish the refectory and the experimental method.

**Image:** The refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan, c. 1495. A long high white-plastered hall, vaulted ceiling, cool clerestory north light from above. Two long oak refectory tables. The north wall — broad, white-prepared — has a wooden scaffold platform built against it. Leonardo (45 lock) stands on the scaffold, charcoal in hand, sketching the first ghost-lines of a long horizontal composition: a long table with thirteen figures behind it. Below the scaffold: jars of pigment, oil flasks, a smaller assistant grinding pigment. The opposite wall already holds a finished Crucifixion fresco by another painter. Two Dominican friars in white-and-black robes glance up from the doorway and continue past.

**Image text:**

Top caption band:
"In 1495, the Dominican friars of Santa Maria delle Grazie gave Leonardo their refectory wall. They wanted a Last Supper — Christ and the twelve, painted at the head of the room where the friars ate."

Mid-right caption box:
"The standard method for painting on a wall in 1495 was true fresco: pigment mixed with water, brushed onto fresh wet plaster, absorbed chemically into the wall as it dried. Once dry, the painting was the wall."

Bottom caption band:
"Fresco was permanent. It was also fast and unforgiving — a painter had to finish each section before the plaster dried, in a single morning. Leonardo did not paint fast. He glazed. He revised. He sometimes spent hours studying the curve of a single hand. He did not want to commit to a wet plaster he could not change. So he tried something else."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~160 words). **Lettering placement:** top band, mid-right box, bottom band.

## PAGE 15 — The Method That Should Not Have Worked

**Narrative job:** Annotated breakthrough page. Leonardo's experimental technique. Show clearly why it would fail.

**Image:** Close detail on the Last Supper wall under construction, c. 1496. The wall surface, in cross-section view (a slight cutaway visible at the lower edge), shows: dry plaster, then a sealing layer of pitch-and-mastic, then a thin white-lead priming, then pigment in tempera-and-oil. Above the cutaway: a section of the painting in progress — Christ at the center beneath the triple window, the apostles in clusters of three, the moment of "one of you will betray me" caught in their hands and faces. Leonardo on a low platform applies a thin layer with a soft sable brush. Four small annotation callouts anchored to features.

**Image text:**

Top caption band:
"Leonardo invented his own technique. Instead of painting on wet plaster, he let the wall dry, sealed it with pitch and mastic, primed it with white lead, and painted on it in oil and tempera — like an oil painting on a panel, only the panel was the wall."

Callout 1 (anchored to the dry plaster cross-section):
"Dry plaster. The wall was finished and dry before any paint touched it. This let him work slowly."

Callout 2 (anchored to the sealing layer):
"Sealing layer. Pitch and mastic, meant to keep the dry plaster from drinking the paint. It did not."

Callout 3 (anchored to the white priming):
"White-lead priming. A bright base for transparent oil glazes. The paint sat on this — it did not bond into the wall."

Callout 4 (anchored to a glazed apostle):
"Oil glazes. Layer over layer, slow, revisable, luminous. Beautiful. And not chemically connected to the wall behind them."

Bottom caption band:
"It let him paint the way he wanted to paint. It was also, though no one yet knew it, a way of putting an oil painting on a wall and asking the wall to keep it forever."

**Density:** T5+ (top caption + 4 callouts + bottom = 6 elements, ~180 words). **Lettering placement:** top band, callouts arrayed around the cross-section, bottom band.

## PAGE 16 — The Moment

**Narrative job:** The Last Supper completed, 9 February 1498. Show what makes the painting itself.

**Image:** The completed Last Supper on the refectory wall, viewed at a slight angle from the floor below. Christ at the center, hands open, beneath the triple window onto a Lombard landscape. Twelve apostles in four clusters of three, each in a different motion of reaction — Peter leaning sharply forward, John inclined sideways, Thomas raising one finger, Judas with his back half-turned and a small purse clenched in his right hand, his left hand reaching for the bread at the same moment as Christ's. The room is empty of friars; it is evening, candlelight low. Leonardo stands at the foot of the scaffold, looking up. He has just stepped back. The painting is finished.

**Image text:**

Top caption band:
"On 9 February 1498, Leonardo finished. The painting showed the moment in the Gospel when Christ tells the twelve, \"One of you will betray me\" — and caught each of them in the half-second after."

Mid-right caption box:
