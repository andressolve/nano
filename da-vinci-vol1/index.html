End of Book One

The Boy Who Watched Birds

In December 1499 Leonardo rode south out of Milan with two leather saddlebags of notebooks, the mathematician Luca Pacioli, and the boy he called Salaì. He left behind a flaking wall, a clay horse shot full of arrows, and ten years of work in a duchy that no longer existed. The next part of his story would put a particular smile onto a small panel in Florence, and would carry him eventually to a small house in France — and to the king of that country sitting beside his bed at the end.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why was Leonardo barred from his father's profession of notary?
Yes. Leonardo was the son of ser Piero, a Florentine notary, and a peasant woman named Caterina. They were never married. The notary's guild — like most of the careful guild careers of Florence — was closed to children born outside marriage. The accident of his birth pushed him out of books and into the workshop, where he would learn to read the world directly.
Not quite. Leonardo's parents were never married. By the rules of the guild city, an illegitimate child could not become a notary. He was barred not by talent or interest or any failing of his own — but by the accident of his birth, and so he was sent into a painter's workshop instead.
2. What was unusual about Leonardo's angel in Verrocchio's Baptism of Christ?
Right. Leonardo was given the small angel at the lower left. He painted it in oil glazes, with a face that seemed to be thinking and feeling at the same moment, on a panel where the rest of the figures were in stiff tempera. Vasari later wrote that when Verrocchio saw the angel, he gave up coloring forever — the apprentice had passed the master.
Not quite. Leonardo's angel was painted in oil glazes — soft, layered, almost alive — on a panel where the other figures were tempera. The face seemed to be feeling something. Vasari said it was the moment Verrocchio gave up coloring, because the apprentice had outpainted him.
3. In Leonardo's 1482 letter to Ludovico Sforza, what came first — and what came last?
Exactly. Leonardo wrote to a duke at war. He led with what a duke at war would buy: portable bridges, siege engines, mortars, covered carts that could not be broken. Only at the tenth point, almost as an afterthought, did he offer painting and sculpture — including the bronze horse for the duke's father. He had read the room.
Not quite. Leonardo's letter to Ludovico Sforza put nine military-engineering offers first — bridges, siege engines, cannons, war machines — because the duke was at war. Painting and sculpture came tenth, almost as an afterthought, alongside the offer of the bronze horse.
4. Why did Leonardo's Last Supper begin to flake within years of being finished?
Yes. Leonardo wanted to paint slowly, glazing and revising as he would on a wooden panel. True fresco demanded speed and finality — pigment had to be brushed into wet plaster in a single morning. So he invented his own method: dry plaster, sealed with pitch and mastic, primed with white lead, painted in oil and tempera. The paint sat on the wall instead of bonding into it. Within five years it began to come away.
Not quite. Leonardo painted the Last Supper in oil and tempera on dry plaster, sealed with pitch and mastic and primed with white lead — not in true fresco. The paint never bonded chemically with the wall behind it. Within a few years the first flakes began to fall to the refectory floor.
5. What happened to the great clay model of the Sforza Horse in 1499?
Right. Before the French arrived, Ludovico had already requisitioned the seventy tons of bronze gathered for the casting and melted it into cannons. The cannons did not save Milan. When French soldiers entered the courtyard in December 1499, they used the unfired clay model — ten years of Leonardo's work, twenty-four feet tall — for archery practice. The horse Leonardo had drawn for a decade was never cast in his lifetime.
Not quite. In December 1499, French soldiers used the clay model of the Sforza Horse for archery practice. The seventy tons of bronze meant to cast it had already been melted into cannons by Ludovico before the French arrived — and the cannons had not saved Milan. The horse Leonardo had drawn for ten years was never cast in his lifetime.
