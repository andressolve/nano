The Duel Never Ends

Sky Duel — How Fighter Jet Weapons Actually Work

A stubborn engineer in the desert believed a missile could follow heat. Flares answered the missile. Thermal cameras answered the flares. Radar answered the eye, and stealth answered the radar. Somewhere right now, an engineer is sketching the answer to stealth. One quiz stands between you and the end of the book — it tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why does the Vulcan cannon have six spinning barrels instead of one?
Right. A hundred shells a second through one barrel would heat it until it glowed and wore away. Splitting the work six ways is the same trick as passing a hot plate around the table — nobody's hands get burned. It's a heat problem, not an aiming problem.
Not quite. The six barrels are about HEAT. Firing 100 shells every second through one barrel would make it glow and wear out in seconds. Each barrel fires only once per spin, so six barrels share the work — and the gun lasts more than 10,000 rounds without jamming.
2. Why did pilots flying against early Sidewinders start carrying flares?
Exactly. The early seeker was simple: follow the brightest infrared glow in view. A burning magnesium flare out-glows a jet engine — so the missile took the bait and chased a falling spark while the jet escaped. Every weapon teaches the enemy how to fight it.
Not quite. Flares work because of how the early seeker THINKS: it chases the brightest heat it can see. Magnesium flares burn hotter than the engine itself, so the missile swerves after a falling spark while the real jet escapes. The flare exploits the seeker's simplicity.
3. Why doesn't a modern Sidewinder fall for flares the way old ones did?
Right. The modern seeker doesn't see a bright dot — it sees a PICTURE, like a thermal camera. A jet has wings, tails, and a glowing body; a flare is a shapeless falling point of light. The missile compares shapes and stays locked on the one shaped like a jet.
Not quite. The answer is the picture. Today's seeker is a tiny thermal camera that sees the target's SHAPE. A flare is just a shapeless falling spark — wrong shape, wrong motion — so the seeker checks it, rejects it, and stays on the jet-shaped glow.
4. The missile never calculates where the jet is going. Why does it still catch it?
Yes — this is the most beautiful idea in the book. Sailors knew it for centuries: if another ship stays at the same angle off your bow while getting closer, you WILL collide. The missile runs that rule in reverse — it steers to hold the angle constant, which forces a collision course without ever predicting anything. Proportional navigation.
Not quite. The trick is the sailors' rule: constant angle plus closing distance equals collision. The missile simply steers to keep the target at the same angle — and geometry does the rest, locking both machines onto a collision course. No prediction, no joystick, no radio.
5. Why do stealth jets carry their missiles INSIDE the body?
Exactly. Stealth is shape: every surface angled so radar waves bounce away instead of echoing home. Hang one missile on a wingtip and you've bolted a radar mirror onto a jet that reflects less than a glass marble. So the weapons hide inside — doors snap open, the missile drops, doors snap shut.
Not quite. It's about radar. A stealth jet's whole shape is designed to bounce radar waves AWAY — on a screen it reflects about as much as a glass marble. One missile hanging outside would shine like a flashlight and undo all of it. So the weapons ride inside, behind closed doors.
