LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Europe celebrates. With the same text in Greek and in hieroglyphs,

- Caption (p2): In 1801 the British defeat the French in Egypt and take the stone as a

- Caption (p3): But the real race runs on paper. Printed copies — lithographs — travel

- CLUE box (p4): CLUE: The stone is broken. Most of the hieroglyphic section snapped off

  is missing.
- Caption, bottom, gold-edged: Weeks pass. Then years. Then twenty. The case that was
  supposed to close in a month stays open for twenty-three years. Why?

## P4 — THE FALSE TRAIL

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: none locked (period scholars).
Beats: (1) A 17th-century Jesuit (Kircher, unnamed in image) in a dark baroque study,
gesturing grandly over a hieroglyph engraving, mystical diagrams behind him. (2) Two
scholars (de Sacy, Åkerblad) by lamplight over the demotic band, a few name-groups
circled, then pushing the papers away. (3) WIDE: the evidence table — the three bands as
exhibits — with a heavy word stamped diagonally across the hieroglyph exhibit: SYMBOLS.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): Why so long? Because of an idea. For centuries, Europe's wisest men
  taught that hieroglyphs were not writing at all — each sign was a pure symbol, a
  picture-mystery holding a thought. In the 1650s the scholar Athanasius Kircher
  published whole "translations" this way. Nearly every word was invented.
- Caption (p2): Around 1802, two brilliant readers — Silvestre de Sacy in Paris and
  Johan Åkerblad in Sweden — attack the middle script, the demotic. They pick out a few
  royal names… then stall, and give up.
- Caption (p3, wide): The hieroglyphs they barely touch. Everyone still believes the
  sacred signs stand for ideas, not sounds. You cannot sound out a picture.
- Case card, bottom right, gold-edged: A detective's worst enemy is not a missing clue.
  It is a wrong idea, held so long it feels like a fact.

Density: T4.

## P6 — THE RIVAL DETECTIVE

Layout: THREE panels — two top, one WIDE bottom. Refs: ref_cast_plate, ref_young,
ref_stone.
Beats: (1) Young's bright orderly London study; he studies a prism at the window —
precision incarnate. (2) Young at his desk, the stone's lithograph flat before him,

with the oval name-rings highlighted gold.

LETTERING:
- Caption (p1): London, 1814. Enter the rival: Doctor Thomas Young — physician, and one
  of the cleverest men in Europe. He has already proved that light travels as waves.
  Now he turns to the stone.
- Caption (p2): Young works like a machine. He shows the three scripts are one family —
  the everyday demotic grew out of the sacred hieroglyphs. And he counts: there are far
  too few different signs for each sign to mean a whole word.
- Caption (p3): One thing more. Some names in the hieroglyphs sit inside carved oval
  rings — each ring is called a cartouche. Young reasons: the Greek text keeps naming
  King Ptolemy… so the ovals should hold the royal name.
- Bubble (Young, p3): If the king's name is foreign — Greek, not Egyptian — perhaps they
  spelled it out… by sound.
- CLUE box, bottom right: CLUE: A cartouche = a royal name in a ring. Find a name you
  already know, and the signs inside might become letters.

Density: T4/T5.

- Caption (p3): Champollion holds one more thread. From a Greek-and-demotic papyrus he

- Caption, small: A real detective does not just explain the evidence he has. He
  predicts the evidence he has not yet seen.

  SOUNDS. The case has cracked open.

## P16 — THE WHOLE ANSWER (system page)

Layout: case-file splash. Refs: plate_ramesses_thutmose, plate_clue_key, ref_champ_man.
Scene: a grand evidence board, gold-pinned: center, the RAMESSES cartouche decomposed —
each sign on its own card with its role labeled; around it, three lamplit zones titled
SOUND, IDEA, MARKER. Champollion inset lower right, older by two years, calm at last.

LETTERING:
- Caption, top band: 1824. Champollion publishes the full solution — the Précis — and it
  explains why EVERYONE had been partly right, and everyone wrong.
- His verbatim words, framed card: A script all at once figurative, symbolic and
  phonetic — in one and the same text, one and the same sentence… one and the same word.
- Zone card SOUND: Most signs spell SOUNDS — like letters. The lion is L. The folded
  cloth is S.
- Zone card IDEA: Some signs stand for the THING itself — the sun-disc can simply mean
  the sun-god RE.
- Zone card MARKER: And some signs are silent guides — set at a word's end to whisper
  which KIND of thing it is: a man, a city, a motion.
- Caption, bottom band: One writing, three kinds of sign, woven together in every line —
  that is why symbol-hunters and sound-hunters both kept half-failing for so long.
  And Champollion was not finished being corrected either: in 1837 the German scholar
  Lepsius showed many "letters" carry two or three sounds at once. Science ends no case;
  it reopens them better.

Density: T5.

## QUIZ (reader HTML, 5 questions, WHY-form, answers shuffled: c / a / d / b / c)

**Q1. Everyone expected the Rosetta Stone to solve the mystery in weeks. Why couldn't
the stone do it alone?**
a) The Greek section turned out to be a completely different text from the other two.
b) The stone was kept locked away in the British Museum where no scholar could study it.
c) Most of its hieroglyphic section had broken off — and scholars read the surviving
signs with a wrong idea, as symbols instead of sounds. ✔
d) The demotic script in the middle had never been seen anywhere else in Egypt.
Feedback ✔: Both halves matter — the best evidence was physically missing, AND the
false trail (signs = pure symbols) made even the surviving lines unreadable. A key is
useless while you hold it upside down.

**Q2. Why was Cleopatra's cartouche the decisive test of the sound-spelling idea?**
a) Because her name shares sounds with Ptolemy's — so the same signs HAD to appear in
both cartouches, exactly where the shared sounds fall. Champollion predicted it before
looking. ✔
b) Because Cleopatra was the most famous ruler Egypt ever had, so her name appeared on
the most monuments.
c) Because her cartouche was the only one on the Rosetta Stone that survived undamaged.
d) Because her name was written in Greek letters inside the cartouche itself.
Feedback ✔: A theory earns trust when it predicts evidence you haven't seen yet.
P, O, L landed exactly in place — coincidence was no longer possible. (And remember:
Cleopatra's name isn't on the stone at all — it came from the Philae obelisk.)

**Q3. Champollion could already read Ptolemy, Alexander, Caesar… Why did the RAMESSES
cartouche still matter so much more?**
a) Because Ramesses built more temples than all the Greek rulers combined.
b) Because his cartouche was the first ever found containing the sun-disc sign.
c) Because it proved hieroglyphs could also be read in Coptic.
d) Because every name read before him was a FOREIGN ruler's — Ramesses was a true

Feedback ✔: That was the exact hole in the case — Young's "foreign names only" theory.

**Q4. Why was Coptic — a language chanted in Egyptian churches — Champollion's secret
weapon?**
a) Coptic uses hieroglyphic signs, so he could match them one to one.
b) Coptic is the last living descendant of ancient Egyptian — so once a sign yielded a
sound, Coptic could tell him what the sounds MEANT: re, the sun; mise, to give birth. ✔
c) Coptic priests had secretly preserved the ability to read hieroglyphs since 394.
d) The Rosetta Stone's middle band turned out to be written in Coptic.
Feedback ✔: Sounds alone give you a name; a living cousin-language gives you MEANING.
RE-MES-SES wasn't just readable — it made sense: "Re gave him birth."

**Q5. The signs stood in plain sight for 1,400 years, and the stone for 23 more. What
kept the mystery unsolved so long?**
a) Not enough copies of the inscriptions existed for scholars to compare.
b) The languages of Egypt had vanished so completely that no clue survived anywhere.
c) A wrong idea held as fact: that hieroglyphs were pure symbols, not sounds — an
assumption so comfortable that even Champollion believed it until 1821. ✔
d) Scholars refused to share their discoveries with rivals in other countries.
Feedback ✔: The evidence was public; the assumption was the lock. The detective's
lesson of the whole case: test the idea you are most sure of — that is where the case
is stuck.
