# Batch 21–30 sequence gate

## Blind sequence read

Pages 21–23 move from surveillance to discovery. Athos, Porthos, and Aramis encounter the Cardinal during their night patrol and escort him to the Red Dovecote, where his carriage waits. Inside, Athos listens through a broken stovepipe while the Cardinal commissions Milady to sail for England, carry out the work they discussed, seize the young woman hidden at Béthune, and accept a signed paper broad enough to protect anything done for France. Milady bargains for revenge on the Gascon and the woman he loves. After the Cardinal rides away, Athos goes upstairs instead of following him.

Pages 24–26 turn discovery into a personal confrontation and then an urgent disagreement. Milady recognizes Athos as the Comte de la Fère, whom she believed dead; he reveals that he knows her, takes the Cardinal's paper, and leaves her with a warning that any renewed move against the young couple will have witnesses. At dawn he watches her carriage depart, returns to camp, tells d'Artagnan that she is heading for Béthune and will have a half-day's lead unless they move, and sees d'Artagnan reach instinctively for his sword. Athos stops him from riding out without leave because it would get all three followers shot. D'Artagnan answers that he will secure leave that day and will not forgive Athos for the delay; Athos accepts the anger and insists only that he depart first and survive.

Pages 27–30 convert that conflict into a public stratagem and a private council. Aramis and Porthos arrive after hearing the argument. Athos says they need a place where nobody can listen and leave from the King; Porthos's appetite supplies the idea. Porthos publicly challenges the army that the four friends can breakfast for an hour in the exposed bastion of Saint-Gervais, prompting the whole army to bet against them. The next morning the four walk out with food, tablecloth, and swords while the army watches from the trenches. In the ruined bastion, Athos shows them the Cardinal's paper. The friends understand that its sweeping authorization could shield almost any crime. D'Artagnan wants to burn it and leave because every minute brings Milady closer to Constance, but Athos keeps it as leverage: once they catch Milady, the threat of the Cardinal's own paper may prevent him from having them hanged.

## Script comparison and gate judgment

The visible sequence preserves the script's material story and every scripted line, caption, speaker attribution, and paper text across the ten pages. The named characters remain legible through the inn, tent, camp, open-ground, and bastion transitions. The Cardinal's authorization is visibly handed to Milady on page 23, surrendered to Athos on page 24, carried by him on page 25, and unfolded and read on page 30. The northbound carriage, Béthune route, camp restrictions, public wager, supplies, fort, and observing army all carry forward without a consequential continuity break.

The focal line remains Athos's: he discovers the threat, confronts but does not execute Milady, takes responsibility for delaying d'Artagnan, and turns the breakfast wager into both cover for the council and a route to royal leave. D'Artagnan's anger and urgency supply an unbroken counterpressure from pages 25–30. Page turns consistently advance the active problem: discovery becomes confrontation, restraint becomes conflict, conflict produces the stratagem, and the stratagem creates the private council.

Pacing is varied rather than compressed or monotonous. Illustrated prose on pages 21 and 25 handles travel and inference; dramatic pages 22–24 sustain the inn's listening and confrontation; pages 26–28 turn private conflict into public action; page 29 gives the wager a full spectacle beat; and page 30 returns to intimate dramatic stakes. Consequential beats receive immediate aftermath: Athos letting Milady go leads to her departure and d'Artagnan's fury, the denied impulsive pursuit leads to the leave problem, and the wager leads to the watched march and bastion council. No material beat is stranded.
