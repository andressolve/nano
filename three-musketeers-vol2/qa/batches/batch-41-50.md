# Sequence gate — pages 41–50

## Uninterrupted image-only sequence

A pale-haired woman shares wine with Constance at the convent, covertly dosing Constance’s glass from a ring while the maid watches the gate. Constance drinks, toasts d’Artagnan, and sends the woman to meet the arriving riders. At nine, d’Artagnan and the three musketeers ride through the gate as a carriage is already leaving from the back of the convent.

Constance recognizes d’Artagnan’s distinctive horse and explains that she told nobody where she was except a kind pale-haired lady. The realization reaches d’Artagnan just as Constance collapses in his arms. The friends gather around her in stunned silence; Aramis prays, while d’Artagnan understands that his own disclosure led Milady to the convent. He tells Athos it was Milady. Athos admits that he had her at sword point at the Red Dovecote and let her go. D’Artagnan turns Athos’s attempted self-blame back toward the actual murderer: Milady poured the wine. Learning that her carriage went toward the river, d’Artagnan vows to kill her and Athos redirects the impulse into pursuit.

They ride through a storm, follow the carriage tracks until rain erases them, find the carriage abandoned in a ditch, and see a lantern on a ferry or raft above a roaring weir. At the river, Milady holds the raft’s rope and threatens to cut it if anyone approaches. D’Artagnan wants to attack, but Athos stops him because a solitary killing in the dark would repeat Athos’s own disastrous attempt to hang her; instead, Athos insists that Milady hear the charges aloud.

D’Artagnan accuses her of poisoning Constance, Porthos of sending poison and naming them in a note, and Aramis of bargaining with the Cardinal for another girl’s life and then giving that girl to her. Athos says that, as the husband she deceived, he alone may forgive his own wrong, but the wrongs against the others are not his to forgive. Milady answers by telling them to judge her from the bank and cuts or releases the rope.

The current takes the raft toward the weir. Athos enters the river and the companions form a human chain: d’Artagnan holds Athos’s belt, Porthos holds d’Artagnan, and Aramis anchors Porthos to the post. Athos reaches toward Milady and offers a public trial in daylight rather than death by a rope in the dark. Milady refuses to owe him her life after surviving his first hanging and demands that d’Artagnan let go of Athos’s belt so Athos will drown with her. D’Artagnan refuses, leaving the rescue attempt and the moral confrontation unresolved at the page turn.

## Script comparison and sequence judgment

The material story survives intact. Pages 41–43 establish poison, arrival, recognition, and Constance’s death in a clean causal line. Pages 44–45 give that death a full quiet aftermath, make d’Artagnan and Athos confront their respective guilt without displacing Milady’s agency, and convert grief into pursuit. Pages 46–50 carry the pursuit into the river confrontation, where Athos checks d’Artagnan’s revenge, the accusations are spoken, Milady cuts the rope, and the companions risk themselves to offer her lawful judgment rather than repeat Athos’s earlier violence.

All scripted dialogue, captions, and prose are present with the intended speaker or narrator attribution. Named identities remain legible and stable: d’Artagnan is consistently the young man in mustard on the yellow horse; Athos is the dark-haired, bearded musketeer who restrains and then reaches for Milady; Porthos is the broad red-haired musketeer; Aramis is the fair-haired musketeer with the prayer book; Constance remains the grey-clad young woman; and Milady remains the pale-haired woman in black and then plum.

Consequential objects and settings carry correctly across turns: Milady’s poison ring doses the wine; the empty glass falls beside Constance and remains present during the vigil; the yellow horse enables Constance’s recognition and leads the pursuit; the departing black carriage becomes the wreck beside the river; and the rope, knife, raft, lantern, weir, belt, post, and four-person chain make the river danger and attempted rescue spatially intelligible. The progression from cloister to road to ferry landing is clear, and the storm sustains the river sequence without confusing location or time.

Focal integrity is strong. Constance’s death is experienced through d’Artagnan, the aftermath stays with his grief and guilt, and Athos’s intervention enlarges the moral conflict without taking the emotional center away from him. The page turns consistently advance or sharpen the central question: the poisoned glass, the departing carriage, Constance’s collapse, the silent vigil, the decision to ride, the river light, the threatened rope, the stated charges, the human chain, and the paired refusals on page 50.

Pacing is controlled rather than compressed. The illustrated-prose pages perform two different jobs—page 44 holds time still for grief, while page 46 compresses only the necessary hours of pursuit—and both are supported by dominant images. Spectacle pages 42 and 49 punctuate the dramatic exchanges, so the batch does not become a wall of dialogue scenes. Constance’s death receives both emotional and causal aftermath, and Milady’s cutting of the rope receives an immediate physical and moral aftermath across pages 49–50. The unresolved danger at page 50 is a purposeful turn, not a missing beat.
