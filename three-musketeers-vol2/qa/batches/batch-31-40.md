# Sequence gate — pages 31–40

## Uninterrupted sequence

At Saint-Gervais, the four hold the bastion while Porthos refuses to abandon his chicken. They drive off the first attackers, wait out their wagered hour, then meet a much larger return assault by pushing a cracked wall down the slope. The attackers escape the collapse but flee, and the four mark the captured bastion with Aramis's handkerchief before walking back through the army's cheers. D'Artagnan remains fixed on the danger to Constance and the six-day ride to Béthune.

From a hill behind the lines, Rochefort identifies the three Musketeers and the Gascon to the Cardinal. The Cardinal realizes that d'Artagnan may have overheard the name Béthune, predicts that the King will grant the four leave, and sends Rochefort north to warn Milady. The four ride hard through rain, with d'Artagnan's yellow horse always in front, while the black-clad Rochefort races ahead.

At the Béthune convent, the lonely Constance welcomes a kindly supposed widow who claims to be hiding from the Cardinal. Constance unknowingly tells Milady both that d'Artagnan will come for her and that she warned him about Lady de Winter. Rochefort reaches the convent at night and tells Milady that four men are approaching, led by the Gascon on the yellow horse. Milady orders a carriage for first light and plans either to take Constance to England or ensure that she never leaves. In the morning Constance recognizes d'Artagnan's horse on the road. She refuses Milady's attempt to pass the riders off as the Cardinal's men, so Milady smiles and proposes a drink.

## Gate judgment

The material story reads as one clear causal chain: the bastion exploit earns leave; the overheard destination lets the Cardinal send Rochefort ahead; Rochefort's warning turns Milady's established access to Constance into the imminent poisoning threat. The emotional line also holds: d'Artagnan's urgency survives the comic victory and travel passage, while Constance's loneliness explains her misplaced trust before recognition turns hope into danger.

All scripted text is present and attributed to the intended speaker. Named identities remain clear across transitions: the four are visually stable, the Cardinal and Rochefort are distinct, Constance remains identifiable, and the widow is legibly Milady before and after Rochefort names the Gascon. The consequential objects and settings carry correctly across page turns, especially the collapsing wall from pages 32–33, Aramis's white flag into page 35, Rochefort's black horse and red feather from pages 35–36 and 39, Milady's dark ring across pages 38–39, and d'Artagnan's yellow horse from page 36 through the recognition on page 40.

Focal integrity is intact. Pages 31–36 stay with the race initiated by d'Artagnan and the Cardinal's countermove; pages 37–40 shift cleanly to Constance and Milady, then reunite the two lines at the convent road. Every page turn delivers the promised development without a material spatial, temporal, or attribution break.

The pacing is varied and complete: comic defense becomes coordinated action, the wall collapse receives both immediate safety confirmation and public aftermath, the Cardinal's response converts victory into pursuit, two illustrated-prose pages compress the six-day race and establish Constance's vulnerability, and the closing dramatic pages slow down for trust, disclosure, interception, and threat. The travel compression is purposeful rather than summary-heavy, and the batch does not become a wall of dramatic scenes.

No material sequence defect or reader harm justifies a complete-page redraw.
