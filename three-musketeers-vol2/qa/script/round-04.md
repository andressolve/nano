# Script gate, round 4: *The Three Musketeers, Volume II: Milady*

## Cold read

A boy from Gascony, eighteen, is the only one of four friends without the Musketeers' blue cloak, and he wants one badly. Across the river, the Cardinal is tired of watching his man Rochefort lose to the boy, so he sends a woman called Milady after him. She stages a runaway carriage, the boy stops it, and she invites him in by the front door, which is the thing he has always wanted. Constance, the girl downstairs, is being hidden in a convent in the north. Before she leaves she warns him about ladies who are kind for no reason. Then he tells Milady about the convent anyway.

He overhears Milady and Rochefort, sees a lily branded on her shoulder, and escapes through a window into a hay cart. That night Athos goes grey and tells the story of "a friend", a count who hanged his wife and later found the rope cut. The boy works out that the count was Athos.

The four go to war at La Rochelle and Milady follows. Poisoned wine arrives with the three friends' names on it. Through a broken stovepipe Athos overhears the Cardinal give Milady two things: Constance's convent, Béthune, as her price, and a signed paper that excuses whatever she does. Athos takes the paper and lets Milady go. The boy is furious with him, but leaving the siege without permission means being shot. So they make a bet: breakfast in the bastion for one hour under the city's guns. They push a wall onto the attackers and win their leave. The Cardinal guesses where they are going and sends Rochefort ahead to warn Milady.

At Béthune, Milady has come as a kind widow and won Constance over. When the four ride into sight, she poisons Constance, who dies in the boy's arms. They chase Milady to a ferry in a flood. Each of them names her crimes, and she cuts the rope. Athos wades in, held by a chain of his three friends, and offers her a trial in daylight. She refuses and the weir takes her.

Back in Paris the boy hands the Cardinal his own paper. The Cardinal burns it and gives him an officer's commission with the name left blank. Each of the three refuses it, and Athos writes the boy's name in. The boy lights Constance's lamp himself, and four blue cloaks walk down the street while the man in red watches.

I could tell all of that after one read without the notes. It passes the paraphrase test.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p25:** "'After England,' the Cardinal had said." I went back to p22 to find the line. It isn't there, and the phrase doesn't make sense as written. (Finding 1.)
- **pp 25–27:** On p25 Porthos and Aramis are in the tent. On p26 they are gone. On p27 they "duck into the tent" as if arriving. (Finding 2.)
- **p25, smaller:** the dominant panel has Athos on a rise watching the carriage go, paper in hand. The prose under it has him riding back with the carriage "behind him" and the paper "inside his coat." (Part of Finding 2.)
- **p55, briefly:** why would the Cardinal leave the name blank? The answer arrives in the next balloon ("Men who can't be bought…"), and pp 55–56 turn the blank into the point. I didn't count that as a real stop.

I did not stop anywhere else, including all six transitions between movements.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book. In its last three movements it is better than good, and I felt it.

**Movement I (pp 1–9).** The appetite is set up properly. The boy's want for the cloak (p3) and his want to be let in are the same want, and Milady's line "Not by the kitchen door. By the front door." (p6) is aimed straight at it. That is why he goes back, and the reader respects him for it rather than thinking him a fool, which is what the mandate asks. Constance's farewell gets its two pages. Her warning on p7 is a line of her own, practical and Parisian, not a moral. On p9 the boy betrays her in two balloons, against his own scruple ("I'm not supposed to say… Well —"). That is enacted, not narrated, and it hurts. Milady's "How very clever of her" is subtext doing exactly its job.

**Movement II (pp 10–18).** The tavern on p11 is the best subtext in the book: Athos's "Describe her." / "Her name." / "I don't know that name." with the untouched wine. The lily, the window and "Which shoulder?" / "Then she isn't dead." land one after another without crowding each other. Athos's story on p16 is told in his voice as "a friend". "Without asking her one question" sets up the moral spine of the whole climax. The attribution-strip test passes on every page. Porthos puts himself into every sentence, Aramis is sly and Church-bound, Athos is clipped, and the boy says the big thing first.

**Movement III (pp 19–25).** "That was good grass." Athos's "I have never in my life sent anyone wine. I drink it." is a joke made of character, not a quip. The stovepipe scene has real heat: on p22 the Cardinal hands a girl's life across a table as a price, and the reader hears it with Athos. The confrontation on p24 ("We were both misinformed") is the best two-hander in the book. It is spoiled slightly by the muddle on p25, and the boy's anger on p26 is exactly what the reader feels.

**Movement IV (pp 26–36).** This is appetite and urgency together. The bet is pure pleasure, with a basket, a tablecloth and a falling wall. Because the boy counts the minutes against Constance throughout ("Fifteen minutes. Forty-five more, and all that time she's riding"; "One more minute?"), the fun has a fuse lit under it. The Cardinal on the hill ("I would rather have that boy in my red than in the ground") with Rochefort's reply, then "Then let Milady arrange it", is a villain choosing in front of us.

**Movement V (pp 37–45).** Pp 37–41 belong to Constance, and she stays an active chooser. She refuses to run ("I'm staying right here"), and the poison follows from that refusal. Her telling the kind widow about Lady de Winter by name on p38 is a horrible, excellent irony. The cloister death is quiet and earned. The silent grief page (p44) stays with the moment. On p45, "You didn't pour the wine either" is the boy forgiving Athos without saying so, and it pays off nine pages later when the Cardinal says "I did not pour the wine." That echo is the moral design of the book working in dialogue.

**Movement VI (pp 46–52).** The judgment on p48 gives each friend his own charge in his own voice. Athos's "That one is mine to forgive. The others aren't." is the line of the volume. The chain of four men in the flood is the engine made into a picture. Milady's "He'd drown for me. He always would." / "Never." has real heat. Athos's "Good." on p52, glossed as the only way he can bear things, pays off a habit set up on p3 and p31.

**Movement VII (pp 53–58).** The boy has a want on every page: he takes the paper from Athos, accuses the Cardinal, and refuses to hide behind the paper. On p56 the commission's meaning flips from "his" to "the King's blue" through argument, not ceremony. The lamp on p57 closes the first image of p2. No serene protagonist anywhere; his appetite drives every movement.

**Want-from:** every scene passes. Even the report scene on p39 has Milady wanting a decision out of Rochefort's news ("Then she'll stay here. One way or another."), so it is not logistics.

**Aphorism:** none that blocks. The Cardinal's "Men who can't be bought are rare…" is his own register and answers a direct question.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p25: the deduction quotes a line nobody said.**
- **The failure:** the prose reads: "'After England,' the Cardinal had said. But the road to the sea and the boats for England runs north…" On p22 the Cardinal actually said "You'll take ship for England from the north coast within the week." Nobody ever says "After England", and the phrase doesn't make sense on its own: after England *what*?
- **What the reader has to repair:** this is the page that gives the causal step "she will stop at Béthune on her way to the boat." The first-time reader reaches a quotation in quotation marks, can't place it, flips back to p22, doesn't find it, and has to rebuild the logic for themselves. That is solving the adaptation on the exact sentence the chase depends on.
- **Revision:** quote what was said and make the inference plain. For example: "'Take ship for England from the north coast,' the Cardinal had said. The road to the north coast runs through the flat country, and Béthune lies on that road." The rest of the paragraph can stay as written.

**2. pp 25–27: Porthos and Aramis are in the tent, then absent, then arrive; and p25's picture contradicts its own prose.**
- **The failure:** p25 Panel 2 stages "Athos speaking to the other three by lantern light; the boy already on his feet," and the prose says Athos "told the others." On p26 only the boy and Athos are in the tent, now by "Dawn light through canvas." On p27, "PORTHOS and ARAMIS ducking into the tent." Separately, p25's dominant panel shows Athos "on a rise, watching it go, the folded paper in his hand," while the prose has him riding back with the carriage "behind him" and the paper "inside his coat."
- **What the reader has to repair:** the images will be drawn faithfully, so across a page turn the reader sees two friends present, then vanished, then arriving. They have to guess that the pair left and came back. On p25 the picture and the words underneath disagree about where Athos is and what he is holding.
- **Revision:**
  - Restage p25 Panel 2 as Athos and the boy alone in the tent ("Athos telling the boy by lantern light; the boy already on his feet"), and change the prose to "Athos told d'Artagnan."
  - Then p27's entrance is a true entrance. Optionally add a beat for Aramis such as "We heard you shouting from three tents away. So did everyone else," which also sets up his "every tent has ears."
  - Make p25's panel and prose agree. Either the prose says Athos "watched it go from a rise, the paper in his hand, before he rode back," or the panel shows the carriage leaving behind a rider heading south.
  - Choose lantern light or dawn for the tent and use it on both pages.

## Nonblocking observations

- **pp 24 and 26, the repeated line:** "I judged her once, alone, in an hour. I won't do it twice." appears two pages after "I judged you once, alone, in an hour…" The echo is deliberate, but said to the boy it reads like a stock refrain rather than an answer to his fury. Consider a version spoken to him: "The last time I judged her, I did it alone, in an hour, without one question. I won't do that twice — not even for you."
- **p19:** "I heard my name on a bottle I never sent." is slightly awkward ("heard my name *on* a bottle"). Possible fix: "That's my name on a bottle I never sent."
- **p8 against pp 36–43:** the boy promised to come only "the first day she says", meaning when the Queen says Constance is safe, and he breaks that promise to save her. Nobody remarks on it, and "You promised, and you came" smooths it over. That's acceptable. One clause of interior thought on p36 ("He had promised to wait for the Queen's word. He was not waiting.") would turn it into a felt choice.
- **Text density:**
  - p17 is about 95 words in 8 balloons.
  - p54 is about 98 words in 7 balloons.
  - p56 is about 96 words in 7 balloons.
  - All three are above the 80-word dramatic norm but within the ~105 allowance "only with large protected balloon lanes", so the page contract rows for those pages should mark the lanes. No cut is prescribed. If any of them fails to fit, split the page rather than trim it.
- **p53:** "This was always yours to finish." reads cleanly once p54 shows what "this" is. It works as subtext, and I'm noting it only because "this" refers forward.
- **Five-word audit, all 31 balloons checked:** each is either a complete thought or an answer settled by the line before it plus the visible action. That includes "Her name." and "Describe her." (Athos's clipped voice, with the question obvious), "The Gascon?" (answers "Four of them" and is resolved by the next line), "Never." (a direct refusal of "Let go of his belt") and "Constance?" (over her closing eyes). None fails.
- **Architecture:**
  - Every page declares one mode, one turn and one dominant panel, and no page has more than two locations.
  - Mode share is 37/12/9, about 64/21/16. No chronicle pages, as the mandate specifies.
  - Prose fields run from about 85 to 130 words, under the 140 ceiling.
- **Cast:** within the ceiling of 8 locked characters. No functional crowds; nuns, guards and townsmen stay scenery.
- **Intros:** every returning face is re-identified before the plot depends on recognizing them: Athos, Porthos and Aramis by name on pp 2–3, Rochefort by caption and scar on p4, the Cardinal on p2 and p4, Constance as "the girl downstairs" on p2, the ring and the horse on p2.
