# Script gate, round 5: *The Three Musketeers, Volume II: Milady*

Critic: fresh, read-only, no history. I read the script cold, start to finish, before opening anything else. I skipped the round-history bullets until after that read. After it I read `method/00-CRAFT-MANDATE.md`, `method/02-SCRIPT-LOOP.md` §3, the text budget in `method/06-TYPOGRAPHY.md`, `qa/script/audit-r05.txt`, `00-VOLUME-MANDATE.md`, and then `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md`.

## 1. Cold read

**What happens.** A Gascon boy named d'Artagnan has three Musketeer friends, Athos, Porthos and Aramis. He is the only one of the four without the blue cloak, and he wants it badly ("something nobody in France can forget"). The Cardinal has already lost to him four times through his man Rochefort. Now he sends a beautiful woman, Milady, to find where the boy's sweetheart Constance is hidden, because Constance carried the Queen's secrets.

Milady stages a runaway carriage so the boy can rescue her. She invites him in "by the front door", and he tells her what he knows: a convent in the north. Constance warned him about kind ladies before she left. He goes back to Milady every day and stops turning up for his friends. Athos asks him to describe her, says "I don't know that name," and does not touch his wine.

The boy overhears Milady reporting to Rochefort and confronts her. In the struggle her sleeve tears and he sees a thief's lily branded on her shoulder. She swears he will die for it, and he goes out of a window into a hay cart. Athos asks "Which shoulder?" and then says "Then she isn't dead." He tells the story of "a friend", a count who hanged his branded wife without asking her one question. It was Athos.

Then comes the war at La Rochelle. A poisoned bottle arrives with the three friends' names forged on it. At the Red Dovecote inn, Athos listens through a broken stovepipe. He hears the Cardinal send Milady to England, give her Constance (at Béthune) as her price, and hand her a paper that excuses anything she does. Athos confronts her, takes the paper, and lets her go because he will not judge her alone a second time. The boy is furious with him. Leaving the siege without permission means being shot, so the four win the King's leave the loud way: breakfast for an hour in an enemy bastion, on a bet, with the whole army watching.

On the hill, the Cardinal works out that Athos heard the name Béthune and sends Rochefort north to warn Milady. At Béthune, Milady, dressed as a widow, has already made friends with Constance. Constance names "Lady de Winter" to her face as the woman to beware of. Constance sees the yellow horse coming and refuses to run. Milady drops poison from her ring into a toast. The four come through the gate as her carriage leaves by the back. Constance dies in the boy's arms: "You promised, and you came."

They catch Milady at a flooded ferry. Each of them says out loud what she did, and she cuts the rope. Athos wades in, held by a chain of his three friends, and offers her his hand and a trial in daylight. She refuses it and the river takes her.

Back in Paris, the boy hands the Cardinal his own blank pardon, unasked. The Cardinal burns it and writes a Musketeer officer's commission with the name left empty. The boy offers it to each friend, each refuses, and Athos writes in "D'Artagnan". The last image is four blue cloaks walking abreast, and the man in red at his window.

**Where I stopped.** I never stopped to rebuild what happened. I paused briefly three times:

1. **p43**: "You promised, and you came." On p8 the boy promised the opposite: not to come "one day before" the Queen said it was safe. I read past it as Constance's tenderness. It is an irony the book never acknowledges (observation 1).
2. **p52**: "He always said it when things were bad." I looked back for Athos's "Good." pattern and found only p3 (which is not a bad moment) and p31 (observation 2).
3. **pp 25/34/36**: "a day ahead". The King gives leave "that same afternoon", so by the script's own clock she is about half a day ahead. A child will not do the sum (observation 3).

None of these changes what happened, why, or what changed. All three cold-read questions were answered by the page in front of me.

**Paraphrase test (§8.10).** Passed after one read, for every movement.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book, and its back half is better than good. Movement by movement:

**I — The Lady in the Carriage (pp 1–9).** Charming, and the trap is set where the reader can see it and the boy can't. That dramatic irony (p4 tells us the noon plan before p5 springs it) gives every Milady page a little dread.
- The boy's want is loud and specific: the blue cloak, and being let in by the front door. Milady's "Not by the kitchen door. By the front door" (p6) is a perfect hook into exactly that want, so he reads as hungry rather than foolish.
- Constance's goodbye (pp 7–8) has real heat. Her line "If you don't know, nobody can make you tell. That's the whole point" sets up the irony that he hands her location to the one person who asks.
- "How very clever of her" (p9) is subtext doing its job.

**II — The Lily (pp 10–18).** The strongest early stretch.
- p11 is a model page. Each speaker has a different want from the boy: Porthos wants him back, Aramis wants to warn him without seeming to, Athos wants the truth while hiding why. "I don't know that name" is literally true and a lie in every way that matters.
- "Which shoulder?" / "Then she isn't dead" (p15) is the best turn in the book: three words that change the meaning of every page before them.
- The window escape (p14) gives steel and humour at the right moment.
- Athos telling it as "a friend" (p16) and the boy making him own it (p17) respects both characters.

**III — The Red Dovecote (pp 19–25).** Tense, and the mechanics stay in service of people. The forged bottle repeats the forged-note trick from p5, so the reader learns how Milady works.
- "You saw her carriage, and you didn't tell me?" (p20) is real friction between the friends.
- The stovepipe is clean eavesdropping.
- On p24 Athos and Milady meet as equals: "We were both misinformed." His mercy is a choice, and the reader can see exactly what it costs.

**IV — The Bastion (pp 26–36).**
- p26 is the heart of the friendship: the boy wants to go now, Athos wants him alive, and "Hate me on the road, if you like" is fully earned.
- The bastion is the set piece the readers will remember: chicken under fire, Porthos's wall, Aramis's handkerchief. It never goes slack, because the boy keeps counting minutes toward Constance. Spectacle with an appetite under it.
- The Cardinal on the hill gives the villain a mind: "I would rather have that boy in my red than in the ground."

**V — Béthune (pp 37–45).** Devastating, and fair to Constance. She is an active chooser to the end: she refuses to run because she knows his horse.
- p38 is cruel in the best way. Constance warns Milady against Lady de Winter to her face, and Milady answers "You were right to warn him."
- The poison stays a small, legible gesture of the ring, planted on pp 4, 9, 38 and 39.
- The death page is quiet, and the aftermath is earned. The p44 prose ("Aramis… said the prayer for the dead… without one mistake"; "The roses smelled of nothing at all") is the best prose in the volume.
- On p45 Athos takes the blame, and "You didn't pour the wine either" gives the boy his first adult act of mercy, which the Cardinal later echoes in the reverse spirit.

**VI — The River (pp 46–52).** The climax goes to the friendship, as the mandate wants.
- The chain of four men in the flood is the image of the book.
- Milady stays herself to the end ("I won't owe you my life now"), and the boy holding the belt ("Never.") is his choice of friend over rage, never stated.
- The p52 aftermath refuses the easy catharsis: "He felt cold, and very tired, and he wanted Constance to be alive."

**VII — The Paper (pp 53–58).** It lands. The boy giving back the pardon is a moral act the reader can name, and "I named a convent. I did not pour the wine" convicts the Cardinal in his own voice. The commission scene is funny and tender ("I'd have to read things"; "Next year. Probably."). The lamp the boy lights at Constance's door on p57 closes the image opened on p2 and hurts exactly as much as it should.

**Presence tests across the script:**
- **Want-from:** passes on every dramatic page I checked. No scene runs on "to inform".
- **Logistics dialogue:** none blocking. Arrangements (carriage at first light, noon tomorrow, the north road) are always threats or plans spoken by a character with a want.
- **Aphorism:** the riskiest lines are "Too much for Athos. Too little for the man I was" (p56) and "Men who can't be bought are rare…" (p55). Both pass the attribution-strip test, because only Athos and only the Cardinal could say them.
- **Attribution-strip:** six distinct voices. Porthos puts himself and food in every line, Aramis is sly about the Church, Athos is spare, Milady is warm with the cruelty at the end of the sentence, the Cardinal speaks in quiet complete sentences, and Rochefort keeps counting.
- **Heat and appetite:** the boy is never serene. He is hungry in I, ashamed in II, furious in IV, broken in V, and holding on in VI.
- **Page-turn:** every page has a real reason to turn.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**NONE.**

Checks behind that:

- **Introductions and gaps (§3b).**
  - Every face is named before the plot depends on it: the three by the boy on p3, Rochefort on p4 and p5, Milady by name on p4 and by her own introduction on p6, Constance on p2 and p7.
  - Every rule is planted before it matters: drink only what we drink (p17 before p19), desertion means death (p18 before p26), the King rewards what the army saw (p3 and p28 before p36).
  - The Cardinal's deduction is shown on p35. Rochefort's count runs consistently from four to five to six.
  - The paper passes from Cardinal (p23) to Milady to Athos (p24), then to the boy (p53) and back to the Cardinal (p54), where it is burned (p55).
  - Nobody acts on knowledge the reader didn't see them get.
- **Anti-terse (§7).** I audited all 31 balloons of five words or fewer. Each is either a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before it, with one possible referent:
  - "Describe her." / "Her name." refer to the great lady just mentioned.
  - "Then she isn't dead." is a deliberate mystery, paid off on the next page.
  - "The Gascon?" is answered in the same panel.
  - "Never." answers "Let go of his belt" in the same panel.
  - None fails.
- **Page architecture (§3d).**
  - Every page has one mode, one turn and one dominant panel.
  - No page has more than two locations. p7's street door and stair are one house.
  - Text budgets fit: the densest dramatic pages are p11 (about 84 words), p17 (about 86), p54 (about 92) and p56 (about 92), all inside the protected-lane ceiling. Every prose field is at or under 130 words.
  - No page has four or more equal panels.
- **Fidelity anxiety (§3e).** There is no functional crowd: the cast is 8 locked characters, and guards, nuns and soldiers are scenery with no balloons. Every character changes what the boy wants, knows or chooses. Nothing reads as included out of obligation.
- **Balance (§3f).** 37 dramatic / 12 prose / 9 spectacle, which is 64 / 21 / 16 percent. There are no Chronicle pages, which matches the mandate. Every consequential beat gets its aftermath:
  - the lily: p15 and p16
  - letting Milady go: p26
  - Constance's death: p44 and p45
  - Milady's end: p52
  - the commission: p57
- **Grief, mechanics and revelation** are never stacked on one page.
- **Story stands without Chronicle:** not applicable.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **pp 8 / 36 / 43: the broken promise is never acknowledged.** On p8 the boy promises not to come "one day before" the Queen says it is safe. He comes anyway, rightly, and Constance dies saying "You promised, and you came." A sharp ten-year-old will notice the contradiction. *Suggestion:* add one sentence to the p36 prose, such as "He had promised not to come before the Queen said she was safe. He was coming anyway." That turns a seam into an appetite beat and makes p43 land harder.
2. **p52: "He always said it when things were bad."** The script plants Athos's "Good." only on p3 (not a bad moment) and p31. *Suggestion:* give Athos one more plain "Good." at a clearly bad moment before Béthune, for example after the grass goes black on p20 or at the end of p26. That would let the p52 realization land as recognition rather than as information.
3. **pp 25 / 34 / 36: "a day ahead."** By the script's own clock, Milady leaves at dawn and the four leave before supper, so her lead is about half a day. *Suggestion:* change p36 to "half a day ahead" ("Half a day was nothing. Half a day was everything."), or let Athos's p25 estimate stand and drop the repeats. This is harmless for these readers.
4. **p22: "the work we spoke of"** in England is never paid off. That is acceptable under the mandate rule that the Cardinal's politics are felt, never explained. Just make sure it gets no further mentions.
5. **p11** has nine balloons. They are short and within the word budget, but the packet should reserve balloon lanes, because three speakers share one tavern table.
6. **p57: "He had come to Paris wanting to be let in"** is the only sentence in the book that states its theme. It is allowed as narration revealing a private belief, and the next sentence earns it. Note it, and change nothing unless a later reader flags it.
7. **Ledger drift:** the ledger lists Aramis's "as the Fathers tell us" and Porthos's "I'm hungry", but the script doesn't use them. The script is the authority here, so update the ledger before packets are written.
