# Script gate — round 03 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume II: Milady*

## Cold read

**What happened, as a first-time reader.** It is summer in Paris, and d'Artagnan is the only one of four friends without the Musketeers' blue cloak. He wants one badly. The Cardinal sets a new agent on him: Milady, a pale-haired lady in plum. She arranges for her carriage horses to bolt so that he can rescue her. Then she invites him in "by the front door," which nobody in Paris has done for him before.

Constance, the girl downstairs, is sent by the Queen into hiding in an unnamed convent in the north. Before she goes she warns him about ladies who are kind for no reason. The next Thursday he tells Milady exactly what Constance told him not to tell: "a convent in the north." He keeps going back to Milady and forgets his friends.

Athos asks the boy to describe her and lies that he doesn't know the name. The boy overhears Milady report to Rochefort. He fights her, and her torn sleeve shows a lily branded on her shoulder. He escapes through a window into a hay cart. "Which shoulder?" Athos asks, and then: "Then she isn't dead." Athos tells the story of the count who hanged his sixteen-year-old wife, and the boy says it aloud: the count was Athos.

The army marches on La Rochelle, with a plum-curtained carriage at the back. Someone sends the boy poisoned wine. At the Red Dovecote inn the three Musketeers listen through a broken stovepipe. They hear the Cardinal give Milady Constance's convent, Béthune, as her price, and a signed paper that makes anything she does legal. Athos goes upstairs, takes the paper, and lets her go. The boy is furious with him for it. The four win a breakfast bet in an exposed bastion so that the King will give them leave to go after her, and they ride north. Rochefort is sent ahead to warn Milady.

At Béthune, Milady has befriended a lonely Constance by posing as a widow in hiding. When Constance sees the yellow horse coming and refuses to leave, Milady poisons her wine with her ring. Constance dies in the boy's arms. The four chase Milady to a flooded ferry. She cuts the ferry loose, refuses Athos's offered hand and a trial in daylight, and the river takes her over the weir. In Paris the boy hands the Cardinal back his own paper. The Cardinal burns it and gives him a blank Musketeers' commission. Athos writes in the boy's name, and four blue cloaks walk down the street.

I could tell this to someone else after one read, which is the paraphrase test, and it passes.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p56, the only real stop.** "A Musketeer's commission… Athos, you take it." Athos is already a Musketeer. I reread, and only Porthos's "Me, an officer?" told me what the paper actually is. That is Finding 1.
- **p19.** How did Athos know to run? I worked out that he must have heard the boy read the label aloud.
- **p35.** "He heard me name Béthune." I accepted it, but the Cardinal is stating as fact something he can only suspect.
- **p47.** "I have a knife, and that is a very deep river." It is a threat, but I had to work out what she is threatening to do.
- **p57.** The word "cassock" appears in rendered text for the first time, on the page that pays off "blue cloak."
- **p27.** "And not in here." I had to stop and ask: not *what* in here?

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good script, and in Movements V and VI it is better than good.

**Movement I (pp 1–9): alive, and it earns the boy's mistake.**
- The front door works as an engine. The reader is told on p4 exactly what will happen at noon, and then watches the boy walk into it happily.
- p8's "I can promise you two of those" is the best line in the opening: it is in character, funny, and it plants the promise.
- p9 has real subtext. "I'm not supposed to say where she's gone." / "Well —" and then he says it. Milady answers "How very clever of her," which is cruel in a register only she uses.
- Want-from is clean in every scene:
  - Milady wants the girl's location from him.
  - He wants to be admired by her.
  - Constance wants him careful while she can't watch him.

**Movement II (pp 10–18): alive.**
- p11 is the best scene in the first half. Athos's glass goes down and stays down, "Describe her," "Her name," "I don't know that name." It is a lie the reader can feel.
- p15's "Which shoulder?" / "Then she isn't dead" is a page-turn that lands.
- p16 is told in Athos's voice as "a friend," and p17 lets the boy say the truth for him. That is the right division of labour.
- Heat: the boy is learning that his best friend once hanged the woman who is now hunting him.

**Movement III (pp 19–25): alive, with the most machinery in the book, but the machinery is staged as eavesdropping and confrontation rather than explained.**
- "That was good grass" and "I have never in my life sent anyone wine. I drink it" keep the poison scene funny and frightening at once.
- At the Red Dovecote the Cardinal answers only half of Milady's price. He gives her the girl and says nothing about the Gascon, and on p35 that silence turns into an order. That is real subtext from a man whose voice is unmistakable.
- p24 holds: "We were both misinformed."

**Movement IV (pp 26–36): the most fun in the book.**
- The new p26 does what it was added to do. The boy finally gets to be angry at Athos, and "Hate me on the road, if you like. But get leave first, and go alive" is Athos at his best.
- The bastion is a set piece that ten- and seven-year-old readers will reread: chicken under fire, the wall going over, "All for one — and push!", and Aramis's handkerchief.
- The boy's appetite is never written out. He counts minutes and looks north on every page, so the comedy plays against his urgency rather than replacing it.

**Movement V (pp 37–45): the heart of the volume, and it hurt.**
- p38 is the best dramatic irony in the script: Constance warns Milady about Lady de Winter, and Milady replies "You were right to warn him."
- p40's "Then let's drink to him first" made me turn the page with dread, which is exactly the job.
- In p43, "I told her nobody else would ride a horse that colour" turns the boy's ridiculous horse, a running joke since page 1, into the thing that killed her. That is the best structural payoff in the book.
- p44's silent grief page is correctly placed and earned ("The roses smelled of nothing at all").
- p45's "You didn't pour the wine either" is then echoed by the Cardinal on p54, so the same words absolve one man and condemn the other. Excellent.

**Movement VI (pp 46–52): alive.**
- The chain of four men in the flood is the friendship made physical.
- Milady's "Let go of his belt, boy, and he comes with me. He'd drown for me. He always would" is her cruellest truth, delivered at the end of a sentence exactly as her voice is meant to work.
- The boy's "Never." is earned, and so is p52's aftermath for both the boy and Athos.

**Movement VII (pp 53–58): alive until p56, where the final turn of the book wobbles on a logic gap.**
- The Cardinal's study (pp 54–55) has real heat. The boy is accusing the most powerful man in France to his face, with the man's own pardon in his hand.
- "I named a convent. I did not pour the wine" is the volume's moral spine, said by the man least entitled to it.
- Everything about the ending works emotionally. Finding 1 is about making it make sense on the first read.

**Voices.** The attribution-strip test passes almost everywhere:
- Porthos always puts himself in the sentence.
- Aramis is sly and undercuts himself ("Next year. Probably.").
- Athos speaks in the fewest words.
- The Cardinal speaks in quiet complete sentences.
- Rochefort keeps counting, always one word too many.
- Milady is warm with a sting at the end.
- The one place the voices go flat is the formal triple accusation on p48, discussed under observations.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p55–p56: what the commission is is never said, so the boy's offer to Athos and the three refusals make no sense on a first read.**
- **The failure.**
  - p55, the Cardinal: "A commission in the King's Musketeers. I've left the name empty."
  - p56, the boy: "A Musketeer's commission, with no name in it… Athos, you take it."
  - Athos: "Too much for Athos. Too little for the man I was. No."
  - Since p1 the reader has been told that this paper means the blue cloak, and that Athos, Porthos and Aramis already wear it. Offering a Musketeer's commission to three Musketeers is meaningless. "Too much for Athos" cannot be understood, because the reader cannot see why a plain place in a company he already belongs to would be "too much."
  - Only Porthos's next line, "Me, an officer?", reveals that the paper is a rank. By then the volume's last dramatic turn has already been read wrong, and the reader has to go back and reinterpret Athos's best line.
- **What the reader must repair.** They have to infer, after the fact, from a joke, that "commission" means an officer's rank. This happens on the page that resolves the want the book planted on p3. The resolution is recoverable but not effortless, and recoverable is a revision flag.
- **Prescription.** Name the thing in the dialogue on both pages. Do not remove any causal language. Either of these works:
  - **(a) Name the rank.** This keeps every existing line.
    - p55: "A commission as an officer of the King's Musketeers. I've left the name empty. Write in it whoever you like."
    - p56, the boy: "I gave him back his paper, and he gave me this. An officer's commission in the Musketeers, with no name in it." / "I won't wear his name on me. Athos, you've earned it ten times over. You take it."
    - "Too much for Athos. Too little for the man I was" and "Me, an officer?" then land on first reading.
    - p57's "the blue was exactly the blue of the other three" still holds.
  - **(b) Keep the paper a plain Musketeer's place and recast the offer.** The boy asks the three to write in *anyone's* name except his: "Write in any name you like — some poor soldier from the trenches. Not mine." Each friend then refuses to choose, for his own reason, and Athos writes the boy's name. This would mean rewriting Athos's and Porthos's refusals.
  - Option (a) is the smaller change and keeps the best lines.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p19.** Athos "striding up fast" knows the bottle is labelled from the three before anyone shows him. The reader can infer he heard the boy read it aloud, but one line pays it for certain and adds heat: "I heard my name on a bottle I never sent." The page also passes over the fact that the boy is breaking p17's rule ("drink only what we drink"). One Athos line, "What did I tell you about drinking?", would turn the rescue into a rebuke and give Athos a want-from in the scene.
2. **p26.** Athos answers the boy's fury with the exact sentence he said to Milady two pages earlier: "I judged her once, alone, in an hour. I won't do it twice." Repeated word for word, it reads as a slogan rather than an answer. Either have Athos say it freshly in his own words to the boy, or let the boy hear it as a formula and push on it.
3. **p27, panel 1.** Aramis opens the page with "And not in here," right after the page turn. A listener asks "not what in here?" Use "And don't talk about leaving in here."
4. **p4, panel 2.** "The girl downstairs from that boy carries the Queen's secrets. Find out where she keeps them." This reads as a hiding place for letters, but what Milady actually reports back is where the girl went, and the letter thread is never paid off. Use "Find out where the girl goes, and get him out of my way." It is cleaner and matches pp 12 and 22.
5. **p35.** "He heard me name Béthune" is stated as fact by a man who cannot know it. "I think he heard me name Béthune" keeps the Cardinal clever without making him all-knowing. It is also one word truer to his voice.
6. **pp 25, 34, 36, the time.** Milady leaves before dawn. The four leave "before supper" the same day. The narration on p36 says she was "a day ahead," while also saying a carriage is slower than a horseman. Despite that, she reaches Béthune an afternoon and a night before them. Few readers will compute this, but p36 could simply say "most of a day ahead, and riding without stopping."
7. **p43.** "You came. You promised, and you came." The p8 promise was to come *only* when the Queen said she was safe. The irony is poignant if it is meant, but nothing on the page acknowledges it. Consider letting p44's prose hold it in one clause.
8. **pp 46, 51, the weir.** "Weir" is the key danger at the climax and is never glossed. One clause on p46 fixes it: "a weir, where the whole river fell over a wall of stones."
9. **p47.** "I have a knife, and that is a very deep river." What she is threatening to do is left implicit. Use "Come one step onto this raft and I cut the rope."
10. **p48.** The three accusations are parallel and formal. Porthos's line is his, but the panel slightly loses the voices. Consider letting Aramis's line be sly rather than judicial.
11. **p53.** "This part is mine to do." / "This was always yours to finish." Both lines lean on a vague "this" and are close to aphorism. The boy could say "Facing him is mine to do," and Athos could say something only Athos would say.
12. **p57.** "The cassock was new" is the first time "cassock" appears in rendered text. Every other rendered line in the book says "cloak," including p58's "Four blue cloaks." Use "The blue cloak was new" on the payoff page.

## Five-word audit

All 31 short balloons listed by the mechanical audit pass:
- Each is either a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before it, with one visible referent.
- The marginal case is p7's "What lady?", which reaches back across one exchange. It passes because only one lady has been mentioned, and the change of subject is the point of the line.

## Architecture and balance

- Every page has one mode, one dominant panel, and no more than two locations.
- The mode split is 37 dramatic / 12 illustrated prose / 9 spectacle (64 / 21 / 16 percent), which is within the target.
- There are no chronicle pages, and the volume mandate opts out of them.
- Every consequential beat has its aftermath: Constance's death gets pp 44–45, Milady's end gets pp 51–52, and the commission gets p57.
- There is no functional crowd, and every character earns a place.
