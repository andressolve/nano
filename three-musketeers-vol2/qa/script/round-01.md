# Script Gate — Round 01 — *The Three Musketeers, Volume II: Milady*

Script read: `books/three-musketeers-vol2/08-FULL-SCRIPT.md` (57 pp, Round 1 draft), cold, in order, before any other project document. After that: `qa/script/audit-r01.txt`, `00-VOLUME-MANDATE.md`, `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md`, and `method/06-TYPOGRAPHY.md`.

## Cold read

A Gascon boy of eighteen, d'Artagnan, has three Musketeer friends in blue cloaks. He wants a blue cloak of his own, and he lives in an attic above Constance, the girl who carries the Queen's letters. The Cardinal has failed to get rid of him four times through a scarred man in black. Now he sends Milady, a beautiful lady in plum. She sets up a runaway-carriage rescue, opens her front door to the boy, and he tells her that Constance has been hidden in "a convent in the north." Constance has already left Paris after warning him about ladies who are kind for no reason. The boy overhears Milady reporting to the scarred man. He sees a thief's lily branded on her shoulder and escapes through a window into a hay cart. Athos then tells him the story of "a friend," a count who hanged his branded wife and found the rope cut. The friend was Athos.

The war at La Rochelle takes everyone to the coast, Milady included. Poisoned wine arrives "from" the three friends. Through a broken stovepipe at the Red Dovecote inn, Athos overhears the Cardinal name the convent at Béthune as Milady's price and hand her a blank pardon. Athos goes upstairs, confronts her, takes the paper, and lets her go. To get leave from the King, the four eat breakfast under the guns in a ruined bastion, bring a wall down on the attackers, and win. They ride north for six days. At Béthune, Milady, posing as a fugitive widow, has befriended Constance. She poisons her with a ring minutes before the four ride in, and Constance dies in the boy's arms. In a storm they catch Milady at a ferry. Each of them names a crime. She cuts the ferry rope, refuses Athos's hand from the flood (he is held by a chain of his three friends), and goes over the weir. Back in Paris, the Cardinal threatens the boy. The boy lays the Cardinal's own pardon on the desk. The Cardinal burns it and writes a blank lieutenant's commission. The three refuse it, Athos writes in the boy's name, and the boy lights Constance's lamp himself.

I could paraphrase the book after one read. That is a real achievement. These are the places I stopped, reread or guessed:

- **pp 2–3, and on through the book:** which blue cloak is Athos, which is Porthos, which is Aramis? The names arrive in prose. The faces are never tied to them in rendered text. Aramis is not called by name in a balloon until p55.
- **pp 4, 12, 13, 52:** who is the scarred man? His name first renders on p54, in someone else's mouth.
- **p7 to p8:** "I'm going away tonight." She leaves at dawn.
- **p10 to p11:** a tall man in a blue cassock is watching Milady's house. Then Athos needs her described and doesn't know her name. Did he see her or not?
- **p13:** "You're his." Whose? The Cardinal's, or the man standing right there?
- **p17:** "She swore you'd die for seeing it." The boy never told Athos that.
- **p20:** "I'll write to Constance tonight." He doesn't know where she is.
- **p22:** "the Queen's letters go to the King." So the Cardinal has the letters? On p4 he wanted Constance because she carries them. Who is "the Duke"?
- **p24:** why does Athos let her go when he has just heard her ask for the girl's life?
- **p26:** "the first patrol shoots you as a deserter." The boy isn't a soldier. On p18 he came "because nobody told him he couldn't," and on p21 "not a Musketeer, was not allowed."
- **pp 30–33:** why is the boy grinning and laughing while Constance is a day from death?
- **p34:** how does the Cardinal know they will ride for Béthune?
- **p37:** Milady's smile drops at "a yellow horse," but she has known that horse since p5.
- **p38:** "he can follow us into the sea"?
- **p51:** "Athos had never once meant it." Meant "Good"? Every time? I went back to p3 and p30 and still wasn't sure.
- **pp 53–54:** why exactly does the paper beat the Cardinal? Why does he say "men who can't be bought" about a boy who was never offered anything? And what did the boy do that "men have gone to prison for"? Milady cut her own rope.

## Is it alive?

Mostly yes, and in places it is very good. Several movements are held back by gaps rather than by dead register.

**Movement I (pp 1–9).** Alive. Milady stepping out of the Cardinal's shadow, and "Boys tell me everything," is a strong entrance. Showing the plan to the reader before the rescue is the right trade of surprise for dread. The front-door motif ("Not by the kitchen door. By the front door.") is the best idea in the book, and it pays off on p56. Constance's "What lady?" is real jealousy hidden under a practical question, and "I can promise you two of those" is the boy's voice exactly. One weakness: on p9 he hands Milady the convent less than a page after Constance warned him about her by name, and nothing on the page lets him hear the warning and wave it away. The mandate asks for a reason the reader respects. The front door is that reason, and one beat would make it visible (see observations).

**Movement II (pp 10–18).** The best stretch. "Describe her." / "Her name." / "I don't know that name." works as subtext: he is lying, or hoping. "Which shoulder?" / "Then she isn't dead." gave me a chill. P16 earns its prose. The window jump (p14) is funny and fast. The surplus watcher on p10 muddies the Athos reveal it should be building (finding 6).

**Movement III (pp 19–25).** Strong scenes. "I have never in my life sent anyone wine. I drink it." and "We were both misinformed." are the two best lines in the book. But the movement ends on a hole that a ten-year-old will shout at the page. Athos had her at the point of his sword, had just heard her ask for Constance's life, and let her walk to her carriage. Nobody, Athos included, ever says a word about that choice. It is the most consequential decision any character makes in the volume, and it has no aftermath (finding 11).

**Movement IV (pp 26–35).** Competent and fun, and I felt it lose heat. The boy whose girl is about to be murdered is grinning (p30), laughing (p33), and crowing "Now that's something nobody can forget!" about his own cloak. The bastion is a delight in a vacuum, but his appetite has been swapped for a different one with no comment. "One minute more, for good manners" should be agony for him, and it plays as charm (finding 12). Porthos carries this movement ("That was good grass." / "I haven't finished my chicken.").

**Movement V (pp 36–44).** It hurts, which is the point. "You came. You promised, and you came." and "The roses smelled of nothing at all." are earned. P37's turn is hollow because Milady learns nothing she didn't know (finding 14). Athos absolving the boy on p44 rings false next to his own silence about the inn (finding 11).

**Movement VI (pp 45–51).** The chain of four men in the flood is the image of the book. The mandate's climax works: the trial on the bank, Athos's "That one is mine to forgive," and her refusal. P51's final Athos line asks the reader to solve it (finding 11).

**Movement VII (pp 52–57).** The ending lands emotionally: the blank commission, three refusals, the lamp. Mechanically, the confrontation with the Cardinal is the reader's puzzle. The boy, who has been carried by Athos's decisions for most of the book, is again handed the decisive tool by Athos ("Take this with you"). His only act is laying it down, and the script never makes that a choice (finding 16).

**Voice check (attribution strip).** D'Artagnan, Porthos, Athos, Milady and Constance pass: I could cover their names and still know them. Rochefort mostly passes. **Aramis fails.** Without "in theory" and "as the Fathers tell us," his lines are the book's explanations ("Every letter out of this camp crosses the Cardinal's desk"; "Whoever carries that can do anything at all…"). The tic is pasted on even where it means nothing (p17). See finding 2.

## Mandatory findings

**1. pp 2–3, carried to p55. The three Musketeers are never tied to their faces in rendered text.**
P2's prose names "Athos, Porthos and Aramis," but nothing says which face is which. On p3 nobody addresses anybody by name. Rendered text first connects a name to a face at p15 ("Athos, why does that—"), Porthos at p26 ("Porthos. Are you hungry?"), and Aramis in a balloon only at p55 ("Aramis?"). The p21 and p43 prose names them but doesn't place them. The reader has to guess from the speaker tags they can't see. That is the "solve the adaptation" failure, and in a sequel it is the cheapest one to fix.
**Repair burden:** for fifteen pages the reader can't tell whether the quiet one or the huge one is Athos.
**Prescription:** on p3, have the boy address each friend by name as he goes: lunging "Aramis, you're not even trying!", then turning to "Porthos" for the cloak question, then to "Athos" for the patience exchange. Or add a short caption to p1 that names each figure by a visible trait ("Athos, the tall still one; Porthos, the huge one with the apple; Aramis, the neat one…").

**2. pp 3, 11, 17, 20, 26, 29, 30. Aramis is a verbal tic wrapped around the book's exposition. Voice, want-from and aphorism are all blocking.**
Attribution strip: with "in theory" and "as the Fathers tell us" removed, his lines are "Every letter out of this camp crosses the Cardinal's desk. You'd be writing her address for him" (p20), "Some of them are paid" (p26), and "Whoever carries that can do anything at all, and no court in France can touch them" (p29). Any character could say them. They are information, not want. On p17, "And, as the Fathers tell us, everyone he owns" makes no sense: no Father teaches that. His ledger want (to be believed about the Church) surfaces only as the p55 joke, so the payoff has no setup.
**Repair burden:** the reader meets three tics and an explainer, not a person, and the p43 prayer arrives without a man behind it.
**Prescription:** give Aramis one want-from beat early. P11 is the natural place: his "Ladies on the Place Royale…" line should come from personal, rueful experience of grand ladies, and the boy should hit back at it. Make the tic mean something every time. Cut it from p17 (give him a real line about the war, e.g. the dry wish that the Cardinal would stay in Paris). Let at least one of his explanatory lines (p20 or p29) come from his own fear or slyness rather than from the writer's need to inform.

**3. pp 4, 5, 12, 13, 34, 38, 52. Rochefort is never named until p54.**
The scarred man carries plot on seven pages: the "four times" count, the balcony, the overheard report, the door, the hill, the north ride, the arrest. His name first renders in the Cardinal's line on p54 ("Rochefort tells me…"). On p4 the reader has a scar and "Meung, the Carmelites, the alley, Calais," and no name or role. The red feather (p5, p35) is a second identifier the text never ties to the scar.
**Repair burden:** the reader must decide whether the laughing man on the p5 balcony, the man with Milady on p12, and the rider on p35 are one person.
**Prescription:** p4's caption or the Cardinal's first line names him and his job: "Rochefort, the Cardinal's man, who had tried to stop the boy four times and failed four times." On p5, the caption or the staging says that the man watching from the balcony is Rochefort.

**4. pp 4 and 22. The Cardinal's want and the "Queen's letters" don't fit together, and "the Duke" is never identified.**
On p4 he wants Constance because she "carries the Queen's letters": "I want her." On p22 he threatens the Duke with "the Queen's letters go to the King," as if he holds them, and in the same breath hands Constance's life to Milady as her fee. "The Duke" (pp 9 and 22) is never said to be anyone, and the London errand goes nowhere except to point Milady north. The mandate requires the Cardinal's politics to be "felt, never explained." These lines are neither felt nor explained. They are noise the reader has to sort out.
**Repair burden:** does he have the letters or not? Why give away the girl he wanted? Which Duke?
**Prescription:** cut the letters/Duke clause on p22. Give Milady a one-clause errand that needs no explaining and sends her north toward the sea, e.g. "You'll take ship for England from the north coast within the week. What you do on the road is your own affair." Make p4's want consistent with handing Constance away later: he wants to know where the Queen hid her messenger and wants the boy out of his way. By p22 she is no longer worth anything to him, which is what makes the trade cold. One line from the Cardinal can show that ("The girl is no use to me now. Take her.").

**5. pp 7–8. Time continuity: "tonight" versus dawn.**
On p7 Constance says "I'm going away tonight," and the turn line agrees ("she is leaving tonight"). On p8 she leaves at "Dawn, the street blue and empty."
**Repair burden:** did something delay her? Is this a different departure?
**Prescription:** change p7 to "I'm going away at first light. The Queen is sending me." and fix the p7 turn line to match.

**6. pp 10–11. The watcher in the blue cassock contradicts the Athos reveal.**
P10 stages "a tall man in a blue cassock" watching Milady's door, and the turn line says "one of them has been watching the house." The tall one is Athos. If Athos has watched her house, he has seen her. Yet on p11 he needs her described, and on p15 "Then she isn't dead" lands as a discovery. If he saw her, his surprise is false. If it isn't Athos, the reader can't tell who it is, and the thread is never paid off.
**Repair burden:** the reader either distrusts the best reveal in the book or chases an unpaid clue.
**Prescription:** cut the watcher from p10, panel 1, and change p10's turn line to the empty chair and the friends who have stopped looking at the door. Athos's suspicion should be born on p11 from the description, which is stronger.

**7. p13. "You're his." has no single antecedent.**
"You're his. You were his from the river road." Rochefort is in the room. The Cardinal was last mentioned as "His Eminence" two lines earlier, behind a curtain. Read aloud, a listener asks "Whose?", and a child may take "his" as Rochefort's.
**Repair burden:** the accusation that ends the romance is ambiguous.
**Prescription:** "You're the Cardinal's. You were the Cardinal's from the river road. The horses, the front door, all of it."

**8. pp 15/17, 34, 53. Information Athos never received, plus three unfixed mechanical blockers.**
(a) On p17 Athos says "She swore you'd die for seeing it." The oath was made on p13. On p15 the boy reports her allegiance and the lily but not the oath. Athos is acting on information the reader never saw him get.
(b) `audit-r01.txt` lists three balloons over 24 words that were supposed to be fixed before this gate: p17 ATHOS (26), p34 CARDINAL (26), p53 CARDINAL (25).
**Prescription:** (a) on p15, panel 2, add the oath to the boy's report: "And she said nobody who's seen it stays alive. She meant me." (b) Split each over-length balloon into two balloons on the same panel, or move half to the next panel. Never trim the causal clauses: p17 keeps "eat only what we eat," p34 keeps "Go north tonight," and p53 keeps "One rode a yellow horse."

**9. pp 18, 21, 26, 35. The boy's status in the army is undefined, and p26's central argument depends on it.**
P18: he went "because nobody told him he couldn't." P21: "who was not a Musketeer, was not allowed to come." P26, Athos: "Leave the army without leave, and the first patrol shoots you as a deserter." You can't desert an army you never joined. P35 then has the King granting "the four of them" leave. The whole bastion plan rests on a rule that, as written, doesn't bind the boy.
**Repair burden:** the reader must invent the boy's enlistment, or decide that Athos's plan is unnecessary for him.
**Prescription:** fix his status once, early, in plain words. For example, p18's prose: "d'Artagnan went too, as a volunteer with the King's Guards, because it was the only way to march beside them, and a volunteer who walks away from a siege is a deserter like anybody else." Keep p21's "not allowed to come" consistent with that. On p26, Athos's line can then name both risks: "Leave without the King's leave and they shoot you, and they shoot the three of us for following."

**10. pp 18 and 20. Athos hides Milady's carriage; the boy proposes to write to an address he doesn't have.**
(a) P18: "d'Artagnan never saw it. Athos did." Athos says nothing until after the poison (p20, "There's a black carriage…"). The man who on p17 ordered "drink what we drink" withholds the one fact that would have made the boy suspicious of the bottle, and no reason is given.
(b) P20: "Then I'll write to Constance tonight and warn her." / "You'd be writing her address for him." The boy doesn't know her address; that was the whole point of p7.
**Repair burden:** (a) why didn't Athos warn him? (b) what address?
**Prescription:** (a) give Athos a reason on p20 and let the boy throw it at him: "You saw her carriage and didn't tell me?" / "I wasn't sure it was hers. I'm sure now." That is heat, and it plants Athos's pattern of saying too little, which finding 11 needs. (b) Rewrite p20, panel 3: "Then I'll write to the Queen and beg her to move Constance." / Aramis: "Every letter out of this camp crosses the Cardinal's desk. You'd be telling him there's still something to find."

**11. pp 24, 44, 51. Athos's most consequential choice has no aftermath, and his last beat is a puzzle.**
On p24 Athos has just heard Milady ask for "the Gascon. And the girl he loves." He has his sword point on her table, and he lets her go with a warning. That choice kills Constance. Nobody mentions it again. On p44 Athos tells the boy "You didn't pour the wine," absolving him, with no word about his own part, and the boy never asks the question every reader is asking. On p51: "When he came back he said only, 'Good.' And for the first time, the boy understood that Athos had never once meant it." "Good" has been used twice in rendered text (p3, p30), neither time noticeably as a mask. The ledger's "says 'good' when things are bad" lives only in the docs.
**Repair burden:** the reader must supply why Athos let her go, decide whether he feels responsible, and rebuild a motif from two throwaway lines.
**Prescription:**
- On p24, make the reason his, and audible. He cannot do it again, alone, in the dark ("I judged you once, alone, in an hour. I won't do it twice."). That plants the p46 "Not like this" and the p49 trial.
- On p44 (or a split p44/p44b), let the boy ask, or let Athos say first: "I had her at the end of my sword two weeks ago, and I let her go. If you want someone to blame, it's me." The boy's answer is the beat that tells us what he now believes about his friend.
- Seed "Good" deliberately at least once more as a visibly hollow word, for example on p20 over the blackened grass, so that p51 is recognition rather than riddle. Then give p51 one plain clause: "…understood that every time Athos said 'Good', something in him was breaking."

**12. pp 26–33. During the bastion the protagonist's appetite is written out (blocking under §8b, appetite and heat).**
On p25 the boy is "buckling on his sword before he had finished," with Constance a day from Milady. For the next eight pages he is grinning (p30), laughing (p33), and crowing "Now that's something nobody can forget!" (p33) about his cloak. Athos's "And one minute more, for good manners" passes without protest. The danger to Constance isn't mentioned once between p27 and p34. The stated reason for privacy ("A place where nobody can listen," p26) yields no plan on p29: they read the paper and decide nothing.
**Repair burden:** the reader must decide that the boy has forgotten Constance, or must supply his feelings for him.
**Prescription:** keep the bastion. It is good spectacle, and leave from the King is a real reason. Thread the boy's urgency through it:
- On p29, he counts minutes and wants to leave at once; Athos holds him with a hand on his arm.
- On p30, "Fifteen minutes gone" should be said in agony, not glee.
- On p33, Athos's extra minute should be a fight: "One more minute? She's a day closer!" / "Then it's one minute the army won't forget."
- Replace "Now that's something nobody can forget!" with a line about Béthune; give the cloak joke to Porthos.
- Use the private talk on p29 to decide something: that they keep the paper as their one weapon against the Cardinal afterwards. This sets up p52–53 and closes finding 16's gap from the other side.

**13. p34. The Cardinal acts on knowledge he never got.**
"The King will give them leave, and they'll ride for Béthune." The Cardinal has no way to know the four know about Béthune. The stovepipe was secret, and Athos "followed" later.
**Repair burden:** the reader must assume a messenger, or that the Cardinal is omniscient.
**Prescription:** let him deduce it on the page, which also shows his intelligence. Rochefort: "One of the three stayed behind at the Red Dovecote last night, Eminence. The tall one." Cardinal: "Then he heard me name Béthune." Split the 26-word balloon as in finding 8.

**14. p37. A false turn: Milady learns nothing.**
The panel 3 beat ("the smile gone") and the turn line ("Milady knows exactly who is coming, and Constance has just told her how to recognise him") rest on "A Gascon. With a yellow horse." Milady stopped that yellow horse on p5, entertained the boy for weeks, and asked the Cardinal for "the Gascon. And the girl he loves." She came to Béthune for exactly this girl.
**Repair burden:** the reader must either ignore the contradiction or decide that Milady forgot.
**Prescription:** give the beat a genuinely new fact for her. One option: Constance says he is coming now, "The Queen wrote: he'll be here within the week, with his three friends." Milady's smile drops because the clock has started, and her plan changes on the page. Another: Constance repeats the warning she gave the boy about "ladies who are kind for no reason," and Milady realises the girl suspected her from the start. The ledger calls Constance "the one person who suspected Milady," and the script never uses it. Rewrite the p37 turn line to match whichever you choose.

**15. p38. "He can follow us into the sea" doesn't land.**
Read aloud, a listener asks "Follow you where? Into the sea how?" This line is Milady's plan for the climax movement, and it is a riddle.
**Prescription:** "Then bring my carriage to this gate at first light. I'll take the girl to the coast, and if he wants her back he can follow me onto the boat to England." Keep "One way or another" on panel 3. It works.

**16. pp 52–54. The paper's leverage, the charge, and the boy's choice are all left to the reader (enacted, not narrated; complete causal chain; protagonist agency).**
- On p53 the Cardinal implies that the four killed Milady ("Men have gone to prison for much less than you did"). The boy doesn't say the one true thing a child is shouting: *she cut her own rope, and Athos held out his hand.*
- The paper's power is stated once, by Aramis, 24 pages earlier (p29). On p53 the boy says only "You wrote it." The reader must reconstruct: it says the bearer acted on the Cardinal's order, so arresting the bearer means admitting he licensed a poisoner.
- On p54, "Men who can't be bought are rare" refers to nothing on the page. The boy was never offered, and never refused, anything. The ledger's "a servant who cannot be bought" exists only in the docs.
- The decisive object is handed to the boy by Athos (p52). Across the whole book, the boy's own choices are almost all mistakes or reflexes; Athos makes every decision. This is the scene where the protagonist should choose.

**Prescription:** let the boy say what happened at the ferry, then make the paper both his weapon and his sacrifice. For example, on p53: "We didn't touch her. She cut her own rope and turned her back on Athos's hand." / "Who will believe four men in the dark?" Then he lays it down: "This says whoever carries it did what he did on your orders. I could keep it, and do anything I liked for the rest of my life. I don't want anything you've signed." He pushes it across, unasked. Now "Men who can't be bought" on p54 answers something we saw, and p55's "You did something nobody in France can forget" points at that act. Split p53 or p54 if the text needs the room; never compress.

## Architecture and balance

- Modes: 36 dramatic / 12 prose / 9 spectacle, which is 63 / 21 / 16 over 57 story pages. That is within spirit; spectacle runs slightly high but every spectacle page carries a real event.
- Every page declares one mode, one dominant panel, and at most two locations. Every page has a turn line. There are no Chronicle pages, which is correct per the mandate.
- No text-budget blockers beyond the three over-length balloons in finding 8.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p5:** the caption ("A hundred people watched. One boy… moved.") pre-empts Milady's best line on p6 ("A hundred men watched, and you were the only one who moved."). Give the caption a different idea, or let her line be the first time we hear it. Also, nothing shows how Rochefort "sees that he's there." Half a line would cover it, e.g. the boy sent on a false errand down the river road.
- **p9:** the boy gives up the convent less than a page after Constance warned him by name. One flicker would make him respected rather than foolish, which is the mandate's second failure to avoid. A beat where he hesitates, and Milady's front-door warmth wins it.
- **p12:** Milady is reporting first-day information weeks later. A clause such as "I waited until I was sure he hadn't lied" makes her patient rather than slow.
- **p17:** this page carries two turns, the confession confirmed and the war. It is acceptable as a hook ending, but consider moving the war announcement to open p18.
- **p21:** the Cardinal commandeering the patrol is narrated. It is a good dramatic beat in miniature; acceptable as transition.
- **p27:** the army's bet money is never collected. A one-clause payoff on p35 would be fun for these readers.
- **p36:** dramatic irony is left on the table. Constance told the boy "Be careful of ladies who are kind for no reason," and the prose calls Milady "the kindest person Constance had met." Let the prose or p37 echo her own words.
- **p47:** panel 1 staging says "The four in a row" but lists three. Align before packets.
- **p49:** "Let go of his belt, boy. He'd drown for me." Her intent (to take Athos with her) is readable, but one more clause would help: "Let go, boy, and he'll come to me. He'd drown for me."
- **p55:** "You did something nobody in France can forget." The referent is unclear. Resolved if finding 16 makes the p53 act a choice.
- **p57:** the Cardinal at a street window versus his windowless study (p4) is fine, but keep the cat and posture identical so it reads as the same man.
- **`book.toml`** says 52 pages and the script has 57. Update the toml per the audit warning.
