## Page intent

## PAGE 6

Dramatic. The carriage stopped and the horses blowing, the boy still holding the bridle, hair in his eyes, joking about horses trying to swim to the sea. The dominant moment belongs to Milady stepping down, pale gold ringlets and plum silk, looking at him as if he were the only person on the road; she praises him for being the only one who moved and gives him her name, and he gives his. He has forgotten to let go of the bridle. Then she takes his hand off it and invites him to come on Thursday, by the front door. The one dominant turn: Milady thanks him and asks him in by the front door. The warmth is real on his side and staged on hers. The reader turns because nobody in Paris has ever asked him in by the front door.

## Exact owner-controlled script

## PAGE 6 · *dramatic*

**Location:** the river road in Paris.

**Panel 1 — 30%.** The carriage stopped, the horses blowing; D'ARTAGNAN,
left, still holding the bridle, hair in his eyes, breathing hard. The
carriage door, right, opening; a gloved hand on it.

> **D'ARTAGNAN:** "Are you hurt, Madame? Your horses were trying to swim to the sea."

**Panel 2 — dominant, 50%.** MILADY stepping down, pale gold ringlets, plum
silk, looking at him as if he were the only person on the road. He has
forgotten to let go of the bridle.

> **MILADY:** "You stopped them. A hundred men watched, and you were the only one who moved."
>
> **MILADY:** "I'm Lady de Winter. In Paris they call me Milady — it's English, for 'my lady'. And you are?"
>
> **D'ARTAGNAN:** "d'Artagnan. From Gascony."

**Panel 3 — 20%.** She takes his hand off the bridle and holds it a moment.

> **MILADY:** "Come and see me on Thursday, Monsieur d'Artagnan. Not by the kitchen door. By the front door."

**Why you turn the page:** nobody in Paris has ever asked him in by the front door.

## Numbered blocking criteria

## Page 6 — card

### C1 — Dialogue transcription or ownership fails
All five strings must read exactly from the 600 × 900 proof, once, in order, each hanging from its own speaker's mouth by tail and placement, with no extra text; a string that does not read is blocking, and a string that reads is not to be measured. Nonblocking: spacing around a dash, curly against straight quotes or apostrophes (including the single quotes round 'my lady'), a hyphen for a dash, a comma present or missing inside a line, a regional spelling variant of the same word, and the run length of a repeated letter in a sound cue; the words and their order are what must match. A line placed directly under its speaker's previous balloon, or naming its addressee, is owned even if its tail wanders.

### C2 — Attribution of the introductions
The boy's "Are you hurt"; Milady's two lines, praise then her name and "And you are?"; the boy's "d'Artagnan. From Gascony."; then her invitation. Her name given from the boy's mouth, or his name from hers, is blocking because the reader would not know who is who.

### C3 — The turn lands by eye
Milady stepping down and looking at the boy must own the page, and the last moment must show her taking his hand from the bridle; the reader must feel the invitation. If she reads as a minor figure beside the carriage, it is blocking.

### C4 — Identity and continuity
Milady tall, very pale, pale gold ringlets past the shoulder, plum silk; the boy beardless, black-haired, in ochre with no blue. The black carriage with plum curtains matches the runaway coach. Milady in widow's grey, or the boy in blue, is blocking. Whether his hat is on is nonblocking.

### C5 — Generation integrity
In the hand-taking moment, exactly two hands, hers and his, read cleanly; a fused or extra hand there is blocking. Background bystanders are not.
