## Blind read
At grey dawn, four exhausted companions sit on the bank of a calm brown river with their horses behind them, including a yellow horse; the slack ferry rope and empty water make the loss tangible. The dominant wide image owns the page, with d’Artagnan as the prose viewpoint and Athos carrying the final emotional weight. D’Artagnan’s expected enormous feeling never comes: he remains cold, tired, and wishing Constance were alive; she has been buried, and the turn arrives when he understands that Athos’s solitary “Good.” is the only way Athos can bear what has happened. The smaller final image leaves Athos alone at the water’s edge, looking downstream.

In the morning the river was brown and quiet and empty. The ferry rope hung down into the water, and there was nothing on the end of it.

d’Artagnan had thought that when it was over he would feel something enormous. He felt cold, and very tired, and he wanted Constance to be alive, and none of that had changed.

They buried her at Béthune, in the convent garden, under the roses.

Athos stood a long time by the river. When he came back he said only, ‘Good.’ He always said it when things were bad; and for the first time the boy understood that it was the only way Athos could bear them.
