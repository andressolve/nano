# Page intents

Reader-facing goals, not image-generation specifications. Shown to both
builder and critic. Exact text and staging stay in the script and the prompt.

Format — one section per page, header exactly `## PAGE N`:

## PAGE 1

Spectacle. The book opens wide on a summer morning in Paris: a stone bridge lined with tall houses, the river shining below, and four friends walking straight at the reader, four abreast. Three of them wear the Musketeer's blue cassock with the white cross: the tall still one, the huge one eating an apple, the slim fair one. The fourth, a head shorter, wears a faded ochre doublet and a grey hat with a broken feather and leads an old buttercup-yellow horse; he is talking, and the others are smiling and not listening. The one dominant turn: four friends cross Paris, and only three wear the blue cloak. The title card owns the sky and the caption names the difference. The reader turns because one of the four has no blue cloak, and wants to know why.

## PAGE 2

Illustrated prose. Night in the attic under the roof: the boy at the open window in his shirt sleeves, a small ring on his right hand, looking down the steep stair to the warm lamp at the door below, where the girl lives. A smaller memory panel shows four sword points meeting over a table. The prose owns the page and carries the one dominant turn: who the boy is, a Gascon of eighteen, the oath of the four, the Queen's ring, Constance's smile, and the Cardinal who read his name off a letter. The picture stays quiet and candlelit; nobody speaks. What changes is that the reader now knows the boy and the one man who remembers him. The reader turns because the most powerful man in France remembers his name.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. Late afternoon in the long grass by the high yellow wall: the boy lunging at Aramis with far too much energy while Aramis parries one-handed and bored, Porthos eating a chicken leg on the trough, Athos standing apart and not moving, the yellow horse drinking. The boy owns the page. He complains that nobody has tried to kill him in a month, demands to know when he gets a blue cloak, and hears from Porthos that the King gives it for something nobody in France can forget. Athos's two words close it. The one dominant turn: the boy wants the blue cloak, given for something nobody in France can forget. The mood stays light and teasing. The reader turns because the boy is impatient for something unforgettable, and something is coming for him.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. Across the river, in the Cardinal's red study with no window and one candle, the Cardinal strokes his grey cat while Rochefort, in black, hat in hand, begs for ten men after four failures. The Cardinal refuses: he will try something else. Then the dominant moment: a tall woman steps out of the shadow of the hangings into the candlelight, pale gold ringlets and deep plum silk, turning a dark-stoned ring with her thumb, and Rochefort stiffens. She is Milady; she promises the boy in a week, and with a smile tells Rochefort her horses will bolt tomorrow at noon. The one dominant turn: the Cardinal sets Milady on the boy, and she plans the runaway horses. The reader turns knowing what will happen at noon, while the boy does not.

## PAGE 5

Spectacle. Noon on the river road: a black carriage with plum curtains behind two black horses bolting, the crowd scattering, a fruit stall going over, and racing up alongside on the old yellow horse the boy, leaning right out of the saddle to grab the lead horse's bridle, his hat flying off. The rescue owns the page, all speed and dust. Small, up on a balcony, Rochefort in black watches and laughs through his nose. The caption tells the reader that the note was false and the horses were let go on purpose. The one dominant turn: the boy stops Milady's bolting carriage, exactly as she planned. The reader turns because the boy has done exactly what Milady wanted.

## PAGE 6

Dramatic. The carriage stopped and the horses blowing, the boy still holding the bridle, hair in his eyes, joking about horses trying to swim to the sea. The dominant moment belongs to Milady stepping down, pale gold ringlets and plum silk, looking at him as if he were the only person on the road; she praises him for being the only one who moved and gives him her name, and he gives his. He has forgotten to let go of the bridle. Then she takes his hand off it and invites him to come on Thursday, by the front door. The one dominant turn: Milady thanks him and asks him in by the front door. The warmth is real on his side and staged on hers. The reader turns because nobody in Paris has ever asked him in by the front door.

## PAGE 7

Dramatic. Night at Constance's street door under the lantern. The boy bounds up grinning with his news of a great lady; Constance, white-faced in the doorway with a lamp, a packed bundle behind her, tells him she leaves at first light. The dominant moment is at the foot of the steep stair, Constance holding both his hands: the Queen is hiding her in a convent somewhere in the north, and she will not tell him which, so nobody can make him tell. Then she turns sharp and asks what lady, and warns him about ladies who are kind for no reason. The one dominant turn: Constance must hide in a convent in the north, and warns him about kind ladies. The reader turns because she is leaving at first light, and she has warned him.

## PAGE 8

Dramatic. Dawn, the street blue and empty, a plain coach at the door. The dominant moment is Constance in her brown travelling cloak with her hand on the boy's cheek while he tries not to look as if he minds; she asks for three promises and he jokes that he can keep two. From the coach window as it starts to move she makes him promise to come for her only when the Queen says she is safe, not one day before, and he promises. Then the coach goes down the empty street and the boy stands alone in the doorway watching. The one dominant turn: Constance leaves at dawn, and he promises to come only when the Queen says she is safe. The reader turns because she is gone, and on Thursday there is a front door.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic. On Thursday a footman bows the patched ochre boy in at Milady's tall front door. The dominant moment is her salon of gold candlelight and plum hangings: Milady on the sofa pouring wine, the boy perched on the edge of a gilt chair with his hat on his knee, dazzled, boasting that it was him, well, four of them, who saved the Queen. Then, close, her thumb turns the dark ring and her eyes wait over the glass; she asks whether there is a girl, and he hesitates and then tells her: a convent in the north. She smiles. The one dominant turn: in the gold salon the boy tells Milady the girl is in a convent in the north. The reader turns because he has just told her half of what the Cardinal wants, and she is smiling.

## PAGE 10

Illustrated prose. Time passes in one page: the boy goes back to Milady's house every day, and the prose tells what he gave her — his stories, his father, his sword, his horse, his three friends — because nobody grand had ever wanted to hear them. The dominant image is the boy at evening running up the steps of her tall house with a bunch of flowers while the footman is already opening the door: he is happy, and he is let in. Below, smaller and quieter, the tavern table where three Musketeers sit beside an empty fourth chair and no longer look at the door. The dominant turn is that he goes back every day, and at the tavern there is an empty chair. The reader turns because his friends have stopped waiting for him. The footman is scenery; nobody speaks.

## PAGE 11

Dramatic. The tavern. The boy bursts in late and flushed and drops into the empty chair; Porthos booms that the great lady let him out, and the boy defends her. Aramis, two fingers at his moustache, says a great lady's front door is why he is going into the Church; the boy answers that this is different — she listens to him. Then the page belongs to Athos, who has not spoken: he sets his glass down very carefully and does not pick it up again, and asks the boy to describe her, and then her name. Tall, hair almost silver, eyes like ice water, a smile that comes before she means it; Lady de Winter. Athos says he does not know that name. The dominant turn is Athos asking for the description and going still. The reader turns because he has not touched his wine. Porthos and Aramis are subordinate.

## PAGE 12

Dramatic. Milady's gold salon, afternoon. The boy comes early with flowers and finds the room empty, and voices behind a half-drawn plum curtain. Through the gap he sees Milady at a small writing table and Rochefort, the man with the scar, standing over her. She reports: the girl is in a convent in the north, the boy told her the first day, and His Eminence can find out which one. Rochefort laughs through his nose and asks about the boy; she says that when she has finished with him he is Rochefort's. In the foreground the boy, half hidden, frozen, lets the flowers sink. The dominant turn is the boy overhearing that he told her the first day: every kindness was work. Milady owns the page; the boy's frozen back is its witness. The reader turns because he has heard all of it and is about to be seen.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Milady's salon. The flowers fall; the boy steps through the curtain with his sword half drawn and says it to her face: she has been the Cardinal's from the river road. Milady flies at him with a small knife from the writing table; he catches her wrist, and in the struggle her left sleeve tears open at the shoulder seam. On the back of her bare left shoulder is a small dark lily, burned into the skin. He sees it, and she sees him see it — that look is the page. Then she clutches the torn sleeve, white with fury, and swears that nobody who has seen it stays alive; behind her Rochefort holds the salon door with his sword out. The dominant turn is the lily revealed and her vow. Nobody is hurt. The reader turns because the door is taken and only the window is left.

## PAGE 14

Spectacle. One image. The boy comes out of Milady's tall first-floor window in a tangle of plum curtain, in mid-air above the evening square, hat flying, sword in his fist, falling toward a hay cart passing underneath while the carter gapes up. At the window above, Rochefort leans out, and behind him a pale gold head. One caption at the foot of the page makes the joke: Gascon boys are never taught to run, and nobody mentioned windows. The dominant turn is the escape through the window into the hay. It is a burst of comedy and speed after the danger; the boy owns the page, and the faces at the window are small. The reader turns because he got out, and now he knows what she is, and so does she.

## PAGE 15

Dramatic. Athos's bare room at night, one candle and a glass. The boy, hay still in his hair, bursts in gasping: she is the Cardinal's, and he told her about Constance. Athos only says to sit. The boy paces instead, his own hand on his own left shoulder, and tells him the rest: a mark burned into her shoulder, the kind they brand on a thief, a lily, and her vow. Then the page belongs to Athos, for once not quite still: his hand flat on the table beside the glass, his face gone grey in the candlelight. He asks which shoulder. The left. Then she isn't dead. The dominant turn is that question and that answer. The reader turns because Athos knows this woman and is about to say how. The boy's alarm is subordinate to Athos's stillness breaking.

## PAGE 16

Illustrated prose. Athos tells the story, as a friend's. The dominant image is a memory in faded dusk colours: a young lord in black and silver on a grey horse, small and seen from behind, riding away from the edge of a dark wood, with one bare branch high in the foreground against the sky. The prose carries the whole tale — the count, the beautiful girl from nowhere, the lily seen when her sleeve tore, the judgment made alone in one hour, the cut rope and the empty wood, and the name he gave up. Below, in the candlelit room, Athos stares at nothing while the boy sits on the floor against the wall, listening. The dominant turn is the count who judged his branded wife, and the cut rope. The reader turns because the boy must say aloud who the friend was. Nothing cruel is shown; the memory is a rider leaving.

## PAGE 17

Dramatic. Athos's room. The boy gets slowly to his feet and says it: the friend was Athos. Athos answers that the count is dead. Then the page belongs to Athos acting: standing, he takes his sword down from the whitewashed wall and lays down the rule — she keeps that kind of promise, so from tonight the boy eats and drinks only what they do. The boy asks about Constance; Athos says the Cardinal will find out which convent, and so must they. The door bangs open: Porthos and Aramis, excited, a drummer in the street behind them — war, the King marches on La Rochelle tomorrow, and the Cardinal goes too. The dominant turn is Athos taking up the sword and the rule. The reader turns because everyone is going to war, including her.

## PAGE 18

Illustrated prose. The army goes to war. The dominant image is the march on a dusty road: columns of soldiers and blue cassocks, the boy in ochre on the yellow horse among them, and far behind, after a column of red-cloaked guards, a black carriage with its plum curtains drawn. Athos, riding, has turned in his saddle to look back at it. Below, the destination: La Rochelle, a walled city on its harbour with the grey sea beyond, ringed by the King's trenches. The prose explains the siege, why the boy marches as a volunteer, and why the Cardinal goes — and ends on the carriage the boy never saw and Athos did. The dominant turn is that Milady's carriage goes with the army. The reader turns because she is in the camp, and the boy doesn't know.

## PAGE 19

A dramatic page in the camp before La Rochelle, morning. A camp boy brings d'Artagnan a dark green bottle with a paper tied round its neck, and he reads it aloud, delighted: wine from his three friends. The dominant turn: a poisoned bottle "from your friends", and Athos stops the boy drinking. The largest moment is the cork out and the bottle at his lips while Athos strides up between the tents and brings his hand down over its mouth, Porthos and Aramis behind him. Then Athos holds the bottle away at arm's length and says the thing that chills the page: his name is on a bottle he never sent. The boy owns the delight; Athos owns the turn. The reader turns because if the wine is not from his friends, it is from someone who wants him dead.

## PAGE 20

A dramatic page in the camp. Athos tips the wine out onto the grass, and where it falls the grass goes black and curls; the four stand looking down at it, and Porthos says the only thing he can. The dominant turn: the grass goes black, Milady is in the camp, and no letter is safe. The blackened grass is the image that owns the page. Below it, Athos tells the boy what he saw, a black carriage with plum curtains at the back of the Cardinal's train; the boy rounds on him for keeping it back, and Athos answers that he is sure now. Then the boy wants to write to the Queen to move Constance, and Aramis, low and quiet, tells him every letter out of this camp is read by the Cardinal. The reader turns because Milady is trying to kill him, and they cannot even send a letter to save Constance.

## PAGE 21

An illustrated-prose page across two places: a dark heath at night, and the yard of a lonely inn. The dominant turn: the Cardinal makes the three escort him to the Red Dovecote. The largest image is the heath, where Athos, Porthos and Aramis on patrol are stopped by a cloaked rider and two red-cloaked guards, and a flash of red robe shows under the rider's cloak. Below, the inn with one lit window and a sign painted with a red dove, and in its yard a lady's carriage with its curtains drawn. The prose tells why d'Artagnan is not with them and gives the Cardinal's quiet orders. The riders and the carriage stay small and clear; the prose sits in the night sky. The reader turns because the Cardinal has come to meet someone, and the three are waiting right underneath.

## PAGE 22

A dramatic page in the Red Dovecote, in two rooms joined by one broken stovepipe. Downstairs by the hearth, Porthos and Aramis play dice while Athos stands at the wall with his ear to the pipe and tells them to be quiet. The dominant turn: through the stovepipe Athos hears Milady's price, the boy and the girl at Béthune. The largest image is the room above, by one candle: the Cardinal in a plain cloak over his red, writing at a small table, and Milady across from him in a grey travelling cloak. He sends her to England; she names her price; he gives her the girl and tells her where the girl is. Then, downstairs again, Athos's still face beside the pipe and Porthos's dice stopped in his fist. The reader turns because Athos now knows where Constance is, and so does Milady.

## PAGE 23

A dramatic page in the Red Dovecote, the room above and the room below. The dominant turn: the Cardinal gives Milady the paper, and Athos stays behind and goes up. The largest image is upstairs: the Cardinal folding a paper and handing it to Milady across the candle, telling her that whatever she does with it, she did by his order; she answers that then she shall do a great deal. Downstairs, the Cardinal comes down the stair with his hood up and says they ride; Porthos and Aramis rise, but Athos does not move from the wall and makes an excuse about his horse. Last, Athos alone at the foot of the dark stair, looking up at the line of candlelight under the door, his hand on his hilt. The reader turns because Athos is going up the stair to a woman he once hanged.

## PAGE 24

A dramatic page in the small room above the inn. Athos stands in the open doorway with his hat in his hand and asks Milady whether she knows him. The dominant turn: Athos and Milady face to face; he takes the paper and lets her go. The largest image is Milady rising with the candle between them and her face changing; it is the only time in the book she is afraid. She names him as a count she heard was dead, and he answers that he thought he had hanged her. Then his sword point rests on the table, never on her; he asks for the Cardinal's paper and she pushes it across, mocking him; he tells her he will not judge her alone twice, and warns her off the boy and the girl. The reader turns because Athos has the paper, and he has let Milady go.

## PAGE 25

An illustrated-prose page across two places: the heath at dawn, and a tent in the camp at first light. The dominant turn: her carriage turns north, and Béthune is on the road to the sea. The largest image is the dawn heath: a small black carriage racing away up a long straight road to the north, and Athos on his horse on a rise watching it go, the folded paper in his hand. Below, in the tent, Athos tells the boy alone what it means, and the boy is already on his feet, buckling on his sword. The prose, set in the dawn sky, joins the Cardinal's words to the road and the convent. The carriage stays small and far; Athos stays still. The reader turns because the boy is ready to ride, and nobody has said he can.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. First light through the canvas of Athos's tent. The boy stands at the flap, armed and shaking with anger: Athos had Milady at the point of his sword and let her ride away toward Constance. Athos, seated and still, answers that he once judged her alone, in an hour, without a question, and will not do it twice. The boy says he will go now; Athos tells him plainly that a man who leaves the siege without the King's leave is shot on the road, and the three who follow him too. The dominant turn: the boy rounds on Athos for letting her go, and learns that deserters are shot. He slams his sword home and demands leave today, unforgiving; Athos's "Good" accepts the hate. The reader turns because the boy is furious and there is still no way out of the camp. The tent, the stool and the morning light stay subordinate to the two faces.

## PAGE 27

Dramatic. Porthos and Aramis duck into the tent, having heard the shouting three tents away; Aramis warns, low and dry, that every tent in this camp has ears. Athos stands up at last and looks out through the flap at the city walls and, halfway to them, a ruined fort on the bare ground, the angry boy beside him. He needs a place nobody can listen and the King's leave, and he knows where to get both. Then he turns to Porthos and asks if he is hungry. The dominant turn: Athos's plan, a place nobody can hear and the King's leave, begins with breakfast. Athos standing and looking out owns the page; the ruined fort in the distance is the answer the reader sees before anyone names it. The reader turns on Porthos's "Always. Why?" The tent's furniture stays subordinate.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. Morning in the camp before La Rochelle. Porthos, enormous, climbs onto a barrel in the middle of the camp, arms wide, and shouts his bet to a crowd of soldiers: the four will eat breakfast in the bastion of Saint-Gervais, under the city's guns, for one whole hour. Who will bet against them? Coins rain into an upturned helmet; the whole army bets against them. Below, the boy sees the point (nobody can listen out there) and Athos adds the rest: everybody will watch, and the King rewards what the whole army saw. The dominant turn: Porthos bets the whole army they will breakfast in the bastion for an hour. Porthos on his barrel owns the page. The reader turns because four men are about to walk out to the enemy's walls with a basket. The crowd and the coins stay subordinate.

## PAGE 29

Spectacle. Seen from behind and above the trenches: the four walk out across broken, cratered ground in the bright morning toward a ruined fort, with the grey walls of the city beyond. Porthos carries a huge basket, Aramis a bottle under each arm, Athos a folded white tablecloth over his arm, the boy out in front. Behind them the whole army has climbed up along the trenches to watch. The dominant turn: the four walk out to the bastion with the whole army watching. One image, one caption; the absurd small picnic against the vast dangerous ground is the joke and the courage at once. The reader turns because they are about to sit down to breakfast in a fort the enemy wants back. Individual soldiers stay anonymous and subordinate.

## PAGE 30

Dramatic. Inside the ruined bastion, a white tablecloth over a fallen block of stone, bread, a roast chicken and wine; the four sit down and Porthos is already eating, while behind them a tall cracked wall leans outward. Athos unfolds the Cardinal's paper, and its words are shown large: whoever carries it did what was done by the Cardinal's order. Aramis sees what it means; the boy, half on his feet, wants to burn it and ride; Athos holds him down by the arm and decides to keep it, because when they catch the Cardinal's lady the paper will stop the Cardinal hanging them. The dominant turn: breakfast and the paper, which they will keep as their shield. The four at their absurd breakfast with the paper opening owns the page. The reader turns because the boy wants to run. The food stays subordinate.

## PAGE 31

Dramatic. Over the broken parapet, a dozen townsmen-soldiers climb the slope with a ladder. Aramis points at them with a chicken leg and observes that in theory this is where they leave; Porthos has not finished his chicken. Then the four fire from the parapet into a wall of white musket smoke, and beyond it the attackers drop their ladder and run back down the slope; nobody is hit. Back at the tablecloth, Porthos picks up his chicken, Athos sits down, and the boy stays at the parapet staring north, counting minutes while she rides. The dominant turn: the first attack is driven off, and the boy counts the minutes. The smoke and the running ladder-men own the page; the boy's face in the last panel gives it its ache. The reader turns because Athos says they will come back with more.

## PAGE 32

Dramatic. Down the slope a whole company is coming now, forty men with three ladders, steel caps glinting, and the boy at the parapet shouts it. Porthos answers that the wall is in their way and they should give it to them. Then Porthos sets his huge shoulder against the tall cracked wall that leans out over the slope, the boy and Aramis pushing beside him and Athos braced with his back against it, and the top stones begin to tilt outward with dust falling. The dominant turn: forty attackers come, and Porthos sets his shoulder to the cracked wall. The four straining together against the leaning wall, with "All for one — and push!", owns the page. The reader turns because the wall is going over. The approaching company is a small distant strip.

## PAGE 33

Spectacle. The great wall comes down outward across the slope in a roaring avalanche of stone and dust. The ladders vanish into it; far below, the attackers are all running back toward the city, and nobody is under the falling stone. On the broken top of the ruin, small against the sky, Porthos throws both arms in the air. The dominant turn: the wall comes down between them and the city, and nobody is under it. One full image, one sound and one caption; the scale of the collapse and the tiny triumphant Porthos carry it. The reader turns because the hour is not over yet. Individual attackers stay small, distant and unhurt.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. On top of the rubble Aramis ties his white lace handkerchief to a pike as a flag so the King can see whose bastion this is. Athos looks at the sun: that is the hour, and one minute more, for good manners. The boy rounds on him, desperate: she is half a day ahead. Athos answers that it will be a minute this army never forgets, and they go. They walk back across the open ground with the basket while the army stands along the trenches, hats in the air, and Porthos boasts of his wall while the boy already looks north to Béthune. The dominant turn: the hour and one minute, the handkerchief flying, the walk back. The flag on the rubble and the clash over the minute own the page. The reader turns because the whole army saw it, and so did someone on the hill.

## PAGE 35

Dramatic. On a hill behind the lines, the Cardinal sits a grey horse, his red showing under a dark cloak, with Rochefort beside him on a black horse; far below, four small figures walk into a cheering camp and a tiny white flag flies on the ruined bastion. Rochefort names them sourly. The Cardinal, quiet, works it out aloud: the tall one was at the Red Dovecote and heard him name Béthune; the King will give them leave and they will ride there. He would rather have the boy in his red than in the ground. Rochefort says the boy would sooner die. Then let Milady arrange it, the Cardinal says, turning his horse: ride north tonight and warn her that four men are coming. The dominant turn: the Cardinal deduces Béthune and sends Rochefort north to warn Milady. The two riders above the small distant triumph own the page.

## PAGE 36

Illustrated prose. Four riders on a long straight road through flat country in driving rain, poplars bending, a post inn behind them, the yellow horse in front. The King gave them leave that same afternoon; it is six days to Béthune; they change horses where they can, except the yellow one, which will not be changed and keeps up. The boy does not talk. He promised Constance he would not come before the Queen said she was safe, and he is coming anyway, doing the sum at every milestone: half a day was nothing, half a day was everything. Far ahead, small under a moon, a single rider in black with a red feather gallops north. The dominant turn: leave given, six days north, he is breaking his promise and she is half a day ahead. The riders in the rain own the page. The reader turns because somebody is ahead of them both.

## PAGE 37

An illustrated-prose page. The dominant turn: a kind widow comes to the convent, and Constance trusts her. The large picture shows the quiet cloister, Constance with her basket looking up at the gate as a nun lets in a tall lady in a plain dark widow's dress, pale gold hair under a pushed-back veil; below, the two women sit on a stone bench and the lady holds both of Constance's hands. The prose owns the reading and carries the irony: the reader recognises the widow at once, and Constance, who warned the boy about kind ladies, never asks why. The reader turns because the reader knows and she does not. Subordinate: the nun, the well, the roses; they are the quiet place, not the story.

## PAGE 38

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Constance warns Milady against Lady de Winter, to her face. Walking arm in arm at evening, the widow asks about the boy, and Constance answers gladly that he will come. In the large middle panel Constance, worried for him, names Lady de Winter and repeats her warning, while the widow listens smiling and agrees she was right. The last close beat is the widow's face over Constance's shoulder with the smile gone, her thumb turning her dark ring, as Constance tells her she is not like that. The reader turns knowing exactly how much danger Constance has just talked herself into. Subordinate: the cloister and the evening light.

## PAGE 39

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Rochefort brings word that four riders will arrive by noon, and Milady decides she will take the girl or leave her. At a small back gate in moonlight, Rochefort leans down from a tired black horse to tell her the four are on the north road, the Gascon in front on the yellow horse. Milady, cold, orders her carriage for first light and plans the boat to England; when he asks what if the girl refuses, she turns back toward the dark convent, turning her ring, and answers in four quiet words. The reader turns because the carriage and the four are both coming at first light. Subordinate: the horse and the gate.

## PAGE 40

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Constance sees the yellow horse and refuses to run. In the morning she leans out of her window with both hands on the sill and, far down the long straight road, sees four riders, the first on a horse no one else in France would ride. Behind her the widow, cloak already on, urges her down to the carriage, calling them the Cardinal's men; Constance plainly refuses and stays. The widow goes very still, and then smiles again and offers a toast. The reader turns because that smile has meant harm before. Subordinate: the room's furniture and the riders' faces, which are far too small to matter.

## PAGE 41

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Milady opens her ring over Constance's glass. At a little table in the cloister the widow pours wine into two plain glasses and toasts the lucky boy while Constance watches the gate. In the large close panel, as the widow hands a glass across, her thumb flicks open the dark stone of her ring and a few grey grains fall into the wine; the caption tells us Constance did not see. Then Constance drinks to d'Artagnan, and the widow is already walking away, saying she will meet them at the gate. The reader turns because the glass is empty and Milady is not going to the gate. Subordinate: the jug, the table, the roses.

## PAGE 42

A spectacle page, one picture. The dominant turn: the four ride in at the gate as the carriage leaves at the back. The boy on the yellow horse bursts through the convent's front gate first, the three Musketeers close behind, two nuns stepping back against the wall; far away through the cloister arches a small black carriage is pulling out of the back gate. One caption gives the time and the second departure. The reader turns because they have arrived and someone has just left. Subordinate: the nuns and the carriage, which must be findable but small; the arrival owns the page.

## PAGE 43

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Constance goes quietly in his arms, and her last words are "You promised, and you came." The boy runs into the cloister; she is on the bench, very pale, smiling, an empty glass in her lap, glad she knew his horse. In the large middle panel he kneels and holds her, her head on his shoulder, the glass slipping from her fingers onto the grass unbroken, while she tells him about the kind lady with pale hair and that he kept his promise. Then her eyes close, peaceful, and he says her name; behind them Athos takes off his hat. The reader turns because she is gone and the boy does not understand yet. Subordinate: the glass and the table.

## PAGE 44

An illustrated-prose page, silent in the picture. The dominant turn: silence, and Aramis says the prayer for the dead. The large wide image holds the four friends still in the rose garden: the boy kneeling with Constance in his arms as if she were asleep, Porthos crushing his hat in his hands, Aramis kneeling with his little book open, Athos bareheaded in the arch, two nuns far off. ## PAGE 54

A dramatic page in the Cardinal's study, red and dark, one candle. The Cardinal sits stroking the grey cat and tells the boy, quietly, that a lady in his service is dead and that a yellow horse was seen at the river. The boy, muddy and white with anger, answers with the truth: she poisoned Constance, the Cardinal gave her Constance as her price, and the four never touched her. A dramatic page in the Cardinal's study. The dominant turn is the Cardinal reading his own paper, and his hand on the cat stopping for the first time in two books: he has met a man who cannot be bought, and it is the wrong one. The boy asks why, and the Cardinal's answer is the most honest thing he says in the volume. The fire stays small, inside the candle. The boy stays subordinate until his single question. The reader turns because the boy is holding a blue cloak with no name in it, from the man he hates most.
