## PAGE 53

A dramatic page at a gate of Paris, in the evening. The four friends ride home under the arch and find Rochefort waiting for them with the Cardinal's guards and an order. Porthos offers all four of them; the boy refuses the help. The dominant turn is the arrest: Rochefort's hand on d'Artagnan's shoulder while the boy waves his friends back, and Rochefort, who has lost to him five times, finally gets to win. The page belongs to the boy's choice, not to Rochefort's triumph: he goes on his own, and before he goes he turns to Athos, holds out his hand, and asks for the Cardinal's paper, because this part is his to do. Athos puts it into his open hand without argument. Nobody draws a sword and nobody is chained. The reader turns because the boy is walking into the Cardinal's study carrying the Cardinal's own paper in his coat.

## PAGE 54

A dramatic page in the Cardinal's study, red and dark, one candle. The Cardinal sits stroking the grey cat and tells the boy, quietly, that a lady in his service is dead and that a yellow horse was seen at the river. The boy, muddy and white with anger, answers with the truth: she poisoned Constance, the Cardinal gave her Constance as her price, and the four never touched her. The Cardinal's one defence, that he did not pour the wine, is left standing in the air. The dominant turn is the last panel: the boy lays the folded paper on the desk, opens it flat under the candle, and pushes it across, giving back the one thing that could have protected him for life, because he will not hide behind anything this man has signed. Rochefort stays silent by the door. The reader turns to see what the Cardinal does with his own words.

## PAGE 55

A dramatic page in the Cardinal's study. The dominant turn is the Cardinal reading his own paper, and his hand on the cat stopping for the first time in two books: he has met a man who cannot be bought, and it is the wrong one. He says so in a quiet sentence, then tears the paper slowly across, twice, and lets the pieces burn in the candle flame. Then he writes on a fresh sheet, seals it in red wax, and holds it out across the desk: a commission as an officer of the King's Musketeers, with the name left empty. The boy asks why, and the Cardinal's answer is the most honest thing he says in the volume. The fire stays small, inside the candle. The boy stays subordinate until his single question. The reader turns because the boy is holding a blue cloak with no name in it, from the man he hates most.

## PAGE 56

A dramatic page at the tavern table, lamplit. The boy throws the sealed commission down in front of Athos and tries to give it away: he will not wear the Cardinal's gift, and Athos has earned it ten times over. Athos refuses in three short sentences that carry his whole history. Porthos pushes it on to Aramis because officers have to read things; Aramis is leaving for the Church, next year, probably. The comedy is warm and quick and stays subordinate. The dominant turn is the last panel: Athos takes up the pen and writes on the empty line while the boy watches, and tells him that the blue is the King's, earned at the bastion in front of the whole army, and that he should wear it and make the Cardinal look at him in it. The reader turns because the boy's name is on the commission now.

## PAGE 57

An illustrated-prose page in the attic, in the evening. The dominant image is the boy standing at the attic window in his new Musketeer's blue cassock with the white cross over his old ochre doublet, the ring on his hand, looking not out of the window but down through the open door at the stair, where nobody is moving any more. The prose says what the picture cannot: the cloak is too big in the shoulders and exactly the blue of the other three; he came to Paris wanting to be let in, and the grand lady's front door cost him Constance; he would give the cloak back to hear her downstairs. The dominant turn is quiet and private. The second image is the foot of the stair, Constance's dark door, and his hand lighting the lamp beside it himself. The reader turns because his friends are waiting in the street.

## PAGE 58

A spectacle page, one full image, nearly silent: the last page of the volume. Seen from above and behind, the four friends walk away down a narrow Paris street, four abreast, all four now in the Musketeers' blue cassock with the white cross: the smallest in the middle with the grey hat and the broken feather and his hand on his too-long sword, the huge one with the red plumes, the slim fair one, the tall one on the outside. The old yellow horse follows on a loose rein. At the top of the frame, in the foreground, the Cardinal stands at a window above the street with one hand on his grey cat, watching the man whose commission he signed. The dominant turn is the picture itself: four blue cloaks, and the man in red still at his window. A single caption and the closing title card are the only words.
