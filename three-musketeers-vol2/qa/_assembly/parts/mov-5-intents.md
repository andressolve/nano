## PAGE 37

An illustrated-prose page. The dominant turn: a kind widow comes to the convent, and Constance trusts her. The large picture shows the quiet cloister, Constance with her basket looking up at the gate as a nun lets in a tall lady in a plain dark widow's dress, pale gold hair under a pushed-back veil; below, the two women sit on a stone bench and the lady holds both of Constance's hands. The prose owns the reading and carries the irony: the reader recognises the widow at once, and Constance, who warned the boy about kind ladies, never asks why. The reader turns because the reader knows and she does not. Subordinate: the nun, the well, the roses; they are the quiet place, not the story.

## PAGE 38

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Constance warns Milady against Lady de Winter, to her face. Walking arm in arm at evening, the widow asks about the boy, and Constance answers gladly that he will come. In the large middle panel Constance, worried for him, names Lady de Winter and repeats her warning, while the widow listens smiling and agrees she was right. The last close beat is the widow's face over Constance's shoulder with the smile gone, her thumb turning her dark ring, as Constance tells her she is not like that. The reader turns knowing exactly how much danger Constance has just talked herself into. Subordinate: the cloister and the evening light.

## PAGE 39

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Rochefort brings word that four riders will arrive by noon, and Milady decides she will take the girl or leave her. At a small back gate in moonlight, Rochefort leans down from a tired black horse to tell her the four are on the north road, the Gascon in front on the yellow horse. Milady, cold, orders her carriage for first light and plans the boat to England; when he asks what if the girl refuses, she turns back toward the dark convent, turning her ring, and answers in four quiet words. The reader turns because the carriage and the four are both coming at first light. Subordinate: the horse and the gate.

## PAGE 40

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Constance sees the yellow horse and refuses to run. In the morning she leans out of her window with both hands on the sill and, far down the long straight road, sees four riders, the first on a horse no one else in France would ride. Behind her the widow, cloak already on, urges her down to the carriage, calling them the Cardinal's men; Constance plainly refuses and stays. The widow goes very still, and then smiles again and offers a toast. The reader turns because that smile has meant harm before. Subordinate: the room's furniture and the riders' faces, which are far too small to matter.

## PAGE 41

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Milady opens her ring over Constance's glass. At a little table in the cloister the widow pours wine into two plain glasses and toasts the lucky boy while Constance watches the gate. In the large close panel, as the widow hands a glass across, her thumb flicks open the dark stone of her ring and a few grey grains fall into the wine; the caption tells us Constance did not see. Then Constance drinks to d'Artagnan, and the widow is already walking away, saying she will meet them at the gate. The reader turns because the glass is empty and Milady is not going to the gate. Subordinate: the jug, the table, the roses.

## PAGE 42

A spectacle page, one picture. The dominant turn: the four ride in at the gate as the carriage leaves at the back. The boy on the yellow horse bursts through the convent's front gate first, the three Musketeers close behind, two nuns stepping back against the wall; far away through the cloister arches a small black carriage is pulling out of the back gate. One caption gives the time and the second departure. The reader turns because they have arrived and someone has just left. Subordinate: the nuns and the carriage, which must be findable but small; the arrival owns the page.

## PAGE 43

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Constance goes quietly in his arms, and her last words are "You promised, and you came." The boy runs into the cloister; she is on the bench, very pale, smiling, an empty glass in her lap, glad she knew his horse. In the large middle panel he kneels and holds her, her head on his shoulder, the glass slipping from her fingers onto the grass unbroken, while she tells him about the kind lady with pale hair and that he kept his promise. Then her eyes close, peaceful, and he says her name; behind them Athos takes off his hat. The reader turns because she is gone and the boy does not understand yet. Subordinate: the glass and the table.

## PAGE 44

An illustrated-prose page, silent in the picture. The dominant turn: silence, and Aramis says the prayer for the dead. The large wide image holds the four friends still in the rose garden: the boy kneeling with Constance in his arms as if she were asleep, Porthos crushing his hat in his hands, Aramis kneeling with his little book open, Athos bareheaded in the arch, two nuns far off. A small panel shows Aramis's hand making the sign of the cross beside the empty glass in the grass. The prose carries the grief and the boy's realisation that he told the pale-haired lady where she was. The reader turns because someone must say what happens now. Subordinate: the nuns, the roses, the glass.

## PAGE 45

A dramatic page. The dominant turn: Athos takes the blame, the boy forgives him, and they ride. Athos crouches beside the boy, who says it was Milady and that he told her himself; Athos answers that he let her go at the Red Dovecote, and to blame him. In the large middle panel the boy looks at him a long moment and says Athos did not pour the wine either; Athos's hand is on his shoulder as he says she did, tells him to get up, and gives the road she took. Last, the boy lays Constance gently on the bench, stands and draws his sword; Athos turns it into a chase. The reader turns because they are riding after her. Subordinate: the bench and the cloister.
