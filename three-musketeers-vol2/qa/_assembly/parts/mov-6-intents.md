## PAGE 46

An illustrated-prose page. The storm owns it: four riders flat out along a flooded road at night under lashing rain and lightning, hatless and streaming, the yellow horse in front. The prose field rides in the storm sky above them and tells the chase: the storm, the wheel marks turning to rivers, the carriage found tipped and empty, the coachman fled, and ahead a lantern swinging on a ferry while a weir roars downstream. A smaller panel below shows that empty black carriage in a ditch by the river bank with its door hanging open and, far off through the rain, the ferry lantern. The dominant turn is the storm, the abandoned carriage, the ferry by the weir. The reader turns because she is at the river and there is only one ferry. Nobody speaks; the carriage stays subordinate to the ride.

## PAGE 47

A dramatic page at the ferry landing in the rain. Milady stands on the flat ferry raft, hood back, a knife at its rope, and warns the four arriving on the black timbers not to take one step. The page belongs to the moment the boy, face wild and sword out, strides at her and Athos's arm across his chest stops him: Athos refuses to let him do this in the dark, alone, in a rage, because he once did the same and it came back. The boy shakes, his sword point drops, and he asks what they do with her; Athos answers that first she will hear what she did. The dominant turn is Milady on the ferry raft; Athos stops the boy's sword. The reader turns because four men are about to judge her and she has a knife on the rope.

## PAGE 48

A dramatic page at the ferry landing in the rain. Three of the friends stand in a row at the edge of the timbers, the boy, Porthos and Aramis, and each names one of Milady's crimes to her face. Then the page belongs to Athos alone, a step in front of the others, quiet, rain running off his face, saying the charge that is his own: she lied to him the day he married her; that one he can forgive, the others he cannot. Behind him Porthos and Aramis glance at each other. Milady answers with a smile and cuts the rope, and the raft lurches out into the flood with her kneeling on it. The dominant turn is the four naming her crimes; she cuts the rope. The reader turns because the raft is loose and the weir is roaring.

## PAGE 49

A spectacle page, one great image. The black flood at night, lit by lightning: the loose ferry raft swings and spins out on the current, its lantern swaying, Milady kneeling on it. Athos stands waist-deep in the river, hatless, reaching toward her with his hand open. Behind him the boy grips Athos's belt with both hands; behind the boy Porthos grips the boy; and at the edge of the landing Aramis has one arm round the post and holds Porthos. A chain of four men reaching into the river. One caption across the storm says how they held on. The dominant turn is Athos wading in, held by a chain of his three friends. The reader turns because Athos's hand is out and she is close enough to take it.

## PAGE 50

A dramatic page on the river. Athos's open hand reaches toward the edge of the spinning raft as he offers her a judge and a trial in daylight. The page belongs to Milady kneeling on the raft beside the lantern, looking at his hand and then up into his face, refusing it: he hanged her once and she lived, and she will not owe him her life. Then she turns on the boy and tells him to let go of the belt so that Athos goes with her. Last, the boy, chest-deep behind Athos, grips the belt harder, face set, and says never. The dominant turn is Milady refusing Athos's hand; the boy refusing to let go. The reader turns because she has refused the hand and the boy has refused her.

## PAGE 51

A spectacle page, one great image. The raft, small now, spins away into the dark and the rain toward the long white line of the weir, its lantern a single point of light, Milady kneeling on it with her back to the bank. In the near foreground the chain of four men hauls itself back toward the landing, each holding the next. One caption over the black water says she turned her back on the hand and the lantern went out. The dominant turn is the raft going into the dark toward the weir as the lantern goes out. Nothing is shown beyond the light vanishing; the four are safe and spent. The reader turns because it is over and none of them knows yet what that feels like.

## PAGE 52

An illustrated-prose page at grey dawn. The page belongs to the wide, quiet image: the river brown and calm, the cut ferry rope hanging slack into the water with nothing on its end, and the four sitting on the bank in their wet clothes with the horses behind them, the yellow horse among them. A smaller panel shows Athos alone at the water's edge, looking downstream. The prose field sits in the grey sky and carries the morning: the empty river, the boy's cold and tiredness and wish for Constance, her burial under the convent roses, and Athos's one word, which the boy at last understands. The dominant turn is dawn: the empty river, Constance buried, Athos's "Good." The reader turns because they have to go back to Paris, where the Cardinal is waiting.
