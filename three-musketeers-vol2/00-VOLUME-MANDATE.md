## Story decisions made in Phase 0 (delegated authority, 2026-10-17)

- **Constance dies.** Milady poisons her at the convent at Béthune minutes
  before the four arrive, and she dies in d'Artagnan's arms. The volume is
  about a woman nobody suspects; the one person who suspected her from the
  first page is the one she kills, and the boy's own trust helped her find
  the door. Without this the villain costs nothing and the boy's mistake costs
  nothing. Staged as a quiet storybook death: a glass of wine, a cloister,
  her head on his shoulder, eyes closed. No pain drawn, no convulsion, no
  mark.
- **Milady's end is her own choice.** The four do not execute her. At a
  flooded river ferry she cuts herself loose to escape them; the ferry turns
  toward the weir; Athos wades in, held by a chain of his three friends, and
  offers his hand and a trial in daylight. She refuses it — she will not owe
  her life to the man who once judged her alone — and the river takes the
  ferry into the dark. Nobody is shown in the water; a lantern goes out. This
  keeps the source's moral weight (she is judged, and pays) and gives the
  climax to the friendship: four men holding on to each other in a flood so
  that one of them can be merciful.
- **Athos's secret is told, once, as his own story.** He married her at
  twenty-five; found the lily branded on her shoulder, the mark the law burns
  on a convicted thief; and in the same hour, alone, judged her and hanged
  her from a tree in his own wood. When he came back the rope was cut and she
  was gone. He gave up his name and became Athos. Told on an
  illustrated-prose page in his voice; the tree is never drawn with a rope in
  use — a young lord riding away from a dark wood, and later an empty branch.
- **The ending is a blue cloak.** Volume I planted the want (he is the only
  one of the four without one). The Cardinal, trapped by his own blank pardon,
  signs a commission in the King's Musketeers with the name left
  empty; the boy offers it to each of the three; each refuses; Athos writes
  the boy's name. He gets in, and it has cost him the girl downstairs.

## Owner constraints carried from Volume I

- **No blood, no drawn wound, nobody shown dying violently.** Swordplay is
  loud and constant; defeat is a disarm, a fall, a wall, a river. Constance's
  death is the only death on the page and it is quiet. Milady's end is
  off-image (the boat into the dark). The bastion's attackers are driven off,
  never killed on the page.
- **The Cardinal's politics are felt, never explained.** One illustrated-prose
  paragraph may say what La Rochelle is (a city by the sea that shut its gates
  on the King and is waiting for English ships) because the boy has to go
  there. Nothing else about Huguenots, England, or the court.
- **Swordplay and friendship loud.** Every movement has steel in it, and the
  four are together, or reaching for each other, on more pages than not.

## The specific failure this volume must avoid

**The villain's book.** Milady is the most famous thing in the source, and a
faithful adaptation follows her: to England, to her prison, to her jailer, to
the Duke's death. Every one of those pages has no d'Artagnan in it and no
friendship in it. The countermeasure is structural: **Milady appears only
where one of the four can see her or hear her, or where she is working on
someone the four love** (the reader sees her with the Cardinal twice, pp 4
and 22, because the reader must know more than the boy; and at Béthune with
Constance and Rochefort, pp 37–41, because Constance is an active chooser and
those pages are hers) — she never goes to England on the page, and nothing
happens to her alone.

**The second failure is the dazzled boy who is merely foolish.** d'Artagnan
must be charmed by Milady for a reason the reader respects: she is the first
grand person in Paris who opens the front door to him, and Volume I was about
a boy who wanted, more than anything, to be let in. Every page of Movement I
must make the reader feel why he goes back.

**The third is the opening rushed past its feelings (Volume I lesson 14).**
Constance's farewell gets two pages; the brand gets its own page; the grief
at Béthune gets a silent page before anyone speaks of the chase.
