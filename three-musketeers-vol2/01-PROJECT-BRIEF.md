# The Three Musketeers, Volume II: Milady — Project Brief

**Form:** cinematic illustrated novel, portrait 1024 × 1536, ~57 pages.
**Audience:** Francisco (10) and Sebastian (7).
**Source:** *Les Trois Mousquetaires*, Alexandre Dumas, 1844, chapters 26–67
(Milady, the siege of La Rochelle, the Red Dovecote, the bastion Saint-Gervais,
Béthune, the end of Milady, the commission). Evidence of incident only, never
of wording. Sequel to *The Three Musketeers, Volume I: The Queen's Diamonds*
(`../three-musketeers-vol1/`).

---

## The engine

**A woman nobody suspects comes between four friends who swore all for one —
and the only one of them who knows what she is has never told anyone.**

People still tell this half of the story for Milady: the most dangerous
person in France is a smiling English widow whose carriage everyone knows,
and the one man who could have warned them has spent his life not saying her
name. The engine is that gap — the reader learns the danger before the boy,
and Athos's silence before his story — and the friendship that has to close
it. Protected absolutely.

**If the protagonist reads as calm and above it all for more than a page at a
stretch, the engine has been written out.** That is a script-gate blocker.
d'Artagnan is dazzled, then terrified, then desperate, then grieving, then
furious, and at the river he has to choose to hold on to his friend instead
of to his rage.

## The spine

The summer after the Queen's ball, the Cardinal sets a new hand on the boy
who beat him: Milady, an English widow nobody suspects. She stages a
runaway carriage, the boy saves her, and she opens the front door of the
grandest house he has ever been inside. Constance, sent north into hiding by
the Queen, warns him about ladies who are kind for no reason; he tells Milady
that the girl he loves is in a convent in the north anyway. When he
overhears the truth, Milady draws a knife, her sleeve tears, and he sees a
lily burned on her shoulder — and that night Athos tells him the story he has
never told: he married that woman, and hanged her, and she did not die.

The war takes them to La Rochelle. Milady's poisoned wine reaches the boy
first; then, in an inn called the Red Dovecote, Athos hears the Cardinal give
her a paper that pardons whatever she does, and hears her price — the boy, and
the girl at the convent at Béthune. Athos takes the paper at the point of his
sword, but she is gone north at dawn. To win leave and talk where no letter
can be read, the four bet the whole army they can eat breakfast for an hour in
a ruined bastion under the enemy's walls, and hold it. They ride six days
north and arrive at Béthune one hour too late: Constance dies in the boy's
arms with Milady's wine in her. They catch Milady at a flooded river ferry;
she cuts herself loose; Athos, held by the other three, offers her his hand
and a trial, and she will not take it. The river keeps her.

Back in Paris, the man with the scar arrests the boy, and the Cardinal is
handed his own pardon. He signs a lieutenant's commission in the King's
Musketeers with the name left blank. The boy offers it to each of the three,
each refuses, and Athos writes the boy's name in it. **The last page lands
on a fork the reader can feel: he finally has the blue cloak he came to
Paris for, and the girl who should have seen him in it is buried at Béthune —
and the man in red who signed it is watching four blue cloaks walk away.**

## What this volume is

- A complete story: the lady, the lily, the war, the bastion, Béthune, the
  river, the cloak. Nothing the reader must resolve elsewhere.
- Athos's book as much as the boy's. The silent one finally speaks, and the
  climax is his choice.
- A book with a clock: from the Red Dovecote, the reader knows where Milady is
  going and how far ahead she is.
- A friendship book whose set piece is the most famous breakfast in fiction:
  four men, one basket, an hour, an army watching.
- Funny where it can be: the bet, Porthos's appetite, Aramis's handkerchief
  as a flag, Rochefort's count of defeats, the boy escaping through a window.
- A book with a real loss in it, felt for as long as it deserves.

## What this volume is not

- Not Milady's book. She never goes to England on the page; no jailer, no
  Felton, no Duke's death, no maid, no de Wardes, no executioner of Lille.
- Not a romance with Milady. The boy is dazzled by a grand lady's front door
  and her attention, never courted into her bedroom; there is no disguise in
  the dark.
- Not a war comic. The siege is one camp, one bastion, one morning. Nobody is
  killed on the page at the bastion; attackers run.
- Not a court drama. No King with lines, no Queen with lines, no Tréville.
- Not a lecture: La Rochelle gets one sentence of why.

## The audience promise

**What these readers already enjoy.** A villain they know about before the
hero does. A secret a grown-up has kept for years. A clock — she is a day
ahead. A breakfast eaten for a bet in the middle of a battle. A paper that
says whoever holds it cannot be punished. Swords, horses, a storm, a flood.
Four friends making a chain with their arms.

**The repeatable pleasures.** The yellow horse (Constance knows him by it);
Athos's "good"; Porthos hungry; Aramis "about to join the Church"; Rochefort
keeping count of how many times the boy has beaten him; the man in red at a
window.

**The emotional promise.** The reader knows what it is to be flattered by
someone important and to not see why. They watch a boy learn it at the worst
price, and watch the friend who learned it years ago at a worse one choose
mercy anyway, while the boy holds on to him.

**The central dramatic question.** *Can the four reach Constance before the
woman nobody suspects does?* The book's answer is no — and what they do next
is the measure of them.

**Complexity budget.** Eight names (one new: Milady, also called Lady de
Winter; the brief says why both). Four new places: Milady's house, the camp
at La Rochelle with its inn (the Red Dovecote) and bastion, the convent at
Béthune, the river ferry. One clock. Zero assumed knowledge from Volume I:
every returning face, the oath, the ring, the scar, the yellow horse, and "the
man in red who knows the boy's name" is re-introduced in story before it
matters. "Gascon" is explained in words on page 2 (Volume I lesson 15);
"Milady" is explained in her own mouth on page 6.

**What this project refuses to become.** A tour of Milady's crimes. A book
where the boy is a fool. A book where the four become executioners. A
history of the siege.

## Length

Working count **57**, reached by cutting: forty-two source chapters to seven
movements; forty-five names to eight; the whole English half of Milady's
story to one sentence. The count follows the script and will not shrink by
compression. If it grows past sixty, the split is after Movement III (the Red
Dovecote), and the session decides under delegated authority.

## The reader

A first-time reader who will never read the source. They must never have to
solve the adaptation, supply a missing causal step, or recognize a character
the book has not introduced. "Recoverable," "eventually clear," and
"understandable after looking back" are revision flags, not passing grades.
