The Boy Who Chased Engines

Honda chased one rattling automobile in 1914. By 1963 his small machines were ridden across Japan, Europe, and America by people who had never owned a motor before. The next part of the story will ask whether the same idea can survive its own success.

Test Your Understanding

1. What was Honda's first job, and how old was he when he took it?
Right. At fifteen, Honda left Komyo Village by train and arrived alone in Tokyo, sweeping floors and minding the boss's baby for almost a year before he was allowed to touch a wrench. For a village boy, the chance to touch tools at all was a kind of miracle.
Not quite. Honda's first job was as a live-in apprentice at Art Shokai, a Tokyo automobile-repair shop. He was fifteen, and for almost a year he was allowed to do nothing but sweep, haul, and watch. The wrench came later.
2. Why did Honda's first piston rings fail Toyota's quality test?
Yes. Honda could see the rings looked right, and he had matched their shape exactly. But inside a hot engine, a difference of thousandths of a millimeter is the difference between a part that seals and a part that lets fire escape. He went back to school in his thirties to learn why.
Not quite. The rings looked the same as good ones — but inside a working engine, tolerances measured in thousandths of a millimeter decide whether the part holds. Almost right was wrong. That answer sent Honda back to school at thirty-one.
3. What did Takeo Fujisawa understand that Honda did not?
Exactly. Honda built brilliant engines. Fujisawa saw that an engine without a dealer, a bank, a customer who trusts you, and a mechanic who can fix it tomorrow is just metal. Together they were a complete machine; alone, each one was half.
Not quite. Fujisawa saw what Honda did not: an engine is only the engine. It needs dealers, banks, customers, and service mechanics around it — a working system. From factory to dealer to customer back to service. That was Fujisawa's job.
4. What made the Super Cub different from other motorcycles?
Right. The Super Cub was not a racing machine made smaller. It was a daily machine made carefully: step-through so a skirt or kimono was no obstacle; leg shield so road dirt stayed off your clothes; enclosed chain so oil didn't ruin a trouser cuff; automatic clutch so you didn't have to learn. Many small decisions, all respecting the rider.
Not quite. The Super Cub's genius was not one dramatic invention. It was many small decisions that respected the rider as an ordinary person who had laundry, groceries, and somewhere to be: step-through frame, leg shield, enclosed chain, automatic clutch.
5. Why did Fujisawa want to enter the American market first, instead of Europe?
Yes. Europe was easier — small motorcycles were already a market there. Fujisawa chose America precisely because nobody believed it could work: Americans wanted big, loud, fast machines. If Honda could win there, the world could not look away.
Not quite. Fujisawa chose America because America was harder. Small motorcycles already had a market in Europe. America scared Japanese exporters — that was why he wanted it. If Honda could succeed there, the world could not ignore them.
