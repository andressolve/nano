## PAGE 6 — Beyond Repair

**Narrative job:** Transition from repair to manufacturing. Teach what a piston ring is on the same page.

**Image:** Night workshop, 1936. Honda, 30, alone at a workbench under a single lamp. In front of him: a row of small metal rings he is making by hand, a furnace glowing in the background, gauges, calipers. Inset (upper right corner of the page): a clean cutaway of a piston inside an engine cylinder, with a thin metal ring fitted around the piston's groove — no labels, just shape. Empty teacup. Window dark.

**Image text:**

Top-left caption:
"By 1936, Honda was successful as a repairman — and bored. Repair, he had decided, had a ceiling. You inherited another person's design, another person's mistake, and the best you could do was undo damage someone else had built in. He wanted to make the part before it broke."

Mid-right caption (beside the inset):
"The part he chose was a piston ring. A piston ring is a thin metal circle that sits in a groove around a piston, sealing the burning fuel inside the engine cylinder so its pressure pushes the piston down instead of leaking out. It must survive heat, friction, and thousands of strokes a minute. It looks simple. That is the trap."

**Density:** T5 (2 long captions, ~125 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory.
**Lettering placement:** top-left and mid-right caption blocks; keep workbench and the cutaway inset clear of text.

## PAGE 7 — Almost Right Is Wrong

**Narrative job:** Precision failure teaches humility. Honda goes back to school.

**Image:** Split landscape. Left two-thirds: factory inspection bench, late 1937. Rows of piston rings laid out. An inspector in a Toyota uniform separates a small accepted pile from a much larger rejected pile. Honda stands beside the bench, jaw tight, holding one of his own rings. Workshop amber light, failed-metal gray. Right third: night classroom, Hamamatsu Industrial Institute. Honda sits among younger students at a desk, work clothes still dirty, taking notes while a teacher demonstrates with a metal sample. Chalkboard shapes behind, no readable equations.

**Image text:**

Upper-left caption:
"Honda's first piston rings went to Toyota. Almost all of them were rejected. The rings looked correct on the bench. Inside an engine they failed."

Speech bubble (Inspector):
"These will not pass."

Speech bubble (Honda):
"They are the same shape."

Speech bubble (Inspector):
"Shape is not enough."

Mid-right caption:
"Heat, friction, pressure, and tolerances measured in thousandths of a millimeter made the judgment. Almost right was wrong."

Bottom caption band:
"So at thirty-one, with a wife and a shop and a reputation, Honda went back to school. He audited metallurgy classes at the Hamamatsu Industrial Institute — sitting beside teenagers, learning why metals behave the way they do under heat and stress. Failure had not made him wise. The rejected rings had only told him he did not yet understand enough."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions + 3 bubbles, ~135 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory.
**Lettering placement:** upper-left and mid-right captions; bubble pockets above the inspection bench; bottom caption band over a quiet wall.

## PAGE 13 — What Honda Did Not See

**Narrative job:** Make Fujisawa's contribution visible before the sales-system page lands.

**Image:** Diptych composition. Left half: Honda alone in the workshop, leaning over a half-assembled engine, eyes locked on a stuck valve. Nothing else exists for him. Right half: Fujisawa at a desk with a chain of objects laid out in a line that runs across the panel — a factory diagram, a dealer's invoice, a bank statement, a customer's letter, a service-mechanic's parts list, a ledger. Honda sees the engine. Fujisawa sees the route the engine has to travel.

**Image text:**

Top caption:
"Honda saw the machine first. Always. The valve that did not seat. The bolt with the wrong tolerance. The cylinder that ran hot. He could lose hours inside one part."

Mid caption:
"Fujisawa saw something different — what the machine had to travel through after it left the factory before it could help anyone."

Bottom caption band:
"From factory to dealer. From dealer to customer. From customer back to service. From this month's sale to next month's payroll. Each handoff was a small machine of its own — a machine made of paper and people and trust, and just as capable of failure as the engine itself. That system was invisible to Honda until it broke. Fujisawa's job was to make sure it never did."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~130 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory.
**Lettering placement:** top, mid, and bottom captions arranged so the diptych composition stays readable.

## PAGE 15 — The Cost of Precision

**Narrative job:** Ambition creates financial danger; partnership tested.

**Image:** Two zones. Left: Honda on the factory floor with newly arrived imported machine tools — large lathes, grinders, presses fresh from Switzerland and Germany. He is excited, running a hand along a lathe bed. Right: Fujisawa in an office with a stack of bank documents, the same factory visible through a window, his expression measured and worried. Workers in between look impressed and uneasy.

**Image text:**

Upper-left caption:
"To compete with the world's manufacturers, Honda needed the world's machine tools. In 1952 and 1953 he imported lathes, grinders, and presses from Switzerland and Germany — equipment far more accurate than anything Japanese factories owned."

Mid-right caption:
"The new tools could make parts to tolerances his old equipment could not approach. They could also bankrupt the company."

Bottom caption band:
"The bills arrived whether the products were ready or not. In 1954, with a flawed motorcycle in the field and bankers asking when Honda would repay the loans, the company nearly collapsed. Honda's answer was technical: find the weakness, change the design, fix it. Fujisawa's answer was social: keep banks, workers, and dealers from walking away before the fix arrived. They needed both answers, and they barely got them in time."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~130 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory.
**Lettering placement:** upper-left, mid-right, bottom band.

## PAGE 16 — The World Is Faster

**Narrative job:** Global humility — Isle of Man recon.

**Image:** A wet, walled road on the Isle of Man, 1954. European racing motorcycles sweep past at speed, their riders tucked low. Honda stands at the roadside in a coat too thin for the rain, watching. He looks neither defeated nor inspired — he looks like someone taking a measurement.

**Image text:**

Upper-left caption:
"In 1954, Honda traveled to the Isle of Man — a small island in the Irish Sea where Europe's motorcycle manufacturers raced each year on ordinary public roads. The race was famous for being dangerous. It was also the unofficial measure of who built the world's fastest, most reliable small machines."

Mid caption (over road):
"Honda watched. The European bikes were a generation ahead of his. Some made three times the power of his fastest engine."

Bottom caption band:
"Most people would have come home discouraged. Honda came home with a number. The gap was not embarrassing because it was large. It was useful because it was now measurable. He announced that within a few years, Honda would race here — and win."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~130 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory.
**Lettering placement:** upper-left, mid, bottom band; let the racing motorcycles sweep across the middle.

## PAGE 17 — Watching the Customer

**Narrative job:** The user changes the machine. Europe study, 1956.

**Image:** A European street, mid-1950s. Honda and Fujisawa stand on a sidewalk, watching ordinary people on small mopeds — a young woman in a skirt riding to work, an older man with a bag of groceries strapped on, a delivery rider in a clean shirt. Honda points at something specific (a leg shield); Fujisawa is writing in a small notebook. They are not sightseeing. They are studying.

**Image text:**

Top caption:
"In 1956, Honda and Fujisawa traveled together through Europe. They were planning the next Honda machine. They did not only study engines."

Mid-left caption:
"They studied riders. Who could start the machine without help? Who could carry groceries? Who could ride in ordinary clothes without arriving covered in oil? Who could afford it? Who would trust it every morning to get to work?"

Bottom caption band:
"The answers shaped a list of design problems most engineers had never thought of as engineering problems. How do you make starting simple enough for someone who has never owned a motor? How do you keep the chain from staining a skirt? How do you make the machine feel friendly instead of frightening? Honda and Fujisawa came home with a list. The next machine would have to answer it."

**Density:** T5 (3 captions, ~140 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory.
**Lettering placement:** top, mid-left, bottom band.

## PAGE 19 — Why It Was Different

**Narrative job:** Annotated breakthrough page. The Super Cub as a design solution, not just a famous object.

**Image:** Central anchor: the Super Cub C100 in clean three-quarter view, isolated against a soft pale background. Around it, four small vignette callouts pointing to specific features: (1) step-through frame — a woman in a skirt mounts easily; (2) leg shield — a clean office shoe; (3) enclosed chain — no oil on a trouser cuff; (4) automatic centrifugal clutch — a hand on the throttle, no clutch lever. Each callout has a short caption box anchored beside it.

**Image text:**

Top caption:
"The Super Cub solved problems most people never called engineering problems."

Callout 1 (step-through frame):
"Step-through frame. You did not have to swing a leg over. A skirt or a kimono was no obstacle."

Callout 2 (leg shield):
"Leg shield. Road dirt and engine heat stayed away from your clothes."

Callout 3 (enclosed chain):
"Enclosed chain. The oily bicycle chain — the part that ruined a trouser cuff — was hidden, covered, sealed."

Callout 4 (automatic clutch):
"Automatic clutch. No hand lever to time. You twisted the throttle. The bike went."

Bottom caption:
"Its genius was not one dramatic invention. It was many small decisions that respected the rider as a person who had laundry, groceries, and somewhere to be."

**Density:** T5+ (top + 4 callouts + bottom = 6 elements, ~115 words). **Model:** Pro mandatory. **NOTE:** This is the "annotated breakthrough" format from `reference_biography_format_evolution.md`. Watch for callout-line accuracy; each caption must visibly anchor to its feature.
**Lettering placement:** central image clear, captions arrayed around it pointing to features.

**Image text:**

Upper-left caption:
"By 1960 Honda was bleeding money in America. The big bikes had been a mistake. But Honda had spent fifteen years learning what to do with mistakes."
