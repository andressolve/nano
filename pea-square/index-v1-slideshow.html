Heredity Is Arithmetic

The Pea Square — How to Predict a Living Thing

Mendel found a mysterious three-to-one hiding in his garden. This book built the little machine underneath it — a grid of four boxes you can fill in yourself. From two factors and one square came the 3 : 1, the hidden 1 : 2 : 1, and, scaled up, the 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. None of it was magic — just outcomes, counted. One short quiz stands between you and the end. It tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why does crossing two tall hybrids (Tt × Tt) give about three tall plants for every short one?
Right — the ratio is just the four boxes counted. Fill the grid for Tt × Tt and you get TT, Tt, Tt, tt. Three carry at least one T (tall); one is tt (short). 3 : 1 falls straight out of the arithmetic.
Not quite. A factor is not "stronger" by a number, and offspring do not vote with the majority. Fill the grid for Tt × Tt: you get TT, Tt, Tt, tt — three tall, one short. The 3 : 1 is simply the four equally-likely boxes counted.
2. Why does the short trait vanish in the first cross, then reappear in a later generation?
Right — recessive means hidden, not gone. A Tt plant looks tall because the T shows, but it quietly carries a t. Only when two of those t factors meet (tt) does the short trait appear again.
Not quite. The factor is never destroyed, and it does not have a will of its own. The short trait is recessive: hidden whenever a T is present, but still carried — it shows again only when a plant inherits two t factors (tt).
3. Why is a Punnett square really just counting chances, the way flipping coins is?
Right — that is the whole idea. Each parent's two factors split at random, like a coin landing T or t. Two flips give four equally-likely results, and those four results are exactly the four boxes of the square.
Not quite — the grid has nothing to do with playing cards, and you do not flip a coin to fill it in. It mirrors coin-flips because each parent passes one factor at random, so the four boxes are the four equally-likely results of two flips.
4. Why does crossing a mystery tall plant with a pure-short (tt) plant reveal its hidden factor?
Right — the tt partner is a clean test. Since it can only give a t, every child's height depends on the mystery plant. All tall means it was TT; any short children mean it was hiding a t (Tt). The count cannot be fooled.
Not quite — a cross does not rewrite a plant's factors, and a plant cannot "sense" anything. The trick works because a pure-short plant can only give a t, so the children's heights reveal whether the mystery plant secretly carried a t.
5. Why did Mendel deliberately choose traits like tall-vs-short and round-vs-wrinkled?
Right — he picked the tidy cases on purpose. Clean either/or traits gave sharp counts and clear ratios. He knew the world is richer than that — red and white snapdragons blend to pink — but simple traits let the hidden arithmetic show itself first.
Not quite — it was not about soil or market price. Mendel chose either/or traits because their clean forms kept the counting simple. Many real traits blend (red × white snapdragon → pink) or come in more than two versions; he started with the tidy cases on purpose.
