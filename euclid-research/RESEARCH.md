# Euclid — Research and Invention Ledger

This dossier separates the very small historical core from the dramatic life invented for the graphic novel. It is the factual source of truth for `../euclid/`.

## Editorial premise

Almost nothing reliable survives about Euclid as a person. That is not concealed or padded with false certainty. The book opens by stating the gap, then tells a clearly labeled historical novel inside it. The invented scenes are allowed to be decisive and emotionally complete; the afterword distinguishes them from the historical frame.

## What is historically secure or broadly accepted

- Euclid was a Greek mathematician active in Alexandria around 300 BCE, conventionally during the reign of Ptolemy I.
- He is credited with the thirteen books of the *Elements*.
- The *Elements* organizes geometry, magnitude, and number theory through definitions, postulates, common notions, propositions, and demonstrations.
- Much of its mathematical content predates Euclid. Proclus, writing roughly seven centuries later, says Euclid collected results associated with Eudoxus, completed work associated with Theaetetus, and gave rigorous demonstrations to results earlier writers had proved less securely.
- Book I, Proposition 1 constructs an equilateral triangle on a given finite straight line by drawing two circles with the segment as radius and joining their intersection to the segment's endpoints.
- The surviving work includes thirteen books. The exact traditional total of propositions varies by editorial counting; the novel does not print a total.
- Books VII–IX concern number theory, Book IX includes the proof that prime numbers are inexhaustible, Book X treats incommensurable magnitudes, and Books XI–XIII treat solid geometry and culminate in the five regular solids.
- No autograph manuscript survives. The oldest substantially complete Greek manuscripts are many centuries later than Euclid and belong to a complex history of copying and editing.
- The work passed through Greek, Arabic, Latin, manuscript, and print traditions and became one of the most influential mathematical books ever written.

## Late traditions, not contemporary evidence

The following stories are useful dramatic legends but are not proven incidents from Euclid's life:

1. Ptolemy asks whether geometry has a shorter route than the *Elements*. Euclid answers that there is no royal road to geometry. Proclus preserves this story many centuries later; a similar saying is also attached to Menaechmus and Alexander.
2. A student asks what profit he will gain after learning the first proposition. Euclid orders that he be given a few coins because he insists on profiting from what he learns. The wording and number of coins vary by retelling.
3. Later sources characterize Euclid as a gentle or fair teacher, but they do not supply a dependable biography.

The novel uses both anecdotes, labels them as stories transmitted much later, and changes the coin scene into a turning point rather than presenting Euclid's sarcasm as uncomplicated wisdom.

## Unknown

- Birthplace and birth date.
- Parents, childhood, wealth, marriage, children, and private life.
- Exact education and teachers.
- When and why he came to Alexandria.
- Whether he worked inside a formal Museum or Library institution in the way later imagination often depicts.
- The exact process, timetable, and collaborators behind the *Elements*.
- His physical appearance.
- Date, place, and circumstances of death.

## Inventions in the novel

- A formative scene in an unnamed Aegean port where a ruler's men move a boundary stone.
- Euclid's personal fear that conquest, fire, and human memory can erase knowledge.
- Nefru, an Egyptian surveyor and papyrus copyist, is wholly fictional. She embodies practical measurement, the material labor of diagrams and copies, and the local Egyptian knowledge too often erased from lone-genius stories.
- Philippos, the ambitious student, is fictional. His arc adapts the late coin anecdote.
- All private conversations with Ptolemy are invented around the traditional royal-road story.
- The rejected royal edition, the cracked wax tablet, the damaged shipment of scrolls, and the completion gathering are invented.
- The precise order in which Euclid works through the books is dramatized rather than reconstructed.

## Guardrails for the script

- Never claim that Euclid invented all the mathematics in the *Elements*.
- Never claim that the Library of Alexandria burned in Euclid's lifetime.
- Never call the *Elements* merely a geometry book; it also contains number theory and theories of magnitude.
- Never present the anecdotes as eyewitness history.
- Never claim that Egyptian rope-stretchers directly supplied a particular Euclidean proof unless the scene is clearly fictional.
- Do not use modern symbols, Cartesian axes, algebraic equations, decimal notation, or modern bound books in Euclid's scenes.
- Use papyrus rolls, wax tablets, reed pens, compasses/dividers, straightedges, knotted cords, pebbles, and sand tables.
- Ptolemaic Alexandria should look like a young Hellenistic Mediterranean city with strong Egyptian presence, not classical Athens transplanted whole and not later Roman Alexandria.

## Verified text used as artifact

Book I, Proposition 1, in the 1908 Heath translation:

> On a given finite straight line to construct an equilateral triangle.

The page will use a plain-language rendering for accessibility:

> Begin with any finite straight line. Construct an equilateral triangle upon it.

This is adaptation text, not presented as a verbatim ancient translation.

## Source notes

Primary/late-antique witness:

- Proclus, *Commentary on the First Book of Euclid's Elements*, translated by Thomas Taylor (1792), Project Gutenberg edition: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/74253
  - Places Euclid in the time of the first Ptolemy.
  - Describes him as collecting results from Eudoxus, completing results associated with Theaetetus, and strengthening earlier demonstrations.
  - Preserves the royal-road anecdote.

Text of the *Elements*:

- Euclid, *Elements*, Book I, Proposition 1, Heath edition hosted by Trinity College Dublin: https://www.maths.tcd.ie/~dwilkins/Euclid/Elements_Heath1908/BookI/EuclidElementsBk01Prop01_Heath1908.php

Modern scholarly overview:

- Michalis Sialaros, “Euclid,” *Oxford Classical Dictionary* / Oxford Research Encyclopedias, updated 2021: https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.2521
  - Euclid flourished around 300 BCE in Alexandria.
  - The *Elements* is central to his influence; other treatises are attributed in late sources.

Transmission overview:

- “Euclid's Elements — Early History”: https://euclid.analogmachine.org/early-history
  - Summarizes the lack of biographical evidence, the compilation of earlier work, and the Greek/Arabic/Latin manuscript history.

Secondary checks:

## Research decision

This is shelved as Biography because Euclid is the protagonist and the work concerns the remembered author of a historical text. Its subtitle, title page, opening page, and afterword all identify it as a novel built inside an undocumented life.
