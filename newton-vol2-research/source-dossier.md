## 1. The 1693 breakdown ("black year")

- **Period:** roughly 1692–1693, deepening to acute crisis mid-1693.
- **Letter to Samuel Pepys, 13 September 1693.** Newton: *"I am extremely troubled by the embroilment I am in, have neither ate nor slept well in the last twelve months, nor have my former consistency of mind."* He reported being "seized by a distemper that kept him awake for above five nights altogether."
- **Letter to John Locke (also Sept 1693).** Newton apologised for having accused Locke of trying *"to embroil me wth woemen & by other means"*, and for "thinking that there was a designe to sell me an office", and for wishing Locke dead.
- **Mercury (posthumous evidence).** Hair preserved by the Portsmouth family showed mercury levels up to ~40× normal range, lead/arsenic/antimony also elevated. Mercury poisoning from alchemical work is one accepted contributing cause; psychological exhaustion after the *Principia* is another. Do not present either as proven sole cause — both have weight.
- **Editorial note for the page:** anchor on the candlelit study, the spilled ink, the unanswered letters, the closed door (per HANDOFF safety-filter guidance). Quote the Pepys letter verbatim in a caption.

## 2. Move to London — Royal Mint

- **Warden of the Mint:** appointment Spring 1696. Newton settled in London in May 1696.
- **Master of the Mint:** promoted 1699 (Christopher Neale's successor); held until death 1727.
- **Catherine Barton moved in with him "shortly after" he settled in London in May 1696.**
- **The Great Recoinage of 1696:** triggered by widespread clipping and counterfeiting that had hollowed out the silver coinage. Newton oversaw a massive operation to call in old coin and reissue new milled coin with reeded edges (the security feature). The Mint operated at the Tower of London. Newton expected a quiet sinecure; he treated it as a serious office.

## 3. William Chaloner

- **Master counterfeiter and confidence trickster.** Charismatic, well-dressed, dangerous. Accused Newton's Mint of incompetence in pamphlets to deflect from his own crimes — i.e. tried to position himself as a Mint reformer while being its most prolific abuser.
- **Newton's pursuit:** Newton built the case personally — collecting depositions in coffee-houses and taverns, paying informants, working through the criminal underworld in London. Hugely unusual for a senior civil servant. Newton operated as an investigator-prosecutor.
- **Trial:** **3 March 1699** at the Old Bailey. Less than an hour. Found guilty of high treason (counterfeiting the coin of the realm).
- **Execution:** **hanged, drawn, and quartered at Tyburn on 22 March 1699.** Standard punishment for high treason at the time.
- **Editorial note:** the brutality of the punishment is historical fact. The page should not render the execution. Per HANDOFF safety-filter guidance, anchor on Newton at his Mint desk reading the death warrant; gallows abstractly in the window distance at dawn.

## 4. Robert Hooke and the *Opticks* delay

- **Hooke died 3 March 1703.** A bitter rival of Newton's since the 1670s correspondence on light and colour.
- **Newton elected President of the Royal Society:** **30 November 1703.** Held until his death.
- **Opticks published February 1704.** Full title: *Opticks: or, A Treatise of the Reflexions, Refractions, Inflexions and Colours of Light.* Newton had held it back for ~30 years.
- **Newton's stated reason for the delay (preface):** *"To avoid being engaged in Disputes about these Matters, I have hitherto delayed the printing, and should still have delayed it, had not the Importunity of Friends prevailed upon me."*
- **The experimentum crucis:** first prism splits white sunlight into a spectrum; second prism recombines it back to white. Demonstrates that white light is composite, not modified by the prism but separated.

## 5. Knighthood

- **Date: 16 April 1705.** Queen Anne knighted Newton at Trinity College, Cambridge during her visit.
- **Political context:** orchestrated by Newton's patron **Charles Montagu, Earl of Halifax** as part of an electioneering ploy for the May 1705 Cambridge parliamentary election. Newton was a candidate; the knighthood was meant to swing votes. **Newton lost the election anyway.**
- **First scientist knighted?** Disputed in detail (Christopher Wren predates him), but conventionally cited as a milestone — Newton was the first knighted *for science / for his civil scientific role* in the popular reading. Frame it carefully: "the first scientist most people remembered being knighted," not "the first scientist ever knighted."

## 6. Catherine Barton

- **Born 1679.** Daughter of Robert Barton and Hannah Smith (Newton's half-sister).
- **Moved in with Newton after May 1696,** aged ~17. She kept his London house until his death in 1727.
- **Married John Conduitt in 1717.** Conduitt later kept Newton's papers and wrote the first memoir of him.
- **Relationship to Charles Montagu (Earl of Halifax):** Halifax's will (1715) left her £3,000 and his jewels. The romantic nature of the relationship was widely rumoured at the time and is contested historiographically. **Kid-appropriate framing:** present her as Newton's intelligent niece and the warm centre of his London household; mention Halifax's bequest as the patron honouring her, without staging the romance.
- **Death:** 1739. Buried in Westminster Abbey with Conduitt, near Newton's tomb.

## 7. John Flamsteed and the *Historia Coelestis*

- **Flamsteed:** first Astronomer Royal. Older than Newton, sickly, severe, devout. Spent ~40 years compiling the star catalogue from observations at Greenwich.
- **The seizure (1712):** Newton, with Halley assisting, used royal/Royal Society pressure (Queen Anne, Prince George of Denmark) to force publication of Flamsteed's incomplete catalogue against his protest. 400 copies of *Historia Coelestis* printed; Newton edited it.
- **Flamsteed's recovery (1715–1716):** after Queen Anne's death (1714) and Halifax's death (May 1715), the political wind turned. Flamsteed acquired ~300 of the 400 copies and **burned them publicly in 1716** as "a sacrifice to heavenly truth."
- **Flamsteed's own edition** *Historia Coelestis Britannica* was published posthumously by his wife Margaret Flamsteed in 1725.

## 8. Leibniz priority war

- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646–1716): German philosopher and mathematician, polymath, court counsellor at Hanover. Independently invented calculus in the 1670s, published it 1684 — before Newton, who had developed his "method of fluxions" earlier (1660s) but kept it private.
- **Open dispute:** flared from ~1708 onwards. By 1711 Leibniz formally complained to the Royal Society of being accused of plagiarism.
- ***Commercium Epistolicum*** (the Royal Society "impartial" committee report): committee formed 1712; report published in Latin late 1712, English summary in *Philosophical Transactions* early 1713. **Newton secretly chaired the committee, selected its members (all his supporters, no Continental mathematicians), and drafted the verdict himself.** The committee never asked Leibniz to give his version.
- **Newton's anonymous Account of the Commercium Epistolicum** published 1715.
- **Leibniz died 14 November 1716** in Hanover, ostracised by the Hanoverian court (which had moved to London with George I in 1714). Newton outlived him by 11 years.

## 9. Royal Society presidency

- **Newton: President from 30 November 1703 until death 1727** (~24 years).
- **Style: autocratic.** Controlled younger scientists' careers. Removed Hooke's portrait and instruments (the "missing portrait" episode). Edmund Halley was a loyal lieutenant throughout.

## 10. The private Newton — alchemy and biblical chronology

- **Alchemy:** Newton wrote ~1 million words on alchemical material across his life. Most of it private. Conducted experiments at Cambridge and into the London years.
- **Biblical chronology and prophecy:** another ~1 million words on theological topics, including a private chronology of ancient kingdoms and an interpretation of the Book of Daniel. Newton held heterodox Arian views — he denied the Trinity — and concealed this his entire adult life because it would have cost him his Trinity fellowship and Lucasian chair.
- **Posthumous "discovery":** the Portsmouth Papers were auctioned at Sotheby's in **1936**; **John Maynard Keynes bought a portion** and famously declared: *"Newton was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians."*
- **Editorial decision (this volume):** alchemy gets one Kensington-by-candlelight page; the Arian heresy is mentioned briefly in caption on that same page, not made into a hero page.

## 11. South Sea Bubble 1720

- **Newton invested in the South Sea Company.** Sold his shares once at a profit, then reinvested at the peak. Total losses estimated at **~£20,000** — an enormous sum, perhaps £3-4 million in 2020s GBP.
- **The quote.** Recorded by William Stukeley in his memoir of Newton (drafted 1727, published 1752). Stukeley reports Newton saying he *"could not calculate the madness of people."* The famous longer version *"I can calculate the motion of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people"* is a slightly later paraphrase but is the version that has stuck culturally.
- **What to render in the page caption:** use the Stukeley-attested core *"I can calculate the motion of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people."* Flag in the script that this is the conventional rendering, attested by Stukeley.

## 12. Death and funeral

- **Died 20 March 1727 at Kensington, London.** Unmarried, intestate (his estate divided among Barton/Conduitt and the wider family).
- **Lay in state in the Jerusalem Chamber** at Westminster Abbey before the funeral.
- **Buried in Westminster Abbey on 28 March 1727** — the first scientist buried there with full state honours.
- **Pallbearers:** the Lord Chancellor, the Dukes of Montrose and Roxburgh, the Earls of Pembroke, Sussex, and Macclesfield.
- **Voltaire:** present in London at the time, attended the funeral, and famously wrote later that "the English nation buried their philosopher with the pomp due to a king." (His *Lettres philosophiques* / *Letters Concerning the English Nation* carried Newton's reputation to France.) Treat as historically grounded but use Voltaire as a single named witness in the crowd, not a major character.
- **Pope's epitaph (not on the tomb, but widely associated):** *"Nature and Nature's Laws lay hid in Night: / God said, 'Let Newton be!' and all was Light."*
