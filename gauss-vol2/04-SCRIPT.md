**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top band: `In Book One, a poor boy chose numbers over words. By 1801, at twenty-four, Carl Friedrich Gauss was famous across Europe for the purest mathematics there is — numbers studied for their own sake.`
- Bottom band: `But a question was turning in him. If numbers were this powerful on paper, what could they do out there — in the sky, across the land, inside the invisible forces of the world? This book is the story of the man who pointed his numbers at everything.`

## PAGE 2 — page-02.png · "A planet found by arithmetic." (the heavens)
**Ref:** ref_gauss_young.png. **Tier:** T4.
**Composition:** Night observatory / study. Gauss at a long brass telescope angled to the sky,
but his real attention is on a sheet of orbital calculations in his other hand — the point is
that he found the planet on PAPER, not through the lens. A small bright point of light marked
on a star chart. Cold blue night, warm lamp on the page.
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top-left box: `On the first night of 1801, an astronomer in Sicily had spotted a tiny new world between Mars and Jupiter — and then lost it in the glare of the Sun.`
- Mid-right box: `Gauss had never seen it. From just a few weeks of old measurements, he calculated where the lost world must reappear — and it did, almost exactly where his numbers said.`
- Bottom box: `They called it Ceres. A whole planet, recovered by arithmetic. For the first time, his numbers had reached the sky.`

## PAGE 3 — page-03.png · "Truth out of scattered dots." (least squares)
**Ref:** ref_gauss_young.png + (prose) a chalkboard/paper plot. **Tier:** T4.
**Composition:** Study desk. Gauss leaning over a sheet where a scatter of measured dots is
crossed by a single clean straight line drawn to split the difference between them — the
"best fit." His hand holds the ruler. Beside it the printed title of a book. Warm amber lamp.
The in-image plot must be clean: a handful of separated dots, one ruled line.
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top box: `Every real measurement is a little wrong. So how do you find the truth hidden inside a cloud of imperfect numbers?`
- Mid box (beside the plot): `Gauss's answer: draw the line that misses every dot by as little as possible. It is called the method of least squares — and it is still used today to turn messy data into knowledge.`
- Bottom box: `A French mathematician, Legendre, had published the idea first, in 1805. Gauss said he had been using it since he was a teenager. The two argued over who got there first for years.`

## PAGE 9 — page-09.png · "How to measure what you cannot reach." (TRIANGULATION — the DO-hook)
**Ref:** ref_triangulation.png (object) + (prose) Gauss mid sighting in background. **Tier:** T5. ANNOTATED-BREAKTHROUGH PAGE.
**Composition:** The heart-hook page. A clear annotated diagram dominates: a simple landscape
with a river or valley you cannot cross. A single big triangle — one side a measured BASELINE
along the near ground, two clean angle-arcs at its ends, the apex touching a far tower on the
opposite side. Labels large and separated, like a master's field sketch. Small inset: Gauss
(~45) at his theodolite sighting the far tower. A child must be able to copy this onto paper.
Do NOT crowd it.
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top band: `Here is the trick that let Gauss measure things he could never walk to — a mountain, the far side of a river, a distant church tower.`
- Label on the measured side: `BASELINE — pace out one distance you CAN measure`
- Label at each base angle: `MEASURE THIS ANGLE`
- Label at the apex/far tower: `the point you cannot reach`
- Bottom band: `With one known distance and two measured angles, a triangle is locked — and simple geometry gives you every other length. This is called triangulation. Cover the land in triangles, and you can map a whole country without crossing a single river. You can try it yourself with a ruler and a protractor.`

## PAGE 10 — page-10.png · "A net of triangles over a kingdom." (the survey)
**Ref:** ref_gauss_mid.png (LOCK) + ref_triangulation prose for the overlaid mesh. **Tier:** T4.
**Composition:** Open Hanover countryside, big weather sky. Gauss (~45) on a hilltop survey
station at a brass theodolite, an assistant nearby, a wooden signal-tower on the next hill.
Faint triangulation lines mesh across the whole landscape to distant hazy hills. Working
daylight, sun-and-cloud. The man of the desk now out in the wind and mud.
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top box: `Measuring the land like this is called geodesy — the science of the size and shape of the Earth.`
- Mid box: `Through the 1820s, Gauss surveyed the whole Kingdom of Hanover, hill by hill, triangle by triangle — years of patient work in all weather, far from his quiet study.`
- Bottom box: `He covered the country in an invisible net of triangles, each one measured, each one checked. He was, quite literally, measuring his corner of the world.`

## PAGE 11 — page-11.png · "A star made of sunlight." (the heliotrope)
**Ref:** ref_heliotrope.png (object, LOCK) + Gauss mid in prose. **Tier:** T4.
**Composition:** Hilltop, bright day. The heliotrope on its tripod — a small brass mirror-and-
telescope instrument — throws a single hot spark of reflected sunlight toward a far blue hill.
Gauss (~45) adjusting it, shading his eyes. On the distant hill, a tiny answering glint. The
one hot point of light against cool distance — make the spark vivid.
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top box: `To aim across many miles, Gauss needed to see a station too far away to make out. So in 1821 he invented a tool for it: the heliotrope.`
- Mid box (beside the instrument): `It is simply a mirror that catches the sun and flashes a beam of sunlight toward the distant surveyor — a tiny man-made star, visible for miles when nothing else could be seen.`
- Bottom box: `Across the hills of Hanover, his survey teams found each other by these sparks of reflected sunlight. Geometry, lit by the sun itself.`

## PAGE 12 — page-12.png · "The Earth will not lie flat." (curved surfaces / Theorema Egregium)
**Ref:** ref_gauss_mid.png (LOCK) + (prose) a globe and an orange peel / curved-triangle diagram. **Tier:** T4.
**Composition:** Study, ivory diagram light. Gauss (~45) at his desk with a small globe and a
flattened orange peel that will not lie flat without tearing; on the paper, a triangle drawn
on a curved surface whose angles bulge. The survey out in the world drove a deep question
back at the desk. Calm, scholarly, a quiet "aha."
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top box: `All that measuring led Gauss to a deep idea. On a flat map a triangle's angles add up to exactly 180 degrees — but on the curved Earth, they add up to a little more.`
- Mid box (beside the orange peel): `You cannot flatten an orange peel without tearing it. Its curving is built in — part of the surface itself, not something you can press away.`
- Bottom box: `Gauss proved this is true of any curved surface, and was so pleased he named it the Theorema Egregium — Latin for "the Remarkable Theorem." His triangles had taught him about the shape of space itself.`

- Mid box: `Together they turned to a new mystery: magnetism — the invisible force that swings a compass needle and reaches right through the Earth.`
- Bottom box (Gauss bubble, tail to older Gauss): `If we can measure the heavens and the land, we can measure this too.`

## PAGE 14 — page-14.png · "The first electric word." (the Gauss–Weber telegraph, 1833)
**Ref:** ref_telegraph.png (object, LOCK) + Gauss older & Weber in prose, or ref_gauss_older anchored. **Tier:** T5. ANNOTATED/HERO PAGE.
**Composition:** Night. Split sense of two rooms joined by one wire: the magnetism instrument
with its twitching needle in the foreground; through a high window, a single bare copper wire
runs out over the dark tiled Göttingen rooftops toward the distant lit observatory dome under
the stars. Gauss's (~55) hand at the needle, Weber at the far end implied. Warm glow of the
first signal. A small slip of paper shows a simple dot/line code.
**LETTERING — verbatim:**
- Top band: `In 1833 Gauss and Weber strung a bare copper wire over the rooftops of Göttingen — about a kilometre, from the observatory to Weber's physics laboratory.`
- Mid box (beside the needle): `An electric current sent down the wire made a magnetic needle twitch at the far end — left for one letter, right for another. A code of tiny movements.`
- Bottom band: `Down that wire they sent messages, letter by letter — one of the first electric telegraphs in the world. Years before the telegraph changed everything, two men in a German town were already sending words through a wire by the light of a lamp.`

- Mid box: `He never published it, fearing the argument it would cause. Then a letter arrived: the son of an old friend, a young man named János Bolyai, had discovered the very same thing — and published it.`

- Bottom box: `Gauss could not bring himself to praise the work — because it was his own discovery, found again by someone else. The man who measured the world had kept one of his deepest measurements hidden.`

- Epigraph box (ivory paper, dark serif ink), with English as the main text: `"It is not knowledge, but the act of learning... which grants the greatest enjoyment."` / helper beneath: `— Carl Friedrich Gauss, letter to a friend, 1808`
