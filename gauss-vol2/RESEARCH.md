## BEAT 1 — Ceres aftermath: Theoria Motus & least squares

- **Theoria Motus Corporum Coelestium in sectionibus conicis solem ambientium** — published **1809**. [HARD] (Wikipedia + MacTutor agree). A two-volume Latin treatise on the motion of celestial bodies (planets/comets) moving about the Sun in conic-section orbits. It systematized the orbit-determination methods Gauss had used to recover the lost dwarf planet **Ceres** in late 1801.
- **Method of least squares — the Legendre priority dispute** [HARD on the dispute existing; the underlying priority is genuinely DISPUTED by history]:
  - **Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method first, in 1805** (in his work on determining comet/planetary orbits; he coined the name "méthode des moindres carrés"). [HARD]
    - *Caution:* one search source gave Legendre's 1805 title as "Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des **comètes**." The standard title is **"Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des orbites des comètes."** Use the short English gloss "Legendre's 1805 work on determining comet orbits" in-image to avoid the title error. [DISPUTED wording — use gloss]
  - **Gauss published his version in 1809** in *Theoria Motus*, where he **claimed to have used the method since 1795**. [HARD]
  - Legendre was offended, wrote to Gauss objecting that priority claims need prior publication as proof; Gauss had no such publication. In **1820** Legendre publicly attacked Gauss's priority claim in a supplement to his 1805 memoir. [HARD] Often called the second-most-famous math priority dispute after Newton–Leibniz.
  - **In-image framing:** safe to say "Legendre published it first in 1805; Gauss said he'd been using it since 1795 — they fought over it for years." Do NOT flatly assert Gauss invented it first.

## BEAT 2 — The Duke of Brunswick's death

- **Charles William Ferdinand (Carl Wilhelm Ferdinand), Duke of Brunswick** — Gauss's lifelong patron/benefactor who had funded his education and stipend. [HARD]
- **Wounded at the Battle of Jena–Auerstedt, 14 October 1806** (he commanded Prussian forces at Auerstedt). [HARD] (two sources agree on battle + date).
- **Struck by a musket ball; lost both his eyes (blinded).** [HARD]
- **Died of his wounds on 10 November 1806, in Ottensen** (then near Hamburg / Altona). [HARD]
- **Consequence for Gauss:** the duchy was overrun/abolished and **Gauss's financial support (his ducal stipend) stopped.** This loss of patronage is a direct cause of his accepting the Göttingen post the following year. [HARD]

## BEAT 6 — Geodesy: the Hanover survey & the heliotrope

- **Tasked in 1818** with the geodetic survey (triangulation) of the **Kingdom of Hanover** (commissioned under George IV, King of the UK and of Hanover; intended to link to the existing Danish grid). [HARD]
- **Survey span: commonly given as 1820–1844** for the full geodetic-survey-plus-arc-measurement project; **Gauss personally did the most intensive field measurements ~1821–1825**, then directed refinement of the grid for years after. [HARD, but note the two figures:]
  - **[DISPUTED span]** Sources give the field/project window variously as "**1818** asked," "**1820–1844** survey + arc measurement," and "Gauss surveyed Hanover himself **1821–1825**." All are compatible (tasked 1818, project 1820–1844, hands-on fieldwork 1821–25). In-image, safest: "Through the 1820s Gauss surveyed the Kingdom of Hanover."
- **What triangulation is** (plain): cover the land in a mesh of imaginary triangles between hilltop stations; measure one baseline length and the angles, and trigonometry gives every other distance — so you can map a whole country precisely. [HARD]
- **THE HELIOTROPE:**
  - **Invented by Gauss in 1821.** [HARD] (Wikipedia "invented in 1821 by the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss.")
  - **How it works:** a **mirror that reflects/aims a focused beam of sunlight** from one survey station toward a distant observer, marking the exact position of a far station as a bright flash — visible when an ordinary target would be too small/invisible across many miles. Driven by the survey's need to sight stations over long distances. [HARD]
  - **Range:** routinely useful at tens of miles; the article cites an extreme documented sighting of **192 miles (309 km)** (Mt. St. Helena → Mt. Shasta, California, 1878 — NOT a Gauss-era figure, don't attribute that distance to Gauss). For Gauss's own work, render it as "a flash of sunlight visible across many miles." [HARD on mechanism; the 192-mi figure is a later third-party demonstration]

## BEAT 7 — Differential geometry

- **Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas** — **"General Investigations of Curved Surfaces."** [HARD title]
  - **Date: [DISPUTED 1827 vs 1828].** Wikipedia + the Theorema Egregium article say **proved/dated 1827**; MacTutor's Gauss biography says **published 1828**. Both are defensible (presented to the Göttingen society 1827, printed 1828). **In-image, write "1827" with the understanding that the printed edition is sometimes dated 1828** — or sidestep with "in the late 1820s." Flag, don't hide.
- **Theorema Egregium ("Remarkable Theorem"):**
  - **One plain sentence:** *The Gaussian curvature of a surface is intrinsic — it can be determined entirely by measurements made within the surface itself, and it does not change when the surface is bent without stretching.* [HARD]
  - Verbatim from source: "the Gaussian curvature is an intrinsic invariant of a surface" and "does not change if one bends the surface without stretching it." [HARD]
  - Kid-clear gloss (safe in-image): "You can't flatten an orange peel without tearing it — its curvature is built in. That's the Theorema Egregium."

## BEAT 8 — Magnetism & the telegraph with Wilhelm Weber

- **Wilhelm Eduard Weber** — born **24 October 1804 in Wittenberg**, son of a theology professor; from a scientifically gifted family (brothers Ernst Heinrich and Eduard Weber). [HARD]
- **Came to Göttingen in 1831** as professor of physics, age 27, **on Gauss's recommendation.** [HARD]
- **The electromagnetic telegraph — built 1833.** [HARD] World's first working electromagnetic telegraph for practical message-sending.
  - **Connected the Göttingen Observatory (Gauss) to the Physics building / Physical Cabinet (Weber).** [HARD]
  - **Length: [DISPUTED, ~1 km vs ~3 km].** Sources give different figures: one says "a roughly **1.2 km** double wire from observatory to the Physical Cabinet" (later extended ~60 m to the Gaußhaus); others say the wire ran "**three kilometres**" over rooftops; MacTutor-adjacent sources say messages over "**5000 ft**" (~1.5 km). **Safest in-image: "a wire strung over the rooftops of Göttingen, about a kilometre, from the observatory to Weber's physics lab."** Do not assert a single exact length. [DISPUTED]
  - It used induction signaling and a coded alphabet (an early binary-style code devised by Gauss & Weber). The line was destroyed by a lightning strike in 1845. [HARD]
- **Göttingen Magnetic Union / "Magnetischer Verein"** — founded by **Gauss and Weber in 1836** to coordinate **simultaneous, standardized magnetic observations** across a network of observatories (an early international scientific collaboration); produced the *Atlas des Erdmagnetismus* (atlas of geomagnetism). [HARD on existence/purpose; **1836 founding date is [TRAD]/lightly-sourced here — confirm before putting "1836" in a caption.** Wikipedia's Gauss/Weber pages confirm the Union and the atlas but I did not lock the exact founding year to two independent sources.] [PARTIALLY UNVERIFIED — year]
- **Unit work:** Gauss & Weber pioneered **absolute units** for magnetism (expressing magnetic quantities in terms of mass, length, time). The **CGS unit of magnetic flux density, the gauss, is named for him**; the **weber** (magnetic flux) is named for Weber. [HARD]

## BEAT 9 — Non-Euclidean geometry & Bolyai

- **Gauss privately developed non-Euclidean geometry but did not publish it**, fearing controversy (the "clamor of the Boeotians," in his phrase). He is also credited with **coining the term "non-Euclidean geometry."** [HARD that he developed it privately and named it; the "Boeotians" line is genuine Gauss but verify exact wording before in-image use.]
- **Farkas (Wolfgang) Bolyai** was Gauss's close friend from their Göttingen student days. His son **János (Johann) Bolyai** independently developed non-Euclidean geometry, published **1832 as an appendix ("Appendix") to his father's *Tentamen*.** [HARD]
- **Gauss's reaction:** in 1831 Farkas sent his son's work; **Gauss replied in a letter dated 6 March 1832** (see Beat 10 for verbatim). He privately said the young man was a genius of the first order, but his refusal to praise (because it duplicated his own unpublished work) deeply wounded the young Bolyai. [HARD]

## BEAT 10 — Verbatim quotes (with source + date)

1. **Farkas Bolyai letter, "act of learning"** — [HARD, with a sourcing caveat]
   > "It is not knowledge, but the act of learning, not possession but the act of getting there, which grants the greatest enjoyment."
   - **Original German:** "Wahrlich es ist nicht das Wissen, sondern das Lernen, nicht das Besitzen sondern das Erwerben, nicht das Da-Seyn, sondern das Hinkommen, was den grössten Genuss gewährt."
   - **Source/date:** Letter from Gauss to **Farkas (Wolfgang) Bolyai, 2 September 1808.** Cited via G. Waldo Dunnington, *Carl Friedrich Gauss: Titan of Science.*
   - **Caveat:** date **2 September 1808** is consistently given by quote databases but I did not confirm it against a primary archival source; treat the **wording as HARD, the exact day as [TRAD]**. (Note this is 1808, just outside your 1801-onward window's start but fine for Book Two.)

2. **On Bolyai / non-Euclidean geometry ("praise myself")** — [HARD]
   > "If I commenced by saying that I am unable to praise this work, you would certainly be surprised for a moment. But I cannot say otherwise. To praise it would be to praise myself. Indeed the whole contents of the work, the path taken by your son, the results to which he is led, coincide almost entirely with my meditations, which have occupied my mind partly for the last thirty or thirty-five years."
   - Common shorter rendering: "I cannot praise it, for to praise it would be to praise myself."
   - **Source/date:** Letter from Gauss to **Farkas Bolyai, 6 March 1832**, on receiving János Bolyai's *Appendix*. [HARD — date appears across multiple sources.]
   - *Translation note:* multiple English translations circulate (wording of the first clause varies: "I cannot praise it" / "you will be shocked for a moment"). Pick ONE translation and keep it; the **6 March 1832** date and the "praise myself / thirty to thirty-five years" substance are solid.

3. **"Pauca sed matura"** — [HARD]
   - **Meaning: "Few, but ripe."** Gauss's personal **seal motto** (his seal showed a tree bearing a few ripe fruits — some descriptions say seven fruits). It expressed his publishing philosophy: release nothing until it is complete and mature. [HARD on meaning + that it was his seal; "seven fruits" detail is [TRAD].]

4. **Other genuinely-sourced Gauss lines usable in-image (verify final wording before lettering):**
   - **"Mathematics is the queen of the sciences."** ("Die Mathematik ist die Königin der Wissenschaften.") — widely attributed to Gauss via Sartorius von Waltershausen's memorial; [TRAD/HARD-ish]. *(Per Book One memory, the team chose NOT to lean on the "queen of sciences" framing for the teenage window; for Book Two it's period-appropriate but still verify.)*
   - **"Boeotians" line** on why he wouldn't publish non-Euclidean geometry (fear of the "clamor of the Boeotians") — genuine Gauss sentiment, **confirm exact wording** before in-image use. [UNVERIFIED exact text]
   - Recommend: in-image, lean on quotes 1–3 (each has a clear letter + date). Treat 4 as decoration only if exact wording is confirmed.

## BEAT 11 — Appearance in middle age (rendering reference, ~1801 age 24 → ~1828 age 51)

Real portraits exist — render him aging across the window, anchored on these:
- **Documented adult appearance:** noted by contemporaries for his **"penetrating blue eyes."** [TRAD/HARD-ish — widely repeated.] Broad, high forehead; full but not heavy face in middle age, becoming jowlier and white-haired in old age.
- **Key portraits to anchor on (all real):**
  - **Ritmüller portrait — engraved 1828** (head-and-shoulders; Gauss ~51). Good middle-of-window anchor.
  - **Christian Albrecht Jensen oil portrait, 1840** (the best-known image; painted for Pulkovo Observatory; Gauss ~63 — older than your window but the definitive face). Shows him in **dark formal coat**; the **velvet cap** he is popularly associated with appears in later imagery of him at his desk. [HARD that Jensen 1840 + Ritmüller 1828 exist; the "velvet cap" is genuinely associated with Gauss's late-life desk portraits — render it as an old-age attribute, not necessarily 1820s.]
- **Rendering guidance for the aging arc:**
  - **~1805–1810 (age ~28–33, marriage/grief years):** darker hair, fuller face, no cap, formal dark coat with high collar/cravat — younger and sharper than the famous portraits.
  - **~1820s (age ~43–51, survey/geometry years):** graying, receding hair, high forehead, the Ritmüller-1828 face; outdoors-survey context can show him in a heavier coat.
  - **~1830s (age ~53+, Weber/magnetism years, after Minna's death):** white hair, jowlier, the Jensen/desk-portrait face, and the **velvet cap** is appropriate here.
  - Keep the **blue eyes** consistent across all phases as the signature lock.
