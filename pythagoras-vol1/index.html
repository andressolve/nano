Test Your Understanding

1. What did the young Pythagoras notice at the blacksmith's forge?
Exactly. He weighed the hammers and found the ones that sounded well together had weights in simple ratios — 12, 9, 8, 6. The octave, fifth, and fourth in music are all hiding in those numbers. His first great idea: harmony is number.
Not quite. Pythagoras noticed the hammers that sounded harmonious together had weights in simple ratios — 12, 9, 8, 6. That was the seed of his whole life's idea: the world is made of number.
2. Why did Pythagoras leave Samos the first time?
Right. His teacher Pherecydes told him Samos had grown small under Polycrates — that Egypt knew shapes before the Greeks did. At twenty, Pythagoras packed a bundle and sailed for Memphis.
Not quite. The tyrant Polycrates watched every free man on Samos, and Pherecydes told Pythagoras the Egyptians had known geometry for thousands of years. So he left for Egypt to learn.
3. What did the Egyptian rope-stretchers show him?
Yes! The harpedonaptai laid a rope with twelve knots into a 3-4-5 triangle in the sand — and the corner was always square. When Pythagoras asked why, the priest answered: “We are builders. You are the question-asker.” That triangle traveled with him for twenty years.
Not quite. The Egyptian rope-stretchers showed him that twelve knots arranged 3-4-5 make a perfect right angle — every time. But they never explained why it worked. That question became the work of his life.
4. Where did Pythagoras go after Egypt?
Right. When Cambyses of Persia invaded, the Egyptian temples fell and the foreign scholars were taken east. In Babylon, the Magi taught Pythagoras astronomy — they had been recording the sky in numbers for a thousand years.
Not quite. He was taken to Babylon after the Persian king Cambyses conquered Egypt. There the Magi taught him astronomy — how to read the sky in numbers. He stayed for years.
5. Why did Pythagoras leave Samos the SECOND time?
Yes! A cave could not hold what he had come home to build. He had been home six months. The Greek cities in southern Italy — Magna Graecia — were rich, new, and open to ideas. So he crossed the sea again, at forty, to build his school in Croton.
Not quite. After returning home, he taught a few students in a small cave — but it was plainly too small. Samos could not hold the school he imagined. So he sailed west to Croton in Magna Graecia, where he would finally build it. Volume 2 picks up the morning he arrives.
