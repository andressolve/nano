# Volume Architecture — 49 pages

*(Drafted at 48. At script round 3 page 28 was split into 28 (the monk's turn at the pillar) and 29 (the tiger); former 29–48 are 30–49.)*

Mythic China, some months after Volume II. The road west over White Tiger
Ridge; Flower-Fruit Mountain far in the east, on the coast; a pine wood with a
golden pagoda in it, further west.

---

## I — THE RIDGE · pp 1–5

**Emotional entry:** ease. **Exit:** a staff coming down on a smiling girl.

The company on the road, months past the sand river, re-introduced as they
are now: the monk on the white horse that used to be a dragon, the pig with
the luggage, the river ogre at the monk's back, and the monkey running ahead
with a gold band on his head that tightens when the monk says three words,
and eyes that see the real face under any face. On a white crag a demon made
of bone watches them: one bite of this monk, she has heard, and she will
never die. The monk is hungry; there is nothing on the ridge. Wukong will
fetch peaches from a mountain far to the south and orders everyone to stay
put; Bajie grumbles about being bossed. The demon pulls on a girl's face and
walks down with a basket of rice. The monk is polite; Bajie is in love with
the basket. Wukong drops out of the sky with his arms full of peaches, sees
the skull, and the staff is already coming down.

**The turn:** the demon wants the monk, and the only one who can see her is
not there when she comes.
**Page-turn out:** the staff is falling.

## II — THREE FACES · pp 6–19

**Emotional entry:** outrage. **Exit:** a monkey on a cloud flying east,
crying, the band still on.

The girl falls; a thin white mist slips out of her and away, and only Wukong
and the reader see it. The monk sees a dead girl. Wukong: it is an empty
skin; look in her basket. The rice is maggots, the noodles are frogs. Bajie,
who was once fooled by Wukong in a bride's face, tells the monk the monkey can
make a basket look like anything. The monk, horrified, says the words. Wukong
on the ground, holding his head; the monk stops; never again. An old woman
comes down the ridge with a cane, crying for her daughter; Bajie says it is
the girl's mother. Wukong sees the same skull, sees her bone hands reach for
the monk, and strikes; the mist escapes again. The monk says the words over
and over until Wukong rolls in the dust. In the afternoon they walk in
silence; Wukong walks behind for the first time. Then an old man with prayer
beads, calling for his wife and daughter. This time Wukong asks first —
Master, it's her, let me — and the monk says no. The old man's hand is out
for the monk. Wukong chooses: send me away afterwards, you'll be alive to do
it. He is faster than the mist this time, and the old man falls into a heap of
white bones with her name cut across the skull. Bajie: a trick. The monk
says the words, and then: go; you are not my disciple. "Take the band off,
then." He does not know any words for that. Wukong makes three more of
himself so the monk must receive his bow from every side, tells Wujing to
walk at the monk's back, and goes.

**The turn:** the monk chooses his rule over his monkey, and the monkey is
sent away for saving him.
**Page-turn out:** a monkey crying on a cloud, and a mountain in the east.

## III — HOME · pp 20–22

**Emotional entry:** noise. **Exit:** a monkey on a peak at sunset, a hand
shading his eyes, looking west.

Flower-Fruit Mountain, the waterfall, the cave behind it: the monkeys pour out
screaming with joy. Their king is home. Wukong is crowned with flowers and
announces it: no monk, no words, no walking. He tries to pull the band off and
cannot. A young monkey tugs at the hemp robe to take it off him, and he snaps:
leave the robe. The days at home, told: peaches, swimming, the throne. He was
happy, he told everyone. Every evening he climbed the peak and looked west.

**The turn:** he is home and free and still wearing the robe.
**Page-turn out:** meanwhile, on the road, the monk has no one at the front.

## IV — THE YELLOW ROBE · pp 23–34

**Emotional entry:** unease. **Exit:** a pig on a cloud, heading east.

On the road the company is quieter without Wukong. Bajie goes to beg food and
falls asleep in a ditch; Wujing goes to find him and leaves the monk with the
horse. The monk sees a golden pagoda gleaming in a pine wood and walks in to
ask for food. On a stone bed inside, the Yellow Robe is asleep: huge,
indigo-faced, a scimitar across his knees. One eye opens. Caught, the monk
tells the demon he may eat him but may not hurt anyone else, and the demon
laughs. Bajie and Wujing come shouting to the gate and lose: Bajie runs into
the reeds, Wujing is dragged in and tied. The Yellow Robe, who thinks a monk
who will not hurt anything is the funniest thing he has ever caught, spits a
spell of water on him and makes him a tiger: let's see how gentle you are with
claws; when the tiger eats meat, I will eat the tiger. In an iron cage, three
days, the tiger turns its face from the meat. At night the white horse,
tethered in the wood, becomes the white dragon he used to be and goes in, and
is cut across the foreleg and driven out. At dawn Bajie crawls out of the
reeds and finds the horse lying in the stream, and the horse speaks, for the
first time since he joined: go and get our big brother. Bajie: he won't come;
I lied about the bones. The horse: then tell him that.

**The turn:** without Wukong nobody can save the monk, and the one who must go
for him is the one who sent him away with a lie.
**Page-turn out:** a pig on a wobbling cloud over the sea.

## V — GOING BACK · pp 35–40

**Emotional entry:** dread. **Exit:** two clouds racing west, the monkey's
in front.

Bajie has never gone back for anyone and flies badly. At Flower-Fruit Mountain
the monkeys drag him before their king, who pretends not to know any pigs.
Bajie lies — Master misses you, he sent me — and Wukong's eyes see through it
as they saw through the demon. Bajie tells the truth: he lied about the bones
because he wanted to be the one the monk liked best; now the monk is a tiger
in a cage, Wujing is tied up, the horse is hurt, and the horse talked. Wukong:
he sent me away; he said the words three times. Bajie: I know; I came back for
you anyway, and I hate flying. Wukong's hand goes to the mended patch on his
shoulder. Somebody always has to go back. He goes.

**The turn:** the monkey chooses to go back for the one who sent him away.
**Page-turn out:** the golden pagoda, and the monkey at its gate.

## VI — THE STAR IN THE PAGODA · pp 41–49

**Emotional entry:** fury. **Exit:** the road west, the monk walking beside
the monkey.

Wukong hammers on the pagoda gate: come out and meet the monk's first
disciple. The fight the others could not win. Wukong sees, under the demon's
face, a star: he lived in Heaven once and knows one when he sees it. The
Yellow Robe, seen, flies up and becomes a star again, and Wukong — who would
once have chased him across the sky for the glory — lets him go: he has a monk
to get. In the hall, the cage: the tiger shrinks from him. You didn't eat the
meat. Shapes are his trick; he takes this one off. The monk, a man again, on
his knees in front of him: I couldn't see her face; you could; I should have
believed you. Bajie, who came in behind, tells the monk the truth about the
bones himself. The monk's new promise: if you tell me a face is a demon's, I
will believe you, even when I can't see it. The horse, asked to talk again,
does not. In the morning they go on west, and the monk walks beside the
monkey, and when the next stranger comes smiling down the road, he looks first
at Wukong.

**The turn:** the monk sees Wukong's real face.
**Page-turn out:** none. The road goes on.

## The two structural rules this architecture is built to satisfy

**Emotional duration.** Grief, mechanism, and revelation never share a page.
Every consequential beat gets its aftermath before the plot moves — including
the protagonist's: the first words get p8; the second get p11–12; the
banishment gets pp 18–19 and all of Movement III; the tiger gets p29; the
horse's wound gets p32 before its words on p33; the monk's restoration gets
p46 before the promise on p47.

**One dominant turn per page.** Every page in `07-PAGE-CONTRACT.md` names the
one thing that changed. A page whose turn cannot be stated in one sentence
comes back here, not to the prompt.
