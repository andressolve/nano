# Script gate, round 4: Monkey King, Volume III, *Sent Away*

Files read: `books/monkey-king-vol3/08-FULL-SCRIPT.md`, read cold from start to finish before anything else; then `07-PAGE-CONTRACT.md`, `qa/script/audit-r04.txt` and `04-CHARACTER-LEDGER.md`. I did not open any earlier round report.

## 1. Cold read

On a white ridge, Wukong runs ahead of the monk Sanzang, Bajie the pig, Wujing the river ogre, and a white horse that used to be a dragon. Page 1 sets up three things the whole book depends on:
- Wukong may not fly the monk; the road is the monk's to walk.
- A gold band squeezes Wukong's head whenever the monk says "Gold, hold fast."
- Wukong's eyes see the real face under any disguise.

A skull-faced demon watches from a crag. She wants to eat the monk, because one bite means living forever.

While Wukong flies off for peaches, the demon comes to the hungry monk as a girl with a basket of rice. Wukong drops out of the sky and kills her. Only he and the reader see the skull and the escaping mist; the monk sees a dead girl. Bajie whispers that Wukong is playing a shape trick, and the monk says the words.

The demon returns as the girl's weeping mother. Wukong strikes again, and the monk says the words over and over and gives a final warning. Then she comes as an old man at prayer. This time Wukong asks first and is refused. He chooses anyway ("Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it"), and this time he is faster than the mist. A heap of bones lies on the road with WHITE BONE LADY carved on the skull. Bajie says Wukong scratched it on, and the monk sends Wukong away. Wukong bows to him from four sides at once, tells Wujing to guard the monk's back, and cries all the way home.

At Flower-Fruit Mountain he is crowned king again. He can't get the band off and won't give up the robe the monk patched. Every evening he looks west and says he is checking the weather.

Without him, the monk sends Wujing off after Bajie and walks alone into a golden pagoda that belongs to the Yellow Robe. The Yellow Robe beats the pig and the ogre. Tied to a pillar, the monk admits his mistake to Wujing. The demon bets the monk will turn beast if given claws, and sprays him into a tiger. The tiger turns its face from the meat. That night the horse becomes a dragon, tears in through the window, is cut, and is driven into the stream. At dawn the horse speaks to Bajie and sends him for "our big brother."

Bajie flies east on a sagging cloud, practising the truth. At the cave he tries a lie first, is seen through, and then confesses: he lied about the bones because he wanted to be the favourite. Wukong goes back. He fights the Yellow Robe, sees the runaway star inside him, and threatens to tell Heaven. The star flies home. Wukong frees Wujing, finds the monk's face inside the tiger, and sprays him back into a man. The monk apologizes and Bajie confesses to him. The monk promises to believe Wukong even when he can't see it, and the horse refuses to talk again. On the road next day a woman with a basket comes toward them. The monk looks to Wukong first, and Wukong says it's just lunch.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p16:** I stopped briefly to ask why a name on a skull should count as proof to the monk. I recovered quickly, because the real evidence is bones where an old man stood (see observation 2).
- **p44:** "He's going home to the sky before Heaven counts tonight." I paused over whether this counts as punishment or escape. The shutter (p30) and "nobody ever comes looking for me" (p25) carry it (see observation 4).
- **p49:** the panel has Bajie and Wujing sharing the luggage pole on the same day the monk told Bajie to carry the whole thing (Finding 1).

Otherwise the book read straight through with no rereading. I could retell it from memory after one pass.

## 2. Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book, and I wanted to turn its pages.

**Movement I (pp 1–5).** It opens fast with a clear appetite. The monk is hungry, the demon is hungry, and Wukong is restless. "I can't fly you anywhere, but I can fly peaches" pays off page 1's rule with a joke. Bajie's "I'll say yes for you" is pure Bajie. The page-turns are a real threat each time.

**Movement II (pp 6–19).** This is the heart of the book, and it hurts in the right way.
- The heat is exact: a disciple who can see the truth, and a master who can only see what he sees. Each of them is right by his own lights.
- The escalation is built with real craft. First a blow without a word, then the mist, then "don't you dare," then Wukong asking first, then choosing knowing the price: "You'll be alive to do it."
- The monk's "I don't know any words for that" is the best line in the book. It is evasion, grief and a real fact all at once.
- The attribution-strip test passes easily. Bajie whines and flatters, Wujing says almost nothing ("I'll walk at his back"), the monk is polite and stubborn, and Wukong is blunt.
- Want-from works on every page. Bajie wants the monk to doubt the monkey. The monk wants Wukong to stop. Wukong wants to be believed. The demon wants to get the monk off the horse.

**Movement III (pp 20–22).** Short and well judged. The subtext ("He told the monkeys he was looking at the weather"; "Leave the robe alone") carries Wukong's want without anyone stating it.

**Movement IV (pp 23–34).** The protagonist is gone for thirteen pages and the book holds anyway, because new appetites take over:
- The Yellow Robe's lazy cruelty ("May not! A monk tied to my pillar tells me what I may not do!").
- The monk's courage ("you may not hurt my friends").
- The monk's turn at the pillar (p28), which gets a silent panel to land.
- The tiger's refusal (p30), which is enacted, not narrated.

The horse speaking (pp 33–34) is a genuine surprise and reveals character ("Only when it matters"). "I couldn't beat that demon. Neither can you" is a real want aimed at Bajie.

**Movement V (pp 35–40).** Bajie becomes the book's second protagonist here, and it is the most satisfying turn in the volume. He lies, gets caught ("I don't need magic eyes to see through you"), then tells the truth, and the truth has a motive planted on page 3 ("Who made the monkey the boss?"). "I hate flying, and I hate you bossing me, and I came all the way back for you anyway!" is honest, funny and moving at once. Wukong's decision is played as subtext: the hand on the patch, the crown falling, and "On a cloud like yours."

**Movement VI (pp 41–49).** The climax is won by the book's own device, the eyes, rather than by strength, which is the right choice. Wukong not chasing the star ("I've got a monk to get") shows his growth through action. "You can't even be a tiger properly" stops the reunion from turning soppy. "Then we'll be wrong together" is specific to these two, not an aphorism. The last page echoes the basket and turns the whole book in one glance.

**What could be stronger.** The fight at p42 is one image with a recap caption, so the "fight nobody else could win" gets less room than the build-up promises. This is acceptable because the real climax is the seeing on p43, not the fighting.

I found no logistics dialogue and no aphorism dialogue. The Yellow Robe's "Everybody's a beast underneath" is the villain's own creed, and the tiger refutes it on the page.

## 3. Mandatory findings

**1. p49: the luggage pole contradicts p48.**
- **Defect:** On p48 the monk gives Bajie his punishment: "You can carry the whole luggage pole today." Bajie accepts: "That's fair, Master." On p49, the same day, the dominant panel shows "Bajie and Wujing behind with the luggage pole."
- **Reader harm:** The penalty the reader just watched Bajie accept disappears in the next image, with no reason given. The reader has to decide whether the monk relented, Wujing quietly helped, or the book forgot. That is solving the adaptation, on the final page, where every detail is read as the book's last word. This panel note will become the image, and nothing at the page gate will catch it, because the card will match the script.
- **Required fix (pick one):**
  - **(a) Keep the punishment.** Restage the p49 panel as: "Bajie behind, carrying the whole luggage pole alone, both bundles swinging, grumbling; Wujing walking beside him with only his crescent spade." Optionally add one prose sentence that echoes page 1, e.g. "Bajie carried the whole luggage pole and complained about it, and nobody minded that either." The page goes from 93 to about 108 words, well inside the prose budget.
  - **(b) Make the help a story beat.** If Wujing is meant to take one end out of kindness, write it in: a prose clause such as "After the first hill, Wujing took one end without being asked," or a panel note to the same effect. That lets the reader see it as Wujing's choice.

## 4. Nonblocking observations

1. **Five-word audit.** I audited all 16 balloons the tool listed. Every one is a complete thought or a direct answer to the line or visible action just before it, with only one possible referent. "Wukong, don't you dare" (p9) follows "Same skull, under a different face" with the staff already shown twice, so the object is clear. "He was sent away" (p26) gives the Yellow Robe the fact his next move depends on, and it is forceful, not clipped. "And what if I'm wrong?" (p48) is an exact answer to "I'll believe you." None fail.

2. **p16, the carved skull.** Wukong says "Read it!" as though the name proves she is a demon, but the monk has never heard of a White Bone Lady. The reader accepts it as fairy-tale logic, and the bones are the real evidence. Optional fix: have Wukong say "A demon's name, on a demon's skull. Read it!" so it is clear why he thinks a name is proof.

3. **p47, "and nobody could see it."** Two pages earlier Wukong *did* see the monk inside the tiger ("I'd know your face under any face"). Changing the line to "…and nobody could see it but you. That's what I did to you." removes a small contradiction and makes the apology hit harder, because it names the gift the monk refused.

4. **p44, the star's fate.** Bajie's "He's getting away!" is a fair objection, and Wukong's answer depends on the reader remembering the shutter (p30) and "nobody ever comes looking for me" (p25). It works. If there's room, half a clause would make going home read clearly as the defeat he feared, e.g. "…going home to the sky, where he's been hiding from all along, before Heaven counts tonight."

5. **Mode balance.** The audit counts 30 dramatic, 7 prose and 12 spectacle pages: 61% / 14% / 24% against the roughly 60 / 20 / 13 target. The dramatic share is on target and 39% of pages are non-dramatic, so the book breathes as intended, but prose is light and spectacle heavy. Pages 31, 32 and 40 carry narrating captions and work partly as prose. No change required.

6. **p1 is at 140 words,** the exceptional ceiling for a prose page. That's allowed, but it is the densest page in the book, so the builder prompt should protect its reading field.

7. **p42's page-turn line,** "the monkey has not lost yet," is the weakest in the book. The caption's list of who could not beat him ("The pig could not… The dragon could not.") does build suspense, though. Optional: end the caption with "The monkey had not finished."

8. **Ledger checked against the script.** Every character's on-page behaviour matches their ledger want:
   - Wujing's secret want is answered on p45 ("Nobody could have kept him from that demon").
   - Bajie's want to be brave and honest where someone sees is met on pp 39 and 47.
   - The horse speaks only on pp 33–34 and refuses on p48.
   - Every returning face and rule is reintroduced in the story before the plot depends on it: the band and its three words, the eyes, the needle-staff, Bajie's exile from Heaven, the horse being a dragon, "big brother," and the patched robe.
   
   The ledger's state (b) page numbers (tiger on "pp 28–30") lag the script by one page, as the brief warned; the script governs.

Once Finding 1 is fixed, I expect this script to be approvable.
