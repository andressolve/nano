VERDICT: REVISE

# Script gate, round 2: Monkey King, Volume III, *Sent Away*

## Cold read

I read the 48 pages once, in order, before opening any other project document.

**The story as a first-time reader would tell it.** Months after the river of sand, the monk, his three disciples and his horse are crossing a bare white ridge. The monk is starving. Wukong flies off for peaches and tells the monk not to move. A skull-faced demon on the crag wants one bite of the monk so she can live forever. She puts on a girl's face and brings a basket of "rice and noodles." Wukong drops out of the sky and kills the girl without a word. The girl turns out to be an empty skin, and the basket is full of maggots and frogs. Bajie says Wukong faked it, and the monk says the words that squeeze the band. The demon comes back as the girl's weeping mother. Wukong strikes again, and the monk says the words over and over and warns him: one more and you're not my disciple.

Then comes the old man. This time Wukong asks first. The monk says no. Wukong chooses the monk's life over his own place: "Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it." The bones have WHITE BONE LADY carved on the skull. Bajie whispers that Wukong scratched it on, and the monk sends Wukong away. The band stays on, because the monk knows no words to take it off. Wukong bows four ways, makes Wujing promise to walk at the monk's back, and cries the whole way home.

At home he is crowned, can't get the band off, won't give up the patched robe, and looks west every evening, "at the weather." On the road, Wujing leaves the monk alone to find Bajie. The monk walks into a pagoda hoping for supper, and a huge demon catches him. Bajie and Wujing are beaten, and the demon turns the monk into a tiger to prove that "everybody's a beast underneath." The tiger won't touch the meat. That night the horse becomes a dragon and tries a rescue. The demon cuts the dragon's leg, and at dawn the horse speaks for the first time and sends Bajie for "our big brother," with the truth. Bajie lies, is seen through, and then confesses: he was jealous and he knew. Wukong goes back with him, and waits for the pig's slow cloud the whole way. At the pagoda Wukong sees a runaway star inside the demon and threatens to tell Heaven. The demon flees upward. Wukong turns the tiger back into the monk. The monk apologizes and Bajie confesses. On the last page, a woman with a basket comes down the road, and the monk looks at Wukong first.

**Where I stopped, reread or guessed:**
- **pp 6, 10, 11: I stopped.** The monk is kneeling next to what the script stages as a flat, hollow skin, and Wukong says "that's an empty skin." Why doesn't the monk see it? Can he see the mist? p10's "Master, did you see the mist?" suggests not, but nothing on p6 says so.
- **pp 24–29: I guessed.** I didn't know what to call the pagoda demon until the narrator calls him "The Yellow Robe" on p29, as if I already knew. On p40 Wukong shouts "Yellow Robe!" and I wondered who told him.
- **p24 against p26: I reread.** The staff falls on the threshold of the hall doorway inside the pagoda, then lies on the threshold of the wood's iron gate outside. On p26 the gate is open, Bajie bangs on it, and then it "swings open."
- **p23: I guessed.** The monk is left alone because of one errand line. I didn't feel Wujing break his promise; I was only told he left.
- **p43: I stopped.** Bajie yells "He's getting away!" and the picture shows the demon becoming "one bright star among the first stars." He feared Heaven, so why does he fly into the sky? Did he escape, or go home? I had to work out the villain's fate myself.
- **p28: small pause.** Why does the monk believe Wukong now? I inferred it from being fooled by the pagoda. It holds, but only just (nonblocking).

## Is it alive?

Yes, and much of it is very good. This is a book I would want read to me.

**Movement I (pp 1–5): lively and quick.** p1 carries a lot, but the prose is clear and warm. Two lines plant the monk's hunger and his vow ("I've never let anything die for my supper"), and the cost of the climax is built in. Bajie's "Who made the monkey the boss?" plants his motive, which pays off on p37. That is good craft. p5 is a strong spectacle page.

**Movement II (pp 6–19): the heart of the book, and genuinely painful.** Three things build on each other: the rising pain of the words, Wukong walking behind for the first time (p12), and "I'm asking you first this time." "Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it" is the best line in the volume. It is a choice, it has heat, and only Wukong could say it. "I don't know any words for that" is quietly devastating. So are the four bowing Wukongs, and "He cried the whole way home." Wants are sharp everywhere: Wukong wants to be believed, the monk wants to be good, Bajie wants to be the favourite. Subtext is present: Bajie's whispering, and the monk's "I saw a girl with a basket." The movement's one weakness is a fairness defect (Finding 1). If the monk can see a hollow skin and still says "her," he looks obtuse rather than tragically blind. That would spoil the best movement.

**Movement III (pp 20–22): short and exactly right.** The patch line and "He told the monkeys he was looking at the weather" show feeling through action instead of naming it.

**Movement IV (pp 23–33): the weakest in heat.** The pagoda capture works. The demon's voice is distinct ("May not! A monk tied to my pillar…"), and his creed has a body in the tiger who won't eat. p29 is lovely. The horse speaking on pp 32–33 is a delight. But:
- The movement opens on an errand (Finding 2).
- Its villain goes unnamed for five pages (Finding 3).
- pp 26–27 and 30–31 are captioned action with little at stake between people.
- The middle runs on mechanism, and its geography contradicts itself (Finding 4).

**Movement V (pp 34–39): the best-written movement.** Bajie's flight ("He practised the truth all the way there. It sounded terrible every time") and his lie being seen through are both strong. The confession is the funny character stopping being funny, as the ledger promises, and "I hate flying, and I hate you bossing me, and I came all the way back for you anyway!" is pure voice. "He waited for the pig" forgives Bajie through action without a speech. This movement is alive.

**Movement VI (pp 40–48): satisfying.** Winning by sight rather than strength links the climax to the eyes the monk refused to trust, which is the right resolution. "You can't even be a tiger properly," "Then we'll be wrong together," and the horse refusing to talk again are all earned. The last page is a perfect echo. One defect: the villain's exit is ambiguous (Finding 5).

**Attribution-strip:** passes throughout. Each voice is distinct: Wukong is blunt, and laughs except when he doesn't. Sanzang is polite, plain and stubborn. Bajie whines and flatters. Wujing is flat, and says the worst about himself first. The Yellow Robe is lazy and cruel. The horse is plain. I found no aphorism dialogue. The one logistics line is Finding 2.

## Mandatory findings

**1. pp 6, 7, 10, 11: the rule for what the monk can see is never set, so his disbelief reads as stupidity rather than blindness.**
- **Defect.** p6 panel 1 stages the fallen girl as "flat and hollow and wrong, like a skin a cicada leaves behind," with the mist and skull streaming out. Nothing says the monk can't see it. He is "on his knees near the girl." Wukong tells him, "That's not a girl, Master, that's an empty skin." The monk never answers that, and Bajie's argument on p7 covers only the maggots. p10 repeats the problem: the old woman folds "like an empty skin" in front of the monk. p11 places "the old woman's empty skin" in a neutral wide shot. Only p15 marks its vision "for Wukong and the reader only." The planning docs say the mist is Wukong-only, but the skin is unruled in the script and the docs alike, and the reader never sees the docs.
- **Reader harm.** The reader sees the monk look at a deflated skin and call it "her." Either the monk is refusing evidence in plain sight (which makes him a fool, not a tragic figure), or the reader has to invent a rule the book never stated. The whole moral weight of pp 16–17 and p46 ("I couldn't see her face, but you could") depends on the monk truly not seeing it.
- **Required fix.** Set one rule and stage it the same way on pp 6, 10, 11 and 15. Through the monk's eyes the disguise lies still, a body. The hollowness and the mist appear only inside the flare of Wukong's red-ringed eyes, framed as his view, as p15 already does. Use the same staging on p10 panel 2 and in the p11 wide shot ("the old woman lying still at the road's edge"). If the skin must stay visible to everyone, Bajie has to explain it away on p7 too: "If he can make a girl look like an empty skin, he can make rice look like maggots." The first fix is better.

**2. p23: the monk is left alone because of a logistics line, and Wujing's broken promise happens without a choice.**
- **Defect.** "Bajie went to beg food an hour ago, Master. I'll go and find him. Stay here with the horse." That is pure arrangement. On p18 Wujing promised, "I'll walk at his back." p28 and p44 hinge on him having broken that promise. Here he breaks it without hesitating or being pushed, so there is nothing at stake between him and the monk.
- **Reader harm.** Wujing's guilt on p28 ("I didn't keep you") and the monk's answer ("No, Wujing. I sent away the one who could have kept me") rest on a decision the reader never felt being made. The movement opens on an errand instead of a want.
- **Required fix.** Dramatize the choice in panel 1. Wujing is torn, with his promise in his mouth: "Wukong told me to walk at your back, Master. But Bajie's been gone an hour with the begging bowl." The monk, hungry and a little proud, sends him: "Go and find him, Wujing. I'm not a child. I can sit by a horse." Then the monk's own will causes the danger, Wujing's guilt has a moment to point back to, and the narration caption can stay.

**3. pp 24–29 and p40: the second antagonist goes unnamed for five pages, and Wukong uses a name he never learned.**
- **Defect.** The Yellow Robe is onstage from p24. No caption, balloon or printed object names him until p29's prose ("The Yellow Robe put the tiger in an iron cage"), which assumes the reader already knows the name. Bajie calls him "You in the gold house." On p40 Wukong shouts "Yellow Robe! Come out…", but Bajie's report (p37) only said "a demon," and nothing on the page shows Wukong learning the name.
- **Reader harm.** The reader has to connect an unexplained name to a figure, and then accept that Wukong somehow knows it. That is a character acting on information the reader never saw him get.
- **Required fix.**
  - p24 caption: "There were no monks in the pagoda. There was only the demon they called the Yellow Robe."
  - Or let him name himself on p25 or p26: "Nobody bangs on the Yellow Robe's gate."
  - On p37 Bajie names him to Wukong: "a demon in a yellow robe, big as a door."
  - Then p40's shout is earned.

**4. pp 24 and 26: the gate and staff contradict each other, and the drawings will carry the contradiction.**
- **Defect.** p23 puts an open black-iron gate a short way up a path into the wood, with the pagoda roofs "above the trees" beyond it. p24 puts the monk "in the doorway" of the inner hall, with his staff "slipping out of his hand onto the threshold." p26 panel 1 puts the staff "on the threshold of the open gate" in the wood. Bajie is "banging on it," and then in panel 2 "the gate swinging open." So the gate is open, then banged on, then opened, and the staff has moved from the hall doorway to the outer gate.
- **Reader harm.** The reader is shown an open gate being knocked on, and a clue (the staff) that sits somewhere the monk never dropped it. The round history says this was restaged to fix the clue. As written, the restaging contradicts itself, and the error would go straight into images.
- **Required fix.** Make the black-iron gate the pagoda's own front door.
  - p23: the pagoda stands just inside the trees, its door open.
  - p24: the monk is in that doorway and drops the staff on its threshold.
  - p26 panel 1: the gate is **shut** (the demon closed it behind his catch), and the staff lies on the ground before it.
  - Bajie bangs on the shut gate; panel 2: it swings open.
  - Also fix p23's page-turn line ("the monk has walked through the gate alone") so it matches what the page shows: the monk turning toward the open gate.

**5. p43: the villain's fate is ambiguous. Did he escape, or go home?**
- **Defect.** On p42 Wukong threatens the demon with the sky ("Heaven counts its stars every night… Shall I tell them where you're hiding?"). On p43 the demon flies into that same sky and becomes "one bright star among the first stars." Bajie: "He's getting away! Chase him!" Wukong: "Let him go." The words say escape, and the picture says he went back to his place.
- **Reader harm.** The reader has to work out the antagonist's ending. Is he still out there and coming back, or has he gone home? Why would someone who fears Heaven fly up into it? A climax that resolves with a question mark is a flag.
- **Required fix.** Make Wukong's reply carry the resolution in plain words, in his own voice: "He's not getting away, Bajie. He's going home, back into his place in the sky, before Heaven counts tonight. He'd rather be a star than be caught." Keep "I've got a monk to get" after it if it fits the balloon budget, or move it into a second balloon.

## Nonblocking observations

- **p28.** The monk's change of heart would land harder if he linked it to his own mistake: "I saw a pagoda and was sure there were monks. I saw a girl and was sure she was a girl." One line, in his plain voice.
- **pp 29, 30, 44.** The demon pulls the shutter every night so no sky light falls on him. The dragon tears it off on night one, and on p44, two days later, light still comes through "the broken high window." A demon with that fear would board it up. Either show boards Wukong tears away on p44, or let it stand. It is small, but it touches the fear the climax depends on.
- **p1 "the river of sand," p12 "since the mountain."** Returning readers will get these. A few words ("since the day the monk let him out from under the mountain") would make p12 land on its own.
- **pp 13–14.** The needle behind the ear becoming the staff is never re-stated in this volume. p14's art shows it growing, which is probably enough.
- **p38 "On that cloud."** The round history lists this as cut, but it is still in the script. The line is funny and only Wukong could say it, so keep it, but correct the history so the next round isn't confused.
- **Mode balance.** 29 dramatic, 7 prose, 12 spectacle (about 60 / 15 / 25). Spectacle runs high and prose a little low. pp 30–31 are two captioned spectacle pages back to back. That is acceptable, but if a page is added anywhere, make it illustrated prose.
- **Five-word audit.** I checked all 16 flagged balloons. Each is a complete thought or a direct answer, has one visible referent, and is forceful rather than compressed. "He's getting away! Chase him!" (p43) passes on its own terms; its problem is the story logic in Finding 5.
- **Text budget.** No balloon is over 24 words, and every dramatic page is within roughly 105 words. p1 (133 words) is in the exceptional prose band, which is acceptable. p28 (82) and p33 (70) are fine.
- **Aftermath.** Covered for the band pain (p12), the exile (pp 19–22), Wujing's guilt (p44), Bajie's confession (pp 46–47) and the horse's wound (p48). The reconciliation between Wukong and Bajie is shown through action ("He waited for the pig"). That is a strength.
