VERDICT: REVISE

## Cold read

I read the book start to finish before opening any other document.

**What happens.** The monk Sanzang and his three disciples cross a bare white ridge. A bone-faced demon on a crag wants one bite of him, because eating him makes you live forever. Wukong flies off for peaches. The demon comes down as a girl with a basket, and Wukong drops out of the sky and strikes her. The reader sees the skull under her smile and the mist escaping. The monk sees a dead girl. Bajie reminds the monk that Wukong once fooled *him* with a face, and the monk says the three words. It happens again with an old woman, and this time the monk warns him: strike once more and you are not my disciple. Then comes an old man. Wukong asks first. The monk says no. Wukong says "Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it," and strikes. Bones, with her name carved on the skull. Bajie calls it a trick, and the monk sends Wukong away. The band stays on because the monk knows no words to take it off. Wukong bows four times with his copies and cries the whole way home.

At home he is crowned with flowers, can't pull the band off, won't let anyone touch the robe the monk patched, and looks west every evening "at the weather." Meanwhile the monk, alone, walks into a golden pagoda and is caught by the Yellow Robe. Bajie runs. Wujing is taken. In the dark the monk tells Wujing, "I sent away the one who could have kept me." The demon bets that the monk will eat meat once he has a tiger's hunger, and turns him into a tiger. The tiger turns its face to the wall. The horse becomes the dragon, is cut, falls into the stream, and becomes a horse again. At dawn he speaks to Bajie for the first time: go and get our big brother, and tell him the truth. Bajie flies east, lies, is seen through, and tells the truth: he wanted to be the favourite. Wukong is moved that the pig came back on that awful cloud, and goes. At the pagoda his eyes show that the demon is a runaway star, who flees home before Heaven's nightly count. Wukong frees Wujing, sees the monk's face inside the tiger, praises the uneaten meat and changes him back. The monk apologises. Bajie confesses. Next morning the monk promises to believe Wukong even when he can't see the demon himself ("Then we'll be wrong together"). On the road a woman with a basket appears. The monk looks at Wukong first, and Wukong does not reach for his staff.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p47.** The monk bows and says "I'm sorry, Wukong. I sent you away for saving my life." The next panel is about Bajie. I went looking for Wukong's face and it wasn't there. This is Finding 1.
- **p28.** Wujing's "I didn't keep you" made me pause for half a beat over the verb ("keep you" as in *protect*, not *detain*). I got it on the first pass.
- **p40.** Why can't Wukong just carry Bajie, or have Bajie tell him the way? I noticed the question, smiled at "He waited for the pig," and read on.

I never had to reread to know who, where or why.

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book, and parts of it are better than good.

**Movement I (pp 1–5).** Quick and clean. The White Bone Lady on her crag, running a bone finger across her teeth, is a real hook. Bajie's "I'll say yes for you" is the first laugh. Page 1 carries a lot of rules, but they are the rules the rest of the book uses.

**Movement II (pp 6–19).** The best stretch, and it stays faithful to its engine: the suspense is never *whether* she is a demon, only *why won't he believe him* and *what will Wukong do the third time*. The heat climbs each round. The monk is wrong for reasons you respect: he saw a killing, and a pig he trusts has a believable story. Wukong changes. He swings without a word the first time, asks first the third time, is refused, and chooses knowing the cost. "Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it" is the line of the book. "I don't know any words for that" is quietly devastating. The four bows ("I won't look at you" against a monkey on every side) is defiance and love in one image. You can tell every voice without the names: Bajie whines and flatters, Wujing says the worst thing about himself in five flat words, the monk is plain and stubborn, and Wukong is hot.

**Movement III (pp 20–22).** Short and right. "He told the monkeys he was looking at the weather" is all the subtext this movement needs.

**Movement IV (pp 23–34).** The protagonist is absent for twelve pages, and the book holds without him. The monk carries it with an appetite of his own (hunger, then stubbornness: "you may not hurt my friends"). The Yellow Robe is a distinct villain: lazy, delighted, cruel, and secretly afraid of the sky. The shutter and "nobody ever comes looking for me" are planted fairly. The silent panel on p28 earns the turn. The horse speaking is a genuine surprise with a reason ("Only when it matters").

**Movement V (pp 35–40).** Bajie's movement, and it moved me. "He practised the truth all the way there. It sounded terrible every time." The lie, the being seen through ("I don't need magic eyes to see through you"), then the confession that names the real want ("I wanted to be the one he liked best"). Wukong's turn is earned by what Bajie *did* (came back on that cloud), not by anything he argued. Both choices happen on the page.

**Movement VI (pp 41–49).** "He did. So I came back." The star reveal answers the plants and matches the book's idea (a demon's face over a star's) without anyone stating it. "You can't even be a tiger properly" is Wukong all over. The last page is a fine ending.

The one place the book flinches is the moment it has been building toward. The protagonist's secret want is for the monk to see him. When the monk finally says so, the script gives Wukong no face and no reply, and hands the panel to Bajie. Wukong's voice should show there, and nothing on the page shows it.

## Mandatory findings

**1. p47: two major turns on one page, and the protagonist's reaction to the apology is not staged.**

- **The defect.** p47 holds both the monk's apology to Wukong ("I should have believed you. I'm sorry, Wukong. I sent you away for saving my life.") and Bajie's confession to the monk ("Master, it was me…"). The page's own turn line names both: "the monk has apologized, and the pig has confessed."
- **Where Wukong is.** His whole presence after the apology is panel 3's "Wukong on the right, arms folded, letting him," which is staging for Bajie's moment, not a reaction to his own. His next line is on p48, the next morning, in answer to a different speech (the promise).
- **Reader harm.** The book has made the reader want one thing above all: for the monk to see Wukong. At the instant that happens, the reader cannot see what it does to him. Is he hurt, relieved, pretending not to care? The reader has to supply the emotional answer to the book's central relationship. It is reconstructable from p48 and p49, but "recoverable" is a revision flag, not a pass.
- **Rules broken.** Stay for the consequence (§4.3). Protect emotional duration (§4.8). One dominant turn per page (§8 test 3). And the volume mandate's requirement that Wukong is "never above it," which on this page reads as silent and above it.
- **Required fix.** Give p47 to the apology and Wukong's answer:
  - Keep panels 1–2.
  - Make panel 3 Wukong's reaction, close on his face, in his voice: loud or deflecting, but visibly hit. The script should choose the gesture and the line. Possibilities include the hand going to the mended patch (the book's established tell), and a line that half-refuses the apology while plainly taking it.
  - Move Bajie's confession to open p48 panel 1, where the monk's ruling ("You told me yourself, Bajie, and nobody made you…") already answers it. Telling it the next morning, unprompted, makes "nobody made you" truer.
  - If p48 then runs past its dramatic budget (it is 68 words now; the confession adds about 26), add a page rather than trim either exchange. Never cut the causal lines.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p28 / p45, "keep."** "I didn't keep you," "could have kept me," "I didn't keep him," "Nobody could have kept him" all use *keep* in the old sense of *protect*. Read aloud, "I didn't keep you" can sound like *detain*. "I didn't keep you safe" in Wujing's first line on p28 would anchor the other three at the cost of one word. The monk's line can stay as written once the verb is anchored.
2. **p32 caption is 27 words**, over the ~24-word per-balloon guide. Split it into two captions: "He tried to tear the cage open." / "The Yellow Robe woke, and his blade drove the dragon out through the high window and down into the stream."
3. **p40 caption is 25 words**, and a sharp reader may ask why Wukong doesn't carry the pig. A clause that keeps the joke would close it, for example: Bajie refuses to ride a monkey's cloud, or can only find the way by flying it himself.
4. **p34 → p35.** Bajie's agreement to go is never spoken. p35 carries it well in narration, but one balloon in p34 panel 3 (a grudging yes in his voice) would let him choose on the page, as the mandate asks of the "going back" choices.
5. **p47, "That's what I did to you."** It parses, but "that" points back across two clauses. If Finding 1's rework touches the line, consider making the parallel concrete: he couldn't see the man inside the tiger, just as he couldn't see the monkey who was saving him behind the staff.
6. **p44** is a spectacle page carrying three balloons and 29 words. That is acceptable, but it pushes the spectacle mode's light-text intent. Wukong's two balloons could merge.
7. **Mode balance** is 30 / 7 / 12 (61% dramatic, 14% prose, 24% spectacle). Prose sits exactly at the volume's seven-page floor and spectacle runs heavy, which the mandate explicitly allows. If Finding 1 adds a page, make it dramatic or prose, not spectacle.
8. **p1** is at the 140-word prose ceiling. It is acceptable as the series recap. If the builder needs room, say so to the owner; nothing here should be compressed.
