VERDICT: REVISE

# Script gate, round 3: *Monkey King, Volume III: Sent Away*

## Cold read

**What happens, as a first-time reader.** Months after the river of sand, the monk Sanzang is still walking west with his three disciples. Wukong is the Monkey King. He wears a gold band that squeezes his head whenever the monk says "Gold, hold fast", and his eyes can see the real face under any disguise. Bajie is the pig and Wujing is the river ogre, and Sanzang rides a white horse that used to be a dragon. On White Tiger Ridge a demon made of bone wants one bite of the monk, because that bite would let her live forever.

While Wukong flies off for peaches, the demon comes down three times: as a girl with a basket, then as the girl's mother, then as the father. Each time Wukong sees the skull and strikes. The monk sees only a kind stranger lying dead. Each time Bajie stokes him, and each time he says the words. The third time, Wukong asks first and is told no. He strikes anyway ("Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it"), and the heap of bones has WHITE BONE LADY carved on the skull. Bajie claims Wukong scratched it on with his fingernail. The monk sends Wukong away, and the band stays on. Wukong bows four times, using copies of himself made from his hairs, tells Wujing to walk at the monk's back, and cries the whole way home.

At Flower-Fruit Mountain he is crowned king. He won't let the monkeys take off the robe the monk patched, and every evening he looks west and says he is checking the weather.

Back on the road, without its guard, the monk sends Wujing to find Bajie and walks alone into a golden pagoda. The demon there, the Yellow Robe, laughs at him and beats Bajie and Wujing. He sprays the monk with water and turns him into a tiger, betting that the monk will eat meat and become a beast too. The tiger turns his face to the wall. That night the horse becomes a dragon, is cut, and by morning is a horse again in the stream. He tells Bajie to go and get their big brother.

Bajie flies east on his sagging cloud. He tries a lie and gets caught, then tells the truth: he lied about the bones because he wanted to be the favourite. Wukong goes back. At the pagoda Wukong's eyes see starlight inside the Yellow Robe: he is a star that ran away from Heaven. Wukong threatens to tell on him, and the star flies home. Wukong frees Wujing, sees that the tiger never touched the meat, and turns the monk back into a man. The monk apologises and Bajie confesses. On the road the next day a woman with a basket comes toward them, and the monk looks at Wukong first.

**Paraphrase test:** passes. I could tell this story after one read.

**Where I stopped:**
- **p13, panel 3.** "His hand going up toward the needle behind his ear." I had to remember from earlier volumes that the needle is the staff. Page 14 shows it growing, so the confusion clears a page later.
- **p25, panel 1.** The demon's hand comes "from behind", but on p24 the monk was facing him and backing out of the door. This is a staging snag, not a story one.
- **p28.** This is the stop that matters. The monk's realisation ("I sent away the one who could have kept me… three times I said the words") goes by in one panel, and before I had taken it in, the demon was spraying him and he was a tiger. I went back to reread the confession. Then p46 opens with "In that cage I had three days to think," and I wasn't sure when he had actually understood.
- **p30–31.** The dragon tears off the shutter that keeps the sky from seeing the demon. I wondered whether the sky could now see him, and nothing answers it.
- **p38.** Wukong says "On that cloud." He was in the cave when Bajie landed (p35), so I wondered how he knew about the cloud.
- **p39.** This is the stop I could not explain. The caption reads "One somersault would have been faster. He waited for the pig." The monk is a tiger in a demon's cage. Wukong does not know the demon is waiting for the monk to eat meat. Why would he slow down by a night and a day?

## Is it alive?

Yes. This is a good book, and in places a moving one. I would want it read to me.

**Movement I (pp 1–5).** It opens efficiently and with an appetite. The monk's hunger drives the plot, which is exactly right. The vow is planted in his own mouth ("I've never let anything die for my supper") so it can pay off later. The demon's finger across her teeth on p2 is a superb page turn, and Bajie's "I'll say yes for you" is pure Bajie.

**Movement II (pp 6–19).** This is the heart of the book, and it hurts in the right way. Showing the same body two ways on p6 (a hollow skin to Wukong, a dead girl to the monk) makes the tragedy fair to both sides. A ten-year-old will feel the injustice and also understand why the monk can't see it.

Each of the three repetitions escalates: no warning, then a long punishment and an explicit warning, then Wukong asking first. The repetition is the point here, not a duplicated episode. The best line in the book is "Then send me away, Master. You'll be alive to do it." p17 ("I don't know any words for that") is devastating, and p19's "He cried the whole way home" lands.

The heat is clear: Wukong wants to be believed by the one person whose belief he needs, and the monk wants to stay a good man and can't see that his goodness is killing his protector. There is plenty of subtext. Bajie's "concern" is jealousy and Wukong's demand to "take the band off" is grief. The attribution-strip test passes on every dramatic page: Bajie whines and flatters, Wujing is flat and blames himself first, the monk is polite with firm full stops, and Wukong is blunt and quick.

**Movement III (pp 20–22).** Short and right. The robe and the westward look ("He told the monkeys he was looking at the weather") carry the whole interior arc without one stated feeling.

**Movement IV (pp 23–33).** This movement runs eleven pages without the protagonist, and it mostly earns them. Wujing's torn choice on p23 is a real want-from: he wants the monk's permission to break a promise. The Yellow Robe is a good bully ("May not!"). The tiger turning his face to the wall on p29 is the best prose page in the book. The horse talking ("Only when it matters") is funny and true to him.

The one weak spot is p28, where the monk's moral turn is crowded off the page by the transformation (Finding 1).

**Movement V (pp 34–39).** Bajie's arc is the surprise of the volume. "He practised the truth all the way there. It sounded terrible every time" is lovely. The p36 lie and Wukong's "I don't need magic eyes to see through you" give the confession a real obstacle. "I hate flying, and I hate you bossing me, and I came all the way back for you anyway!" is earned.

Then p39 lets the urgency out of the rescue at the exact moment it should peak (Finding 2).

**Movement VI (pp 40–48).** The star solution is set up by the shutter and by "nobody ever comes looking for me", and it uses the book's own device, Wukong's sight. "You can't even be a tiger properly" is subtext doing the work of forgiveness. p48's woman with a basket is a perfect last image.

## Mandatory findings

**1. Page 28: the monk's moral turn and the tiger transformation are packed onto one page, and his turn gets no time.**

- **Defect.** Panel 1 carries three balloons: Wujing's confession, then the monk's "No, Wujing. I sent away the one who could have kept me," then "I saw a girl and was sure she was a girl. He told me three times, and three times I said the words." This is the protagonist-B turn of the whole volume: the moment the monk understands. Panels 2–3 then go straight into the demon's bet and the spray, and the page ends on "the monk is a tiger." That is two dominant turns on one page, remorse and a transformation. The mandate rules this out (§4.5 and §4.8: protect emotional duration; one dominant turn). The page also runs to 90 words, above the normal dramatic budget, while panel 3 has to stage a full transformation.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The tiger is the image that lands. The confession is read on the way to it and has to be found again afterwards; I went back for it. Then p46's "In that cage I had three days to think" makes it unclear when the monk understood: in this panel, or in the cage? A climax apology works best when the reader has felt the understanding happen once, clearly.
- **Required fix.** Split p28 into two pages. Move existing lines; do not cut any.
  - **First page (dramatic), at the pillar:** Wujing's confession, then the monk's two lines, and then a silent panel. In the silent panel the monk, tied to the pillar, looks at nothing, and Wujing looks at him. Let the reader sit with "three times I said the words." Turn: the monk knows what he did.
  - **Second page (dramatic):** the Yellow Robe crouches with the cup, gives both taunts, and sprays. Turn: the monk is a tiger.
  - **On p46,** keep "three days to think" but point it at the new thing the monk learned in the cage, so it doesn't repeat p28. For example: "In that cage I was a beast on the outside and myself inside, and nobody could see it. That's what I did to you."
  - Renumber everything after the split and note it in the round history.

**2. Page 39: the hero slows his master's rescue by a night and a day, and the caption says so without giving a reason.**

- **Defect.** "They flew all night and all the next day. One somersault would have been faster. He waited for the pig." As far as Wukong knows (Bajie's report on p37), the monk is a tiger in the cage of a demon who beat everyone else. Wukong never learns that the demon is waiting for the monk to eat meat; only Wujing and the monk heard that bet. Waiting for the pig is meant as a gesture of loyalty to Bajie, but nothing stops it from also reading as Wukong putting a gesture ahead of his master's life.
- **What the reader has to repair.** The caption raises the question itself ("would have been faster") and answers it only with feeling. A sharp ten-year-old will ask, "Why didn't he just go? The demon could eat him!" To answer, the reader has to invent a reason the script never gives, such as "maybe he didn't know the way." That is solving the adaptation, and it happens on the transition into the climax, so it lets the heat out of the rescue run.
- **Required fix.** Give the delay an in-story reason without losing the gesture. Either of these works:
  - **Option (a):** Wukong doesn't know where the pagoda is, and only Bajie does. Revise the caption, for example: "They flew all night and all the next day. One somersault would have been faster, but only the pig knew which pine wood. So the Monkey King waited for the pig." Bajie's balloon can stay. Optionally, plant the reason earlier: on p38 Wukong says "Where is this pagoda?", and Bajie says "I'll show you. You'll have to go slow."
  - **Option (b):** Wukong tows Bajie's cloud by the ear and they arrive fast, which keeps the loyalty and the urgency. If you take this option, adjust the "three days" count on pp 29, 44 and 46 to match.

## Nonblocking observations

1. **p13, panel 3 / p1.** The silent cliff on p13 (Wukong's hand going to "the needle behind his ear") only reads as "he is reaching for his weapon" if the reader knows the needle is the staff. Page 14 shows it, so this is recoverable, but one clause in p1's prose would make p13 land the first time. For example, after the eyes sentence: "His iron staff he kept as a needle behind his ear." The prose field has room under 140 words.
2. **p38, "On that cloud."** Wukong never saw Bajie's cloud; Bajie landed while Wukong was inside the cave (p35). Either let Wukong have seen it ("I watched that cloud wobble in from the west," which ties to his evening watch on p22), or reword the line to "On a cloud like yours."
3. **p25, panel 1.** "His huge hand closing on the monk's shoulder from behind" contradicts p24, where the monk is facing the demon and backing out of the door. Drop "from behind", or have the monk turn to run first.
4. **p30–31, the shutter.** The dragon rips off the shutter that keeps the sky from seeing the demon, and the demon then sleeps two more nights with the window open to the sky. Either show the window boarded up again later (a detail on p44's staging), or leave the shutter alone and let the dragon come in another way. As it stands, the plant you need for p42 is slightly weakened.
5. **p44, a missed payoff.** The tiger shrinks from Wukong, and Wukong recognises the monk only by the untouched meat. This is the one moment in the book where the monk is the one in disguise and Wukong is the one who sees through it. Consider a single eye-flare panel: inside the tiger, the pale ghost of the monk's face, with a line such as "I'd know your face under any face, Master." It would close the book's central device on the monk himself. The meat line can stay too.
6. **Mode balance.** The book is 29 dramatic / 7 prose / 12 spectacle pages, which is 60 / 15 / 25 against the 60 / 20 / 13 target. Spectacle runs heavy and prose light. Two "spectacle" pages carry dramatic dialogue: p43 has three balloons including the villain's fate, and p11 carries the key warning. That is acceptable, but if you add pages for Finding 1, look for one more prose breather. For example, the night in the cage before the dragon comes, or the second evening at Flower-Fruit Mountain.
7. **Five-word audit.** All 16 listed balloons pass. Each is either a complete thought or a direct answer to the line before it, with one visible referent:
   - "Wukong, don't you dare" answers "Same skull."
   - "He's, um, busy" and "He was sent away" answer "Where's the monkey?"
   - "And what if I'm wrong?" has its object in the line before it.
   - "Gold, hold fast" is the rule set out on p1.
   - Exclamations such as "Master, no!" and "Wukong, no!" are carried by the visible action.
8. **Page contract.** `07-PAGE-CONTRACT.md` is out of date against the script:
   - p8 gives "or you go", which is not in the script.
   - p12 gives "the warning repeated", but the prose does not repeat it.
   - p25 gives "you may not hurt anyone else"; the script says "my friends."
   - p36 gives "these eyes… can see through a pig."

   Regenerate the contract from the approved script.
9. **The presence tests otherwise pass.** No logistics dialogue was found; p26's "His staff is lying at your door" is evidence used as a threat in Wujing's flat voice, not an arrangement. No aphorism was found either. The Yellow Robe's "Everybody's a beast underneath" is his own cruel bet, belongs to him alone, and the tiger disproves it. The protagonist's appetite is visible on every page he appears on.
