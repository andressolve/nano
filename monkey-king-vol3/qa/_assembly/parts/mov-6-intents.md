Illustrated prose. The monk is a tiger now, shut in an iron cage in the black hall, and the page belongs to his refusal. One iron lamp shows a thin tiger in a torn red kasaya sitting upright with his front paws together, his face turned to the wall, an untouched joint of meat on the cage floor. Wujing, tied to the pillar in the dark, watches. The prose tells the reader what the picture holds: a monk who never ate meat now has a tiger's hunger, and he will not eat. The dominant turn is the tiger turning his face from the meat while the shutter is pulled tight against the sky. The reader turns because someone outside has been watching. Wukong is far away and absent. The demon's bed, the lamps and the stonework stay subordinate; the quiet tiger is everything.

## PAGE 31

Spectacle. Night in the pine wood. The white horse, tied to his pine, has seen everything: the pig run, the ogre dragged inside. Then the reader learns his secret with him. In the dominant image the horse uncoils into a slim white dragon, sea-green eyes and pale-blue mane, the empty bridle falling away. In the last beat the dragon tears the shutter off the pagoda's high window and slides in with the moonlight behind him. The dominant turn is the horse becoming the dragon and going in. The reader turns because the dragon has gone in after the monk. Wukong is absent. The pagoda's storeys, the pines and the gate stay subordinate to the transformation; two short captions carry the words and the pictures carry the rest.

## PAGE 32

Spectacle, one image across the whole page. The dark hall, moonlight now falling through the torn window. The white dragon is coiled round the iron cage, wrenching at its bars, while the tiger inside looks up at him. The Yellow Robe has risen from his bed and his silver scimitar catches the dragon across the right foreleg. Wujing strains at his ropes at the pillar. The dominant turn is the rescue failing: the cut foreleg and the dragon driven out, the cage still shut. Two captions tell the attempt and the defeat. The reader turns because the dragon is hurt and the monk is still a prisoner. Wukong is absent. The wound is a clean dark gash, never blood; the furs, lamps and stonework stay behind the struggle.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic. Dawn at the stream by the pine wood. Bajie, who hid in the reeds all night, crawls out muddy and calling, then finds the white horse lying in the shallow water with a clean dark gash on his right foreleg and his bridle broken. A caption tells the reader the dragon could not hold his shape. Bajie kneels and asks who did this. Then the page's one turn: the horse lifts his head, sea-green eyes steady, and speaks for the first time in the book. The reader turns because the horse has spoken. Bajie owns the comedy of the first two beats, the horse owns the last. Wukong is absent. The reeds, the water and the far pagoda stay subordinate.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. At the stream, the horse tells Bajie what has happened and what must be done. Bajie falls backward into the water at hearing him talk; the horse says only when it matters, that the master is a tiger in a cage and Wujing is tied, and that neither of them can beat the demon. Face to face, the horse tells him to bring back their big brother from Flower-Fruit Mountain, and Bajie confesses that he lied about the bones. The dominant turn is that confession, answered by the horse: then tell Wukong that, tell him the truth. The reader turns because the pig must go back for the monkey he sent away. Wukong is absent and only named. The stream and the woods stay subordinate to two faces.
