## PAGE 20

Illustrated prose. The homecoming. After the long cry across the sky, the reader arrives with Wukong at the place that has always been his: the green peak on the eastern sea, the waterfall roaring white over the cave where he was once king, the split stone, the old flagpole with its rag of faded pink banner. The page belongs to the place and to the flood of young brown monkeys pouring out from behind the waterfall and up the rocks toward the one small golden figure landing on the peak, band on his brow. The dominant turn: the homecoming — the waterfall, the monkeys, the king back. Three short paragraphs of prose sit in the open sky above the peak and tell it. The grief of the last page stays underneath, unspoken. The reader turns because the king is home and his monkeys want to crown him.

## PAGE 21

Dramatic. Inside the Water Curtain Cave, behind the falling water, Wukong is crowned again and tries to undo the whole journey in one afternoon. The monkeys set a crown of flowers and leaves on his head, on top of the gold band, while he lounges on the stone throne and announces that there will be no monk, no magic words, no walking, ever again. Then the page's one dominant turn, which the middle panel owns: he hauls at the band with both hands and a chain of monkeys hauls at him like a tug-of-war, and the band will not move. Last, a small monkey tugs at his hemp robe and he snatches it back, fierce, a hand over the patch his monk sewed. He is the only speaker; the monkeys are silent. The reader turns because he is home and free, and he will not take off the robe.

## PAGE 22

Illustrated prose. The days at home, and the evenings. At sunset Wukong stands alone on the peak under the rag of faded pink banner, the flower crown slipping over the gold band, one flat hand shading his eyes, looking west over the hills, while below him the monkeys sleep in the peach trees. The prose, set in the open western sky and never over his face, tells the reader he was happy, said so, ate and swam and lounged, and that nobody told him to hurry or wait or said three words to him — and that every evening he climbed up here to look west and called it the weather. The dominant turn: happy, he said; every evening, west. The page is quiet and lonely and lets the reader see what he will not say. The reader turns because, far to the west, the monk has nobody at the front.
