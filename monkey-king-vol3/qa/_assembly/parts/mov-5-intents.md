## PAGE 23

Dramatic. The road along a dark pine wood, without the monkey: it is quieter and slower, and the monk is hungry. Wujing, torn and uneasy, admits the pig has been gone too long with the begging bowl; the monk, stubborn and a little offended, sends him to find Bajie and says he can sit by a horse. Then the monk is alone. He ties the white horse to a pine, his ringed staff in his hand, and looks up a short path to a tall pagoda with gleaming gold roofs just inside the trees, its heavy black-iron door standing open. The dominant turn: Wujing goes to find Bajie, and the monk alone sees a golden pagoda. His delight about monks and supper should read as innocent and wrong. The reader turns because he is about to walk up to that open door with nobody at his back. Wujing's warning is subordinate; the pagoda and the lone monk own the page.

## PAGE 24

Spectacle. Inside the gold pagoda there is no daylight and no monk: a great black stone hall lit by a few iron lamps, and on a wide stone bed heaped with furs, a huge indigo-faced demon in a mustard-ochre robe with silver stars over black armour, a scimitar across his knees, asleep. Small in the open black-iron doorway, the monk is backing away, his ringed staff slipping out of his hand onto the threshold. One of the demon's pale silver eyes has opened and is looking at him. The dominant turn: inside, the Yellow Robe asleep, the monk backing away, one eye opens. The scale between the vast sleeping demon and the tiny monk carries the page; the single caption names the demon. The reader turns because the demon is awake.

## PAGE 25

Dramatic. The Yellow Robe's huge hand closes on the monk's shoulder, and he enjoys himself: everybody on the road wants to eat this monk, and this one walked into the one house where nobody comes looking. Then the monk is tied to a thick black pillar, frightened, chin up, and he speaks the bravest sentence he has: he has never eaten meat or hurt anyone; the demon may eat him, but may not hurt his friends. The demon, sprawled on his stone bed tossing his scimitar, throws back his head and laughs at the word "may". The dominant turn: caught, the monk forbids the demon to hurt his friends, and the demon laughs. The monk's small defiant face at the pillar owns the page; the grab and the laugh frame it. The reader turns because the demon thinks the monk is a joke.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. Outside the pagoda, its black-iron door now shut tight, Bajie bangs on it with his rake and shouts for their master; Wujing, spade raised, points out the monk's ringed staff lying on the ground in front of the door, the proof he is inside. The door swings open and the Yellow Robe fills the doorway, delighted: a pig and an ogre, and where is the famous monkey? Bajie fumbles that he is busy. Wujing says flatly that he was sent away. The dominant turn: Bajie and Wujing at the gate demand their master back, and the demon fills the doorway. The scale of the demon over two small fighters owns the page; the last exchange lands quietly. The reader turns because the demon now knows the monkey is gone.

## PAGE 27

Spectacle. The fight in the clearing among the black-green pines, in one great image: the Yellow Robe's scimitar sweeping in a silver arc, Bajie's rake spinning out of his hands, Wujing flat on his back under the demon's boot with his crescent spade knocked away, and Bajie running for the reeds by the stream, ears flying. The caption says it took the demon one breath to beat them both. The dominant turn: rake and spade against the scimitar; Bajie runs; Wujing falls. Nobody bleeds; this is power and speed, comic at the edges where the pig flees, grim at the centre where the ogre is pinned. The reader turns because the pig has run and the ogre is caught.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. In the black hall, Wujing, bruised and bound, is tied to the same black pillar as the monk, and he says the worst thing about himself first: he was told to walk at the monk's back, he went looking for the pig, he did not keep him safe. The monk turns his face to him and, for the first time, says what he did: he sent away the one who could have kept him; he was sure the girl was a girl, he was told three times, and three times he said the words. Then silence: the monk staring at nothing with wet eyes, Wujing watching him. The dominant turn: the monk answers "I sent away the one who could have kept me". The close on the monk's face owns the page. The reader turns because the monk knows what he did, and the demon is coming back.

## PAGE 29

Dramatic. The Yellow Robe crouches grinning in front of the monk at the pillar, a cup of water in his hand, and makes his bet aloud: a monk who will not hurt anyone, given claws and teeth; everyone is a beast underneath, and when the monk eats meat, the demon will eat him. Then he sprays a fine silver mist from his mouth over the monk, and the monk changes: swelling, black stripes rising through the saffron robe, the face becoming a tiger's with the monk's own dark eyes, the red kasaya tearing at the shoulder, the ropes falling slack, while Wujing strains away at the pillar. The dominant turn: the demon's bet, the spell of water, the monk becomes a tiger. The transformation owns the page; the bet sets it up. The reader turns because the monk is a tiger.
