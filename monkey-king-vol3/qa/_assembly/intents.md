# Page intents

Reader-facing goals, not image-generation specifications. Shown to both
builder and critic. Exact text and staging stay in the script and the prompt.

Format — one section per page, header exactly `## PAGE N`:

## PAGE 1

Illustrated prose. The book opens on the road west along the spine of a white, empty ridge: the monk on his white horse, the pig and the river ogre sharing the luggage pole, the pig complaining, and far ahead on a boulder the monkey with the gold band on his brow, shading his eyes. The prose owns the page and carries the turn: who these travellers are, the band and its three words, and the monkey's eyes that see the real face under any face. The picture is wide, bright and drained to bone; nobody speaks. What changes is that the reader now holds the two rules the whole book turns on, the band and the eyes. The reader turns because the ridge is too empty, and something on it is watching.

## PAGE 2

Spectacle. From the black mouth of a cave at the top of a tooth of white rock, the White Bone Lady in her true shape, a tall lady of bone with a skull for a face, green pinpoints in its hollows, long black hair and gray-violet burial silk, draws one finger across her teeth and looks down. Far below, tiny on the pale road, the four travellers and the horse walk toward her. She owns the page; the travellers are small and unaware. The one turn: she wants the monk, because one bite of him means she will never die. The caption names the waiting and her one line names the want. The reader turns because the company is walking straight toward her.

## PAGE 3

Dramatic. The travellers stop on the bare ridge. The monk is so hungry he can't see straight; Wukong, up on a dead stump, finds no village but knows of peaches to the south. The dominant moment is Wukong on a puff of cloud, pointing at the seated monk like a teacher: sit on that rock and don't move, I can't fly you but I can fly peaches, while Wujing promises to stand at the monk's back. Then Wukong is gone, a streak south, and Bajie throws down his end of the pole and grumbles about who made the monkey the boss; the monk answers mildly. The turn: Wukong flies for peaches and leaves an order behind. The grumble stays light. The reader turns because the monk is alone on the ridge with two guards and an empty stomach.

## PAGE 4

Dramatic. Up on the crag, the White Bone Lady draws a pretty girl's face down over her skull like a mask; a peach-pink jacket, a black plait and a lidded basket grow onto her. On the road the girl walks up smiling and offers the hungry monk rice and noodles; he rises, palms together, glad there is no meat, because he has never let anything die for his supper. The girl and the monk own the page; the offer is the turn. Then the girl lifts the lid, two tiny green pinpoints in her eyes for the reader alone, while Bajie leans over the monk's shoulder and accepts for him. Nobody on the road suspects anything. High behind them, a small streak of cloud comes fast from the south. The reader turns because the monk is about to eat from the demon's basket.

## PAGE 5

Spectacle. Wukong drops out of the sky onto the road, the peaches tumbling from his arms, his red staff already swinging down, the red rings of his gold eyes flaring. The reader is shown what only his eyes see: through the smiling girl's face, the faint pale ghost of a skull, and bone fingers under her hands. To the monk and the pig on the left she is still only a smiling girl; their mouths hang open at a monkey attacking her, too late to stop him, while Wujing reaches from behind. Wukong owns the page. The one turn: he comes back, sees the skull under the face, and swings without a word. The staff has not landed. The reader turns because the staff is falling.

## PAGE 6

Dramatic page. The turn: the demon's mist escapes, the monk cries "you killed her!", and Wukong's answer, "look in her basket," turns up maggots and frogs. The page is built on two ways of seeing the same road. First, through Wukong's flaring eyes, the girl is an empty skin and a thin white mist with a skull face in it streams away toward the crag. Then the monk's view owns the page: a girl lying still, face-down, nothing more, and the monk on his knees beside her, shaking, accusing. Wukong points his staff at the basket. Last, the basket itself, tipped over and crawling, and Bajie jumping back. The reader knows Wukong is right; the monk has not looked at the basket, only at the girl. No blood anywhere. The frogs are gross and funny, never horror.

## PAGE 7

Dramatic page. The turn: Bajie tells the monk Wukong once fooled him with a face, and the monk says the words. Bajie owns the first two beats, whispering at the monk's ear and then in full cry, hands wide: if Wukong can become a bride, he can make rice look like maggots. The monk, still on his knees, turns from the frogs to look at Wukong, and Wujing watches Wukong in silence. The dominant beat is Bajie's argument landing on the monk's face. Then the monk presses his palms together, shuts his eyes and says "Gold, hold fast," and Wukong, stepping toward him, freezes with both hands flying up to the gold band. The reader turns because the words have been said and the pain is coming.

## PAGE 8

Dramatic page. The turn: the pain, the monk stops, warns him never again, and Wukong insists she was a demon. The dominant image is Wukong curled in the white dust, both hands clamped on the band, teeth clenched, begging the monk to stop, while the monk stands over him with his eyes shut. Then the monk opens his eyes, his hands shaking apart: he promised he would only say the words if he must. Wukong gets up, furious and hurt, and says he saw the face under her face; the monk, turning away to the horse, answers that he saw a girl with a basket. Bajie picks up the luggage pole, pleased. The page ends on two honest people who saw two different things, and the reader knows the demon is not finished.

## PAGE 9

Dramatic page. The turn: a weeping old woman comes looking for her daughter, Bajie cries "her mother!", and Wukong sees the same skull under the new face. First, on the crag, the White Bone Lady draws an old woman's kind wrinkled face down over her skull. Then the dominant beat on the road: the old woman hobbling up on her cane, crying for her daughter, the monk stricken on his white horse, Bajie pointing and crowing that the monkey is in trouble now. Last, close on Wukong's flaring eyes, and through the old woman's face beyond him the faint ghost of the skull, which only he and the reader see. He warns the monk; the monk tells him not to dare. The reader turns on that order.

## PAGE 10

Dramatic page. The turn: the bone hands reach for the monk, Wukong strikes a second time, the mist escapes again, and the monk's hands come together. The old woman, cane dropped, reaches up for the monk on his horse, and the reader sees faint bone fingers behind her hands closing on his robe; the monk sees only an old woman's hands. The dominant beat is the staff coming down and the old woman falling face-down and lying still, while inside Wukong's sight, for him and the reader only, the white mist streams away toward the crag. Wukong shouts after it and asks if the monk saw the mist. The monk, climbing down, white-faced, says he saw an old woman looking for her daughter, and his palms are already closing. No blood.

## PAGE 11

Spectacle page. The turn: the words, over and over, and Wukong rolling in the dust. One image carries the page. Wukong tumbles over and over in the white dust, both hands clamped on the gold band, tail thrashing, howling. Above him the monk stands with his palms pressed together and his eyes shut, saying the three words again and again, and then the last warning: strike one more person and you are not my disciple. Behind the horse, Bajie and Wujing stand back; Wujing looks away. At the road's edge the old woman lies still, face-down, her cane beside her. The monk's voice is the only text. The reader turns because this warning is the last one.

## PAGE 12

Illustrated-prose page. The turn: the silent afternoon, the monk riding in front without looking back, Bajie whistling, and Wukong walking behind for the first time. One wide amber image of the ridge road carries it: the monk on the white horse leading, Bajie and Wujing with the luggage pole, and far at the back, small, Wukong with his head down, watching the rocks. A clear gap separates him from the others. The prose field sits in the open sky and tells the silence, the order of march, what this costs a monkey who always ran ahead, and that on her crag the demon has one more face. The quiet distance between Wukong and the company is the page; the reader turns knowing the third face is coming.

## PAGE 13

Dramatic. Late amber afternoon on the ridge. A gentle white-bearded old man with prayer beads comes up the road asking after his lost wife and daughter, and the monk's face softens. The page belongs to Wukong at the horse's side: for the first time he does not swing first. Low, urgent, not joking, he asks permission to stop the third face, and the monk refuses him. The dominant turn is that exchange: Wukong asks first, and the monk says no. The last beat is Wukong silent, the gold band on his brow, looking from the monk to the old man and back while his hand drifts toward the needle behind his ear; the reader turns to see what that hand will do. The old man's softness, the beads, and the road stay subordinate to the ask and the refusal.

## PAGE 14

Dramatic. The old man reaches both hands up to the monk, smiling, inviting him down to pray; the reader alone glimpses the green pinpoints in his eyes and the bone fingers behind his hands. The monk starts to slide off the horse toward him. The page belongs to Wukong, and its dominant turn is his decision: the needle comes out and grows into the red staff in his fist, his face set, and he tells the monk to send him away, since the monk will be alive to do it. The last beat is the monk half off the horse, turning, seeing the staff go up, crying out. The reader turns because the blow is coming. The monk's descent and the old man's hands are setup; Wukong's choice owns the page.

## PAGE 15

Spectacle. One image, seen as Wukong sees it through the flare of his red-ringed eyes. The red staff comes down, the old man's shape bursts apart, and in the middle of the burst, for Wukong and the reader only, the White Bone Lady's skull face shrieks, caught, her mist with nowhere to go; white bones fall onto the road and prayer beads fly. Wukong hangs in mid-air above it, both hands on the staff. At the side the monk has buried his face in the white horse's neck with his eyes shut: he sees none of it. The dominant turn is the third blow, faster than the mist, leaving a heap of white bones. The page is triumph and dread at once; nothing is gory, and the monk's blindness is as important as the blow.

## PAGE 16

Dramatic. A neat heap of white bones lies on the road with the skull on top. Wukong crouches beside it, pointing, demanding the monk read the demon's name; the monk answers that he saw an old man struck and then there were bones. The dominant turn is the close skull itself, her name cut deep and plain across its brow: WHITE BONE LADY. The proof is right there for the reader. Then Bajie, at the monk's elbow, whispers that the monkey scratched it on with a fingernail, and the trembling monk brings his palms together and says the words again. The reader turns knowing the truth is on the road and the monk has chosen not to believe it. The heap's details and the road stay subordinate to the name and to the monk's palms.

## PAGE 17

Dramatic. Wukong gets up out of the dust holding his head. The monk stands very straight, his face wet, and names the three kind people and the three raised staffs. The dominant turn is the monk alone, close, saying the hardest thing he has ever said: go back to your mountain; you are not my disciple anymore. Then Wukong, small, answers that the monk would be dead three times without him and asks him to take the band off; the monk, half turned away, says he knows no words for that. The reader turns because Wukong has been sent away and the band is staying on. The monk's grief must be real, not cold; the bones behind them stay small and quiet.

## PAGE 18

Dramatic. Wukong kneels to bow once, for the robe the monk gave him, and the monk turns his back and refuses to look. The dominant turn is Wukong's answer, wordless and clever: four identical Wukongs, each with the gold band, kneeling and bowing on four sides of the monk, so that whichever way the monk turns a monkey is bowing to him, while Bajie gapes. It is funny and heartbreaking at once. Then one Wukong again, standing close to Wujing, asks him low to walk at the monk's back and not let the pig talk him into anything, and Wujing promises. The reader turns because he has said goodbye. The trick serves the farewell; it is love, not showing off.

## PAGE 19

Spectacle. One image: Wukong on his cloud streaking east over hazy valleys, tears flying back from his face, the hemp robe flapping, the thin gold band on his brow. Far behind and below, the tiny white ridge with three small figures and a white horse. The dominant turn is the flight itself: the monkey who can cross the world in one somersault, crying the whole way home. The reader turns because he is going home. The distance between him and the little company carries the page; the scenery is space and air, never a travelogue.

## PAGE 20

Illustrated prose. The homecoming. After the long cry across the sky, the reader arrives with Wukong at the place that has always been his: the green peak on the eastern sea, the waterfall roaring white over the cave where he was once king, the split stone, the old flagpole with its rag of faded pink banner. The page belongs to the place and to the flood of young brown monkeys pouring out from behind the waterfall and up the rocks toward the one small golden figure landing on the peak, band on his brow. The dominant turn: the homecoming — the waterfall, the monkeys, the king back. Three short paragraphs of prose sit in the open sky above the peak and tell it. The grief of the last page stays underneath, unspoken. The reader turns because the king is home and his monkeys want to crown him.

## PAGE 21

Dramatic. Inside the Water Curtain Cave, behind the falling water, Wukong is crowned again and tries to undo the whole journey in one afternoon. The monkeys set a crown of flowers and leaves on his head, on top of the gold band, while he lounges on the stone throne and announces that there will be no monk, no magic words, no walking, ever again. Then the page's one dominant turn, which the middle panel owns: he hauls at the band with both hands and a chain of monkeys hauls at him like a tug-of-war, and the band will not move. Last, a small monkey tugs at his hemp robe and he snatches it back, fierce, a hand over the patch his monk sewed. He is the only speaker; the monkeys are silent. The reader turns because he is home and free, and he will not take off the robe.

## PAGE 22

Illustrated prose. The days at home, and the evenings. At sunset Wukong stands alone on the peak under the rag of faded pink banner, the flower crown slipping over the gold band, one flat hand shading his eyes, looking west over the hills, while below him the monkeys sleep in the peach trees. The prose, set in the open western sky and never over his face, tells the reader he was happy, said so, ate and swam and lounged, and that nobody told him to hurry or wait or said three words to him — and that every evening he climbed up here to look west and called it the weather. The dominant turn: happy, he said; every evening, west. The page is quiet and lonely and lets the reader see what he will not say. The reader turns because, far to the west, the monk has nobody at the front.

## PAGE 23

Dramatic. The road along a dark pine wood, without the monkey: it is quieter and slower, and the monk is hungry. Wujing, torn and uneasy, admits the pig has been gone too long with the begging bowl; the monk, stubborn and a little offended, sends him to find Bajie and says he can sit by a horse. Then the monk is alone. He ties the white horse to a pine, his ringed staff in his hand, and looks up a short path to a tall pagoda with gleaming gold roofs just inside the trees, its heavy black-iron door standing open. The dominant turn: Wujing goes to find Bajie, and the monk alone sees a golden pagoda. His delight about monks and supper should read as innocent and wrong. The reader turns because he is about to walk up to that open door with nobody at his back. Wujing's warning is subordinate; the pagoda and the lone monk own the page.

## PAGE 24

Spectacle. Inside the gold pagoda there is no daylight and no monk: a great black stone hall lit by a few iron lamps, and on a wide stone bed heaped with furs, a huge indigo-faced demon in a mustard-ochre robe with silver stars over black armour, a scimitar across his knees, asleep. Small in the open black-iron doorway, the monk is backing away, his ringed staff slipping out of his hand onto the threshold. One of the demon's pale silver eyes has opened and is looking at him. The dominant turn: inside, the Yellow Robe asleep, the monk backing away, one eye opens. The scale between the vast sleeping demon and the tiny monk carries the page; the single caption names the demon. The reader turns because the demon is awake.

## PAGE 25

Dramatic. The Yellow Robe's huge hand closes on the monk's shoulder, and he enjoys himself: everybody on the road wants to eat this monk, and this one walked into the one house where nobody comes looking. Then the monk is tied to a thick black pillar, frightened, chin up, and he speaks the bravest sentence he has: he has never eaten meat or hurt anyone; the demon may eat him, but may not hurt his friends. The demon, sprawled on his stone bed tossing his scimitar, throws back his head and laughs at the word "may". The dominant turn: caught, the monk forbids the demon to hurt his friends, and the demon laughs. The monk's small defiant face at the pillar owns the page; the grab and the laugh frame it. The reader turns because the demon thinks the monk is a joke.

## PAGE 26

Dramatic. Outside the pagoda, its black-iron door now shut tight, Bajie bangs on it with his rake and shouts for their master; Wujing, spade raised, points out the monk's ringed staff lying on the ground in front of the door, the proof he is inside. The door swings open and the Yellow Robe fills the doorway, delighted: a pig and an ogre, and where is the famous monkey? Bajie fumbles that he is busy. Wujing says flatly that he was sent away. The dominant turn: Bajie and Wujing at the gate demand their master back, and the demon fills the doorway. The scale of the demon over two small fighters owns the page; the last exchange lands quietly. The reader turns because the demon now knows the monkey is gone.

## PAGE 27

Spectacle. The fight in the clearing among the black-green pines, in one great image: the Yellow Robe's scimitar sweeping in a silver arc, Bajie's rake spinning out of his hands, Wujing flat on his back under the demon's boot with his crescent spade knocked away, and Bajie running for the reeds by the stream, ears flying. The caption says it took the demon one breath to beat them both. The dominant turn: rake and spade against the scimitar; Bajie runs; Wujing falls. Nobody bleeds; this is power and speed, comic at the edges where the pig flees, grim at the centre where the ogre is pinned. The reader turns because the pig has run and the ogre is caught.

## PAGE 28

Dramatic. In the black hall, Wujing, bruised and bound, is tied to the same black pillar as the monk, and he says the worst thing about himself first: he was told to walk at the monk's back, he went looking for the pig, he did not keep him safe. The monk turns his face to him and, for the first time, says what he did: he sent away the one who could have kept him; he was sure the girl was a girl, he was told three times, and three times he said the words. Then silence: the monk staring at nothing with wet eyes, Wujing watching him. The dominant turn: the monk answers "I sent away the one who could have kept me". The close on the monk's face owns the page. The reader turns because the monk knows what he did, and the demon is coming back.

## PAGE 29

Dramatic. The Yellow Robe crouches grinning in front of the monk at the pillar, a cup of water in his hand, and makes his bet aloud: a monk who will not hurt anyone, given claws and teeth; everyone is a beast underneath, and when the monk eats meat, the demon will eat him. Then he sprays a fine silver mist from his mouth over the monk, and the monk changes: swelling, black stripes rising through the saffron robe, the face becoming a tiger's with the monk's own dark eyes, the red kasaya tearing at the shoulder, the ropes falling slack, while Wujing strains away at the pillar. The dominant turn: the demon's bet, the spell of water, the monk becomes a tiger. The transformation owns the page; the bet sets it up. The reader turns because the monk is a tiger.

## PAGE 30

Illustrated prose. The monk is a tiger now, shut in an iron cage in the black hall, and the page belongs to his refusal. One iron lamp shows a thin tiger in a torn red kasaya sitting upright with his front paws together, his face turned to the wall, an untouched joint of meat on the cage floor. Wujing, tied to the pillar in the dark, watches. The prose tells the reader what the picture holds: a monk who never ate meat now has a tiger's hunger, and he will not eat. The dominant turn is the tiger turning his face from the meat while the shutter is pulled tight against the sky. The reader turns because someone outside has been watching. Wukong is far away and absent. The demon's bed, the lamps and the stonework stay subordinate; the quiet tiger is everything.

## PAGE 31

Spectacle. Night in the pine wood. The white horse, tied to his pine, has seen everything: the pig run, the ogre dragged inside. Then the reader learns his secret with him. In the dominant image the horse uncoils into a slim white dragon, sea-green eyes and pale-blue mane, the empty bridle falling away. In the last beat the dragon tears the shutter off the pagoda's high window and slides in with the moonlight behind him. The dominant turn is the horse becoming the dragon and going in. The reader turns because the dragon has gone in after the monk. Wukong is absent. The pagoda's storeys, the pines and the gate stay subordinate to the transformation; two short captions carry the words and the pictures carry the rest.

## PAGE 32

Spectacle, one image across the whole page. The dark hall, moonlight now falling through the torn window. The white dragon is coiled round the iron cage, wrenching at its bars, while the tiger inside looks up at him. The Yellow Robe has risen from his bed and his silver scimitar catches the dragon across the right foreleg. Wujing strains at his ropes at the pillar. The dominant turn is the rescue failing: the cut foreleg and the dragon driven out, the cage still shut. Two captions tell the attempt and the defeat. The reader turns because the dragon is hurt and the monk is still a prisoner. Wukong is absent. The wound is a clean dark gash, never blood; the furs, lamps and stonework stay behind the struggle.

## PAGE 33

Dramatic. Dawn at the stream by the pine wood. Bajie, who hid in the reeds all night, crawls out muddy and calling, then finds the white horse lying in the shallow water with a clean dark gash on his right foreleg and his bridle broken. A caption tells the reader the dragon could not hold his shape. Bajie kneels and asks who did this. Then the page's one turn: the horse lifts his head, sea-green eyes steady, and speaks for the first time in the book. The reader turns because the horse has spoken. Bajie owns the comedy of the first two beats, the horse owns the last. Wukong is absent. The reeds, the water and the far pagoda stay subordinate.

## PAGE 34

Dramatic. At the stream, the horse tells Bajie what has happened and what must be done. Bajie falls backward into the water at hearing him talk; the horse says only when it matters, that the master is a tiger in a cage and Wujing is tied, and that neither of them can beat the demon. Face to face, the horse tells him to bring back their big brother from Flower-Fruit Mountain, and Bajie confesses that he lied about the bones. The dominant turn is that confession, answered by the horse: then tell Wukong that, tell him the truth. The reader turns because the pig must go back for the monkey he sent away. Wukong is absent and only named. The stream and the woods stay subordinate to two faces.

## PAGE 35

Illustrated prose. Bajie owns the page alone: the worst flyer in the sky, going back. He sits on a small, lumpy, gray cloud that sags in the middle like an old mattress, high over a wide sea, eyes squeezed shut, rake clutched, ears flapping, and far ahead a green peak rises on the coast. The dominant turn is the flight itself: a pig who has never gone back for anybody and never told the truth when a lie would do is flying east to do both, rehearsing a truth that sounds terrible every time. The prose carries the comedy and the shame together; the picture carries the scale of the sea and how small and miserable he is on it. The reader turns because he has reached the monkey's mountain. Nobody else appears.

## PAGE 36

Dramatic. Bajie lands on Flower-Fruit Mountain and a swarm of young brown monkeys leaps on him, grabbing his ears and his rake. Inside the cave behind the waterfall they drag him before the stone throne, where Wukong lounges, the flower crown on top of the gold band, one foot up, eating a peach, not even looking: "I don't know any pigs. Throw him in the waterfall." The dominant turn is that refusal, and the comedy of it holding until the last line, when Bajie, flattened under sitting monkeys, pleads "It's me, Bajie!" and Wukong, still not looking, answers that he once knew a Bajie who talked a monk into sending him away. The monkeys are noise and weight, never the focus. The reader turns because Wukong knows exactly who he is.

## PAGE 37

Dramatic. Bajie tries the old way first: up on his knees, a huge smile, hands spread, he lies that the master misses Wukong, cries every night, and sent for him. The dominant turn is Wukong leaning forward on his throne, close, grinning without any fun in it: he saw through three faces of a demon, and he needs no magic eyes to see through a pig. There is no magic on this page; it is a plain look from someone who knows him. The smile slides off Bajie's face, and Wukong sits back and tells him to try again, the true one this time. The reader turns because the pig now has to tell the truth.

## PAGE 38

Dramatic. Bajie stops being funny. Sitting back on his heels, ears down, he confesses: he lied about the bones, he knew it was her, her name was right there on the skull. The dominant turn is the close on his face, small eyes wet, saying why: Wukong was always the fastest, the bossiest, the master's favourite, and Bajie wanted to be the one he liked best. Then, looking up, he tells what happened since: a demon in a yellow robe, big as a door, has made the monk a tiger, Wujing is tied up, the horse got cut and then talked, and sent him here. Wukong only listens, very still, the peach forgotten in his hand. The reader turns because Wukong has heard the truth and has to decide.

## PAGE 39

Dramatic. Wukong stands up off the throne, the flower crown slipping, and asks the hard question: the monk sent him away, said the words three times in one day; why should he go back? The dominant turn is Bajie's answer, on his feet and shouting, honest for once: he does not know, he hates flying, he hates being bossed, and he came all the way back anyway. That is what decides it. In the close that follows, Wukong lays his hand on the mended patch at the shoulder of his robe as the flower crown falls away behind him and the gold band shines on his brow; a pig came back for a monkey, on a cloud like that. He asks where the pagoda is, and Bajie, in frame, says he will show him, slowly. The reader turns because Wukong is going back.

## PAGE 40

Spectacle. One image owns the whole page: two clouds racing west over the sea toward the setting sun. In front, Wukong's bright cloud, Wukong crouched forward, the red staff in his fist, the gold band on his brow, the flower crown gone; behind him, Bajie's small lumpy gray cloud sagging and struggling to keep up, Bajie calling for him to wait. A caption says the one thing the picture cannot: a single somersault would have been faster, but only the pig knew the way, so the monkey waited for the pig all night and all the next day. The page is speed, sky and a joke about friendship. The reader turns because they are flying back to the pagoda.

## PAGE 41

Dramatic. Wukong is back at the pagoda. He lands in the dark pine clearing, warns off the limping white horse, then hammers the black-iron gate with his red staff and calls the demon out by name, calling himself the monk's first disciple. The dominant turn is that challenge at the gate: the small monkey with the band on his brow, beating on a door a giant lives behind. Then the gate opens and the huge Yellow Robe fills it, grinning, and knows the monkey was sent away. Wukong grins back: he came back anyway. The horse's hurt leg and Bajie's collapsing cloud stay small and behind. The reader turns because the fight nobody else could win is about to start.

## PAGE 42

Spectacle. One huge image of the fight in the clearing: the ground torn up, the pines bending, Wukong in mid-air with the red staff spinning, the Yellow Robe's silver scimitar meeting it in a burst of sparks. The caption reminds the reader who already lost to this demon: the pig, the ogre, the dragon. The dominant turn is that the monkey is holding where everyone else fell. Bajie and the limping white horse watch from the trees, small, subordinate. One sound at the point where staff meets blade. The reader turns because the monkey has not lost yet.

## PAGE 43

Dramatic. Staff and scimitar locked, faces close. The Yellow Robe boasts that nobody in the world beats him. Then the reader sees what Wukong sees: his eyes flare, and silver starlight shines through cracks in the demon's indigo face and robe. That is the dominant turn: "I know a star when I see one" — the demon is a star that ran away from the sky. In the last panel the demon staggers back, the scimitar falling, light pouring out of him, asking who told; Wukong says his eyes can see it, and that Heaven counts its stars every night. The fighting stays subordinate to the seeing. The reader turns because the demon has been seen.

## PAGE 44

Spectacle. Dusk at the pagoda. The Yellow Robe rises into the evening sky as a streak of silver light and becomes one bright star among the first stars. On the ground Bajie points up and shouts to chase him; Wukong, staff on his shoulder, does not chase. He says the star is going home before Heaven counts tonight, and he is already turning toward the pagoda door, because he has a monk to get. The dominant turn is the star going home and the monkey letting it go. The reader turns because the demon is gone and the monk is still inside.

## PAGE 45

Dramatic. Inside the black stone hall. Wukong snaps Wujing's ropes at the pillar with his bare hands; Wujing, head bowed, confesses he left the monk alone, and Wukong tells him nobody could have kept the monk from that demon. The dominant turn is the cage: lit by the iron lamp, the window boarded shut, the thin tiger shrinking into the far corner with the torn red kasaya at its throat and the monk's dark eyes, the meat untouched on the floor — and through Wukong's flaring eyes the pale ghost of the monk's own face inside the tiger. He tells his master not to be afraid. Then, close, he sees the untouched meat: the monk kept his rule even as a tiger. The reader turns because the monk is found and still a tiger.

## PAGE 46

Dramatic. The cage door is open. Bajie gapes in the doorway and asks how you undo a tiger; Wukong, lifting a water jar, says changing shapes is his trick, so he knows how to take one off. The dominant turn is the spray: Wukong blows a mouthful of water over the tiger, and in the silver spray the stripes fade and the monk's shape rises out of the tiger's, the red kasaya whole again. Then Sanzang, a man, kneels dripping on the cage floor and looks up: Wukong came back for him. Wukong teases him that he cannot even be a tiger properly. The reader turns because the monk is a man again and owes the monkey something.

## PAGE 47

Dramatic. The apology. Sanzang, still on his knees, says that in the cage he was a beast outside and himself inside, and nobody could see it but Wukong, which is what he did to Wukong. The dominant turn is the bow: close on the monk, palms together, bowing his head to the monkey, saying he should have believed him and is sorry. Then Wukong is hit by it: close, eyes wet, looking away, one hand on the mended patch at his shoulder, and he deflects with a joke about the pig — then says he heard it the first time. Nothing else happens; the faces carry the page. The reader turns because the monk has said sorry and the monkey has heard it.

## PAGE 48

Dramatic. Morning in the clearing, the company together. Bajie kneels in front of the monk, unasked, and confesses that he lied about the bones to be the favourite; the monk's hand rests on his head, and the penalty is the whole luggage pole, which Bajie accepts. The dominant turn is the new promise: Sanzang and Wukong face to face, the monk saying he will believe Wukong even when he cannot see it, Wukong asking what if he is wrong, the monk answering that they will be wrong together. Last, comic and small, Bajie begs the wounded white horse to talk; the horse looks away with his mouth shut and Wujing almost smiles. The reader turns because the horse will not say a word and the road is waiting.

## PAGE 49

Illustrated prose. The last page: the road west through green hills into far blue mountains in morning light. The monk walks leading the white horse, whose foreleg is bandaged; Wukong walks beside the monk, not ahead; Bajie carries the whole luggage pole alone behind them, grumbling, with Wujing beside him carrying only his spade. Far down the road, small, a woman comes toward them with a basket. The prose carries the turn: the monk stops and looks to Wukong first, and Wukong looks a long moment and tells him she is just a lady with lunch. The dominant turn is trust given and used well.
