before combining, reordering, or inventing, ask what can simply go.

## The cut list — named, unapologetic, famous material included

### Whole sequences cut

- **The four sages' test of resolve (ch 23)** — Bajie's marriage-greed test. A second "Bajie wants a wife" episode after Volume II's; no turn for Wukong or the monk.
- **The ginseng-fruit tree at the Temple of the Five Villages (ch 24–26)** — a famous, whole three-chapter adventure about stealing fruit; it changes nothing the monk or the monkey chooses about each other, and it would cost twelve pages.
- **The Kingdom of Baoxiang (ch 28–31)**: the Princess's letter, the king, the court, the Yellow Robe arriving at court as a handsome son-in-law, the monk framed as a tiger in front of the king, the drinking palace maids. The tiger stays; the court that it was for goes.
- **Wukong's trip to Heaven to roll-call the twenty-eight constellations (ch 31)** and the Jade Emperor's punishment of the star.
- **Wukong disguised as the Princess** to trick the Yellow Robe (ch 31). Volume II already had the bride disguise; this book does not repeat a trick.
- **The hunters who burned Flower-Fruit Mountain and Wukong killing them (ch 28).**
- **Bajie's provocation lie (ch 31)** — "the demon insulted you." Replaced by its opposite: Bajie's confession.
- **The written letter of dismissal (ch 27).** Spoken instead, to the monkey's face.

### Characters cut

- Guanyin (named once as "the lady in white who sent us all"; no appearance)
- the Princess of Baoxiang (Hundred Flowers)
- the King of Baoxiang, his ministers and court
- the Yellow Robe's two sons
- the mountain gods and local spirits
- the twenty-eight constellations and the Jade Emperor
- the hunters of Flower-Fruit Mountain
- the four sages; the Great Immortal Zhenyuan; the ginseng-fruit children

### Characters merged

- **The local spirits Wukong calls to witness + the third blow → Wukong's speed.** He strikes before she can slip out of the face, so the bones are hers.
- **The Princess, the court, the framing → the Yellow Robe's cruelty.** One reason for the tiger, visible on the page: he wants to see a gentle monk with claws.
- **The star-roll in Heaven → Wukong's eyes.** The same eyes that saw through three faces see the star under the demon's.
- **The White Bone Demon's three disguises → one locked character, four sheets.** One face does four jobs; the reader sees the true one under each.

### Why the biggest cut pays for the volume

Cutting Baoxiang deletes a kingdom, a king, a court, a princess, a letter,
two sons, a disguise and a trip to Heaven — eight names and four locations —
and with them the whole second half of the source's Yellow Robe story, which
is about a palace intrigue no reader of this series cares about. What is left
is the part the reader does care about: the monk alone without Wukong, a
demon nobody else can beat, the gentle monk made into a beast, the horse that
speaks, and somebody going back. The tiger survives the cut because it is the
best image in the source and because it turns the monk's rule into something
the reader can see: claws, meat, and a monk who will not.

## Protected claims

None beyond the owner's series decisions: standard pinyin names (Wukong,
Sanzang, Bajie, Wujing, Guanyin); the company fixed (Wukong, Sanzang, Bajie,
Wujing, the white horse); the gold band on throughout.

## Sensitive material and its treatment

- **Wukong strikes three people who look human.** The reader is shown the
  demon's true shape before and under every disguise, so the reader is never
  asked to watch a hero hit a girl or an old man. No blood, ever. The first
  two "bodies" are empty skins (shown as hollow, wrong, drained figures
  lying face-down; never a wound); the third is a heap of clean white bones.
- **The band's pain.** Shown through Wukong's hands on his head, his rolling,
  his face; never as injury.
- **The tiger.** Frightening, and also the monk; never shown eating. The meat
  is a plain joint on a bone, untouched.
- **The dragon's wound.** A gash on a foreleg, no blood pooling; he limps.
- **The ending of the White Bone Lady.** She is destroyed; her bones remain.
  The Yellow Robe is not killed: he goes back to the sky as a star.
