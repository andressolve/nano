# RESEARCH NOTES — "How Rome Became the World"
### (sequel to "Why Rome Kept Winning"; the honest story of how the winning machine changed and dissolved)

All web-verified June 2026. **This is NOT a misconception-busting book.** The frame is an honest
follow-up to book one: book one explained *why a machine built to lose a battle and still win the war
kept winning* (discipline, manpower/socii, resilience, adaptation). This book asks the natural next
question — **how does a thing like that ever stop?** — and tells the actual story. The honest answer:
it was not knocked down from outside. Each of book one's strengths, followed far enough across
centuries, became the thing that transformed it; and "Rome" did not so much end as change shape and
move east, lasting another thousand years.

---

## THE SPINE (mirrors book one, pillar by pillar)

Book one's four pillars and what becomes of each:
- **Manpower (the socii)** → the allies demand to *be* Rome → Social War → citizenship → the Italian
  manpower base becomes Rome itself.
- **The army** (book one: citizen-soldiers + allied troops) → gradually professionalized across the
  late Republic → soldiers loyal to the general who pays and settles them, not the Republic → civil
  wars → one-man rule (Augustus, the Empire).
- **Resilience (the civic centre)** → as power concentrates in one emperor, the centre becomes the
  single prize everyone fights for → the Third-Century Crisis is Romans destroying Romans for the
  throne → resilience inverts into self-destruction.
- **Adaptation (absorb whatever works)** → Rome had always absorbed outsiders; now it absorbs whole
  armed nations (foederati) inside the borders → when absorption finally fails (Adrianople, the sacks)
  the newcomers inherit the West → it dissolves into the world it had made Roman.

**Through-line / closing:** the same three strengths that made Rome win each, taken far enough, became
what changed it. Book one: *order was where it began.* Book two: *the world it ordered is where it
went.* And in the East it never even ended — they went on calling themselves **Romans** for a thousand
more years.

## VERIFIED BEATS, DATES, FIGURES

### 1. The Social War (91–88 BC) — the manpower pillar cracks from inside
- The Italian allies (*socii*) — book one's manpower pillar, ~half of every Roman army — revolted
  because Rome refused them citizenship. Trigger: the reformist tribune **Marcus Livius Drusus**
  proposed extending citizenship; his assassination (91 BC) lit the war.
- Rebels (Marsi, Samnites, others) formed a breakaway confederation **"Italia"** with its own capital
  and coinage.
- Rome won the war by **conceding the cause**: the **Lex Julia (90 BC)** granted citizenship to
  communities that had *not* revolted; the **Lex Plautia Papiria (89 BC)** let individuals from other
  allied towns claim it by registering before a praetor. Holdouts in Samnium/Lucania settled by ~87 BC.
- **Honest takeaway:** the allies didn't destroy Rome — they *joined* it. The thing that gave Rome its
  armies became Rome. Direct, beautiful continuity from book one.

### 2. The army goes professional → loyal to generals → civil war → Augustus
- ⚠️ **EDITORIAL-HONESTY FLAG (important):** Do NOT credit a single dramatic "Marian reform" (107 BC)
  that created the professional army. **21st-century specialists call the comprehensive "Marian
  reforms" a construct of modern (19th-c., Mommsen) scholarship** — there are no ancient sources for a
  large-scale Marius reform package. The change was **gradual**, emerging from circumstance (the Social
  War and the civil wars), and the long-service professional standing army was really only completed
  **under Augustus**. So the book says: *across the late Republic the army slowly became professional*,
  NOT "Marius reformed it in 107 BC." Marius can be named as part of the trend, not its single author.
- Professional soldiers served long term and depended on their **general** for pay and land on
  discharge → loyalty shifted from the Republic to the commander. Marius, **Sulla** (marched on Rome),
  **Julius Caesar** (crossed the Rubicon), then Antony vs. Octavian — a century of civil war.
- Ends with **Augustus** (sole power from 27 BC): Republic → Empire. The army pillar has changed shape.

### 3. The Empire at its height — the Pax Romana
- Roughly 1st–2nd c. AD. Greatest extent under **Trajan** (d. AD 117): from Britain to the Euphrates.
- Roads, law, cities, common coinage, ~two centuries of relative internal peace (Pax Romana).
- **Honest tension to plant:** bigger means a longer frontier to hold and a single throne that becomes
  the ultimate prize. Grandeur and vulnerability are the same fact.

### 4. The Crisis of the Third Century (AD 235–284) — resilience turns on itself
- ~50 years of near-collapse. **~26 men claimed the title of emperor** (50+ if usurpers counted), most
  murdered by their own troops after months.
- **Plague of Cyprian (c. 249–262)** gutted population, farmland, and army manpower.
- **Currency debasement:** the silver content of the denarius / antoninianus fell until mid-century
  coins held almost no silver → severe inflation.
- The empire briefly **fractured into three**: the **Gallic Empire** (c. 260–274) in the west and the
  **Palmyrene Empire** (c. 270–273) in the east, with the Roman centre between.
- **Honest takeaway:** the deadliest enemy was internal — Romans fighting Romans for the throne. The
  civic resilience of book one (everyone has a stake) inverts when the stake is supreme power.

### 5. The last great adaptation — Diocletian & Constantine; the centre moves east
- **Diocletian** (r. 284–305) ended the crisis and rebuilt the state. **Tetrarchy (293):** rule of four
  — two senior *Augusti* + two junior *Caesars* — split administration West/East; smaller provinces;
  civil and military commands separated. (Honest: the Tetrarchy's *succession* design failed after him,
  but the structural East/West division stuck.)
- **Constantine** reunited the empire (324) and founded a new capital at Byzantium, renamed
  **Constantinople (AD 330)**. The empire's real weight shifts east — richer, more urban, more
  defensible.
- After **Theodosius I (d. 395)** the East/West halves are never again ruled as one. Two empires now.

### 6. Foederati — the army of the West fills with the frontier peoples
- The late-Roman army increasingly recruited and settled Germanic and other frontier peoples inside the
  borders as **foederati** (federated allies under treaty, fighting under their own leaders).
- This is book one's adaptation pillar at its limit: Rome had always absorbed outsiders; now it is
  absorbing whole armed nations whose loyalty is conditional.

### 7. Absorption fails — Adrianople, then the sacks
- **376:** Gothic groups (Thervingi/Greuthungi under **Fritigern**, **Alavivus**) flee the **Huns** and
  are admitted across the **Danube** as refugees/foederati. Roman officials **starve and abuse them** →
  revolt.
- **9 August 378 — Battle of Adrianople:** Eastern emperor **Valens** killed; **~two-thirds of the
  eastern field army destroyed.** Often marked as the start of the chain that ends the Western Empire.
- **382:** Theodosius settles the Goths as autonomous foederati inside the empire (a state within the
  state).
- ⚠️ Capital of the West had already moved: **Milan (286) → Ravenna (402)** for defensibility — so by
  the 5th c. **Rome itself was no longer the capital.**
- **24 August 410 — Sack of Rome by Alaric's Visigoths** (3 days). First foreign capture of the city in
  ~800 years; shocking symbolically, though Rome wasn't the seat of government.
- **455 — Sack of Rome by the Vandals under Gaiseric** (2 weeks; generally considered more destructive
  than 410).

### 8. 476 — the quiet end in the West
- **4 September 476:** the Germanic commander **Odoacer** deposed the boy-emperor **Romulus Augustulus**,
  declined to appoint a successor, and **sent the western imperial regalia to the eastern emperor Zeno**,
  ruling Italy as king while nominally recognizing Zeno (and the still-living claimant **Julius Nepos**,
  d. 480).
- **Honest texture:** 476 was *not* a dramatic catastrophe at the time — the title simply lapsed. Latin,
  Roman law, the Senate, the roads, the cities continued; Gothic and Frankish **kingdoms** now ruled a
  West that remained, in a hundred everyday ways, Roman. By convention historians mark 476 as the end of
  the Western Empire, but they note it's a convention.

### 9. The East does not fall — and keeps the name
- The Eastern Empire continued for **~1,000 more years**, to the **fall of Constantinople, 1453**.
- ⚠️ **Honest detail (great closing beat):** its people called themselves **Romans** — *Rhomaioi* — and
  their state *the Roman Empire*; they did not think they lived in a successor state. **"Byzantine" is a
  modern (16th-c.) label**, coined by western scholars to separate the classical Rome they admired from
  the Greek-speaking Christian empire they didn't. So "Rome" didn't end in 476; it moved its heart east
  and went on calling itself Rome for another millennium.

## EDITORIAL-HONESTY FLAGS (must hold in the prose)

1. **No single "Marian reform" of 107 BC.** Professionalization was gradual, completed under Augustus.
   (Biggest factual trap on this subject.)
2. **Don't replace one myth with another.** This is NOT "actually Rome never fell, it just gently
   transformed." There is a real scholarly debate: Peter Brown / Walter Goffart's *transformation* /
   "Late Antiquity" view vs. **Bryan Ward-Perkins & Peter Heather**, who show the 5th-c. West suffered a
   **real, catastrophic collapse in material living standards** (trade, towns, pottery, literacy). The
   honest position the book takes: *the political title faded quietly in 476, but for ordinary people in
   the West the 5th century brought genuine disruption and decline — while the East thrived.* Hold both.
3. **476 is a convention, not a thunderclap.** Say so plainly; don't dramatize it as "the day Rome fell."
4. **Rome ≠ the city by then.** The capital was Ravenna from 402; the sacks were symbolic shocks, not
   decapitations of the government.
5. **The "barbarians" were not a foreign tidal wave.** They were peoples Rome had fought, hired, recruited,
   and settled for centuries — many were Roman soldiers and Christians; Odoacer ruled *as* a Roman
   officer-king. Frame absorption-then-inheritance, not invasion-from-the-void.
6. **The East kept the name "Roman."** "Byzantine" is a later coinage — use it only to explain the label,
   never as if they'd have recognized it.
7. **Casualty/figure discipline:** "~two-thirds of the eastern army" (Adrianople), "~26 emperors" (give
   as "more than twenty"/"dozens of claimants"), Third-Century span 235–284. Keep ranges, avoid false
   precision.
8. **No fake ancient quotes.** Any aphoristic closing line is the author's own, clearly the narrator's
   voice (same rule as book one).
