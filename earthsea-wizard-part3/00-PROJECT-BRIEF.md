# A Wizard of Earthsea - Part Three: The Dragon of Pendor

## Purpose

This is the direct continuation of `../earthsea-wizard-part2/`. It begins where Part Two ends, with eighteen-year-old Ged sailing west from Roke to take his first post as a wizard.

## Scope

- Source coverage: Chapter 5, "The Dragon of Pendor," only.
- Length: cover + 32 landscape pages.
- Arc: Ged arrives on Low Torning wanting to serve quietly, fails to save Pechvarry's dying son, discovers that the shadow can find him, and chooses to discharge his duty to the Ninety Isles before he flees.
- Ending: Yevaud swears never to fly east of Pendor. Ged sails back toward Low Torning with the islands safe and his own danger unresolved.

Chapter 6, "Hunted," is reserved for Part Four. It contains a separate full arc: the shadow's attack, Roke's warding wind, Ged's wandering flight, Osskil, Skiorh, and the threshold of the Court of the Terrenon. Folding it into this volume would compress two different stories and repeat the problem the expanded edition exists to correct.

## One-sentence pitch

On his first charge as a wizard, Ged fails to save one child—then sails alone against an ancient dragon and its nine sons so the thing hunting him will not inherit an island full of victims.

## Editorial center

The dragon fight is the spectacle, but the volume is about service.

Part One showed power awakening. Part Two showed power misused and disciplined. Part Three asks what disciplined power is for. Ged's decisive act is not killing young dragons or guessing Yevaud's name; it is refusing the answer to his own mortal problem because he has only one bargain to spend, and it belongs to the people who trusted him.

The failed healing and the dragon bargain must mirror one another:

1. Pechvarry trusts Ged to do what cannot be done.
2. Ged crosses a boundary because he cannot bear to disappoint him.
3. The child dies, and the shadow learns where Ged is.
4. Ged accepts the limit he denied in the sickroom.
5. At Pendor he uses knowledge within its proper limit and saves the living people in his charge.

## Pacing commitments

- Low Torning receives seven full pages before the illness. Pechvarry must become Ged's friend through work, food, weather, and sailing—not through one explanatory caption.
- The sickroom and Dry Land receive eleven pages. The child's death is not a plot device squeezed between adventures; it changes Ged's understanding of mastery.
- The decision to face Pendor receives four pages. Duty must be visible as a choice made under fear.
- Pendor receives ten pages. The young-dragon action is readable, Yevaud's scale is earned, and the bargain has room for temptation, refusal, naming, and oath.

## Canon and visual laws

1. Ged is the locked scarred identity from Part Two: deep red-brown copper skin, straight black hair, four pale healed scars on the left cheek, dark travel cloak, plain tunic, yew staff.
2. The people of the Ninety Isles are brown- and black-skinned Archipelagans. Crowds must not drift pale.
3. Hoeg remains a large-rat/tiny-cat-sized otak, not a dog or fox.
4. The shadow is absolute flat matte black, faceless and textureless. In the Dry Land it waits on the living side of the low wall.
5. The Dry Land is not a glowing fantasy underworld. It is starless, windless, grey-black, dry, and without warmth. The low wall is ordinary rough stone.
6. Earthsea dragons are lean, ancient, and dangerous rather than bulky Western-fantasy monsters. Yevaud is iron-grey, long-bodied, tower-scale, and cunning. His eyes and breath may carry restrained furnace-gold; no neon fire.
7. Pendor is a drowned ruined city and broken harbor, not a mountain lair or treasure cave.
8. The dragon change must remain visibly Ged in motion and intent; it is a perilous temporary transformation, not a superhero power-up.

## Moderation and depiction notes

- Ioeth is a sick child. Show a bundled child, worried adults, hands, and faces; no medical gore, exposed body, or corpse close-up.
- The child's death is conveyed by stillness, adult grief, extinguished light, and Ged's reaction. Do not show the child's dead face.
- Young dragons fall into the sea or retreat wounded. No torn bodies, severed parts, or explicit blood. The one trace of dragon blood in the source is not needed for this adaptation.
- The shadow threatens Ged through position and silence. It does not visibly injure the child.

## Source policy

There is no complete Chapter 5 source text in this workspace. Planning was cross-checked against the existing compressed adaptation in `../earthsea/`, the [National Endowment for the Arts audio guide](https://www.arts.gov/stories/other/wizard-earthsea-audio-guide), and detailed Chapter 5 summaries from [LitCharts](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/a-wizard-of-earthsea/chapter-5) and the [Earthsea reread](https://www.reddit.com/r/UrsulaKLeGuin/comments/et93zq/earthsea_reread_a_wizard_of_earthsea_chapter_5/).

All production captions and dialogue in `04-SCRIPT.md` are original adaptation prose. They are not presented as quotations from Le Guin. Names, places, and unavoidable story terms retain their canonical forms.

Before generation, a reader with the novel in hand should perform one canon pass on the page sequence. If any story fact conflicts with the book, correct the script before lettering is baked into art.

## WHY-quiz seed

1. Why does Ged try to follow Ioeth when he already knows the child is dying?
2. Why is Hoeg's simple act of staying beside Ged important after the failed healing?
3. Why can Ged not simply flee Low Torning as soon as the shadow finds him?
4. Why does Yevaud's offer of the shadow's name tempt Ged more than treasure?
5. Why does Ged spend his advantage on an oath protecting the islands instead of on saving himself?
