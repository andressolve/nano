The Second Crisis Was Over

Foundation — Book Two: The Priests

Thirty years after his bloodless coup, Salvor Hardin ruled four kingdoms without an army — through a religion whose miracles were machines and whose every priest answered to Terminus. When Wienis aimed a resurrected Imperial battleship at the Foundation, Hardin let him aim it: the priest aboard cursed the ship dark, the interdict blacked out a kingdom at midnight, and the mob came for the Regent with torches. A blaster beam broke on a force shield like water on glass. The religion of science, Hardin said, was their bridle and saddle. But Seldon's hologram left a warning folded inside the victory: spiritual power can defend — it cannot attack. One quiz stands between you and the end — it tests the WHY, not the what.

Test Your Understanding

1. Why did Hardin agree to repair the giant battlecruiser for Wienis — the man planning to use it against Terminus?
Right — "I have handed him a lesson." The gift was a hostage. The relay waited silently in the wiring for the Foundation's signal, and the ship could not sail unblessed — so a priest loyal to Terminus stood on its own bridge. Wienis spent months polishing the weapon Hardin would use against him.
Not quite. No treaty forced Hardin's hand, and he never governed by fear of losing votes — he agreed over the Council's fury. The repair armed his own trap: a hidden shut-down device buried in the wiring, and a Terminus-trained priest standing on the bridge of Wienis's own flagship.
2. Why did the Foundation wrap its atomic power in a religion at all, instead of just selling machines?
Right — the religion was a control system. Four kingdoms ran their lights, medicine and power plants on Foundation machines that only consecrated priests could touch — and the priesthood's loyalty ran back to Terminus. That is how a single city ruled kingdoms that could each have crushed it in open war.
Not quite. The kingdoms wanted the machines badly — no disguise was needed to sell them — and Seldon's Plan was mathematics, not faith. The religion was a control system: the kingdoms depended on power only priests could run, and every priest was trained on Terminus and answered to it.
3. Why did the flagship's crew turn on their own admiral instead of obeying his order to seize Aporat?
Right — the kill switch and the curse were the same event seen two ways. The relay shut the ship down deck by deck at the Foundation's signal; to a crew raised on the Galactic Spirit, a priest had cursed the ship and the ship had died on cue. "Both things were true enough."
Not quite. There were no hidden soldiers, and the story gives no brutal-captain grudge — the crew's terror was real faith. Raised on the religion of science, they watched the ship die deck by deck at the exact moment of the curse, and knew the Spirit's wrath when they saw it.
4. At midnight the priests blacked out every machine on Anacreon. Why did the furious mob march on Wienis — and not on the Foundation whose machines had all just failed?
Right — a blackout by itself is just darkness; the religion gave it a meaning and an address. The interdict was proclaimed as the Spirit's judgment on Wienis, and the High Priest stood on the temple steps naming him while the torches gathered. The fury was manufactured, timed, and aimed.
Not quite. The boy king ordered nothing, and the blackout spared no one — the whole kingdom went dark. What aimed the mob was the story told about the darkness: the priests proclaimed it the Spirit's judgment on the Regent, and Verisof stood on the temple steps naming Wienis while the torches gathered.
5. Why does Seldon warn that the Spiritual Power, which had just won the crisis, "is not sufficient to attack in turn"?
Right — the religion works on kingdoms that already depend on Foundation machines. But every ruler in the Galaxy has now seen what happened to Wienis, and none will let a priest of the Galactic Spirit inside the gates. Defense is solved; growth is not. The next crisis will be won by something else — and Book Three is named after the people who carry it.
Not quite. The Plan forbids nothing — it predicts; and the people's faith was stronger than ever after the interdict worked. The real limit: religion controls only those who already depend on Foundation machines. Rulers who watched Wienis fall will never invite the priesthood in — so expansion needs a different tool.
