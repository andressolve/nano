## What this volume adapts

Volume II draws on the Morcerf thread only — material scattered across Dumas
chapters 39–41, 53–56, and 84–92, pulled out of the interleaved Paris braid and
run as one continuous arc.

| Albert's warmth and hospitality | Retained whole; it is the volume's cost | P7, P10, P35–44 |

## The cut list

Named, unapologetic, famous material included. Not defended.

### Whole sequences cut

- **All of Rome.** Sinbad the Sailor, the blindfolded voyage, the hashish
  grotto, the Colosseum eavesdrop, Signor Pastrini, the Carnival, the
  moccoletti, the Countess G, the Piazza del Popolo execution and its
  last-second reprieve, Luigi Vampa's camp, Albert's kidnapping and ransom.
  Roughly thirty pages of Dumas. **Zero pages here.**
- **The house at Auteuil and Bertuccio's confession** — Villefort burying the
  living child, the Corsican vendetta, Benedetto's origin. → Volume III.
- **The Paris breakfast party.**
- **The entire Villefort poisoning plot** — Madame de Villefort, Valentine,
  Noirtier, Barrois, d'Avrigny. → Volume III.
- **Benedetto and the false Cavalcanti nobility.** → Volume III.
- **Danglars' financial destruction** — the telegraph fraud, the Spanish bonds,
  Madame Danglars and Debray's speculation, Eugénie's elopement. → Volume IV.
- **Maximilien Morrel and Valentine.** → Volume III.
- **Caderousse's Paris career** — the diamond, the jeweller, the burglary, his
  death.
- **The Count's party tricks** — the runaway horses and the rescue of Madame de
  Villefort, the emerald pillbox, the Sinbad theatrics, the displays of Ali.

### Why Rome goes — the one cut that pays for the volume

Dumas needed Franz d'Épinay because his reader had not seen Dantès for two
hundred pages and had to meet the Count from **outside**, as a mystery.

**We spent fifty-five pages inside him.** Importing an outsider POV would throw
away the single largest advantage this series has over its source. The reader
already knows what is behind the Count's face; the pleasure available to us is
dramatic irony, and dramatic irony requires that we stay *with* him, not watch
him from across a room.

Rome's only plot function is manufacturing a debt so that Albert will open a
door. That is replaced by one line of characterization:

> **Fernand invites the Count himself.**

Because the Count is the sensation of Paris and Fernand is a parvenu who
collects legitimacy. He brings his own destroyer to dinner, and pays for the
candles. Thirty pages of machinery deleted, and Fernand is better drawn for it.

## Deliberate departures from Dumas

Three, each stated so no later gate mistakes them for errors.

**1. Morcerf falls first, not third.**
An arc argument. Fernand is the only one of the three whose destruction costs
the Count anything — Mercédès lives in that house, and Albert is her son.
Placing him first means the price is established *before* the reader settles
comfortably into rooting for the revenge, and Volumes III and IV are then read
in its shadow. Fernand's fall is also the most self-contained of the three:
Janina → exposure → suicide needs nothing from the Danglars or Villefort plots,
which are load-bearing on each other and belong together later.

**2. Mercédès recognizes Edmond early.**
In Dumas she knows quietly and says nothing for hundreds of pages. Here she
knows him on P12 — nine pages in — and the volume's spine becomes two people
who both know, neither saying it, for twenty pages, until she says his name in
P38 to ask him for her son's life. This converts the book's best-kept secret
into its best-sustained tension.

**3. Haydée drives.**
In Dumas she waits in a room until she is needed and is largely an exhibit. The
abandoned volume gave her **one line in fifty-six pages.** Here she is the
reason Fernand is first: she overrules the Count's own plan on P3–P4, and she
pushes him faster than he means to go. Her want is her own, it is older than
his, and it is not negotiable.

## What is preserved, non-negotiably

- **Distinct moral responsibility.** Danglars wrote the letter, Fernand posted
  it, Villefort buried it. The conspiracy never collapses into "three jealous
  men." Volume I established each; Volume II must not blur them when it takes
  only one.
- **Fernand is not a cartoon.** He gets a genuine defense in the Chamber and he
  is nearly winning it when Haydée walks in. A villain who cannot almost win is
  not worth destroying.
- **Albert's agency.** He is not a prop for his parents' story. He challenges,
  he learns, and he chooses — publicly, at cost.
- **Mercédès as an active chooser.** She is not rescued, not passive, and not
  punished by the narrative. She works out the truth herself, she acts on it,
  and she walks away from the money on her own terms.
- **Haydée as agent, not décor.**
- The volume's final image and moral fork.
