# Cold read — canonical Pages 1–40

## Verdict

APPROVED

## 1. What happened and why

Nine years after a ruined shipowner unexpectedly recovered his ship, the Count of Monte Cristo arrived in Paris with immense unexplained wealth and deliberately took a house from which he could watch three enemies: the banker Danglars, the King's Attorney Villefort, and Fernand Mondego, now the Comte de Morcerf. Haydée, who lives with the Count and was rescued by him after being sold into slavery, pressed him to begin with Fernand because Fernand betrayed her father, Ali Tebelen, surrendered Janina, took payment for it, and then sold Haydée and her mother.

The Count entered the Morcerf household through Albert, Fernand and Mercédès's son. At the Morcerf party he quietly tested Fernand about Greece, established financial leverage with Danglars, unsettled Villefort by implying that forgotten acts would be judged, and met Mercédès, who recognized something of Edmond in him. Mercédès's offer of fruit and questions about his birthplace confirmed that she recognized him more deeply than she said aloud.

The Count then gave Haydée's documentary evidence to Danglars in a form that encouraged the banker to verify Fernand's past. Danglars caused an accusation to appear in the newspaper. Albert forced his journalist friend Beauchamp to investigate rather than retract it, and Beauchamp found witnesses and records in Janina confirming the charge. Fernand demanded a public hearing, apparently expecting the anonymous accuser to be unable to prove the case. Haydée entered the Chamber, identified herself and her father, and produced Fernand's signed receipt for the sale of herself and her mother. Fernand was condemned and stripped of his public standing.

Albert learned that the woman who destroyed his father's defense lived in the Count's house. He confronted the Count publicly and challenged him to a pistol duel the next morning. Haydée understood that the Count had wanted this confrontation and feared for him. Mercédès then came to the Count, called him Edmond, and asked him not to kill her son. He agreed not to raise his hand even if Albert fired. By Page 40 he had settled his estate for Haydée and put his affairs in order, accepting that the revenge he set in motion might now cost him his life.

## 2. People and identity clarity

- The Count of Monte Cristo is Edmond, a man from Mercédès and Fernand's Marseille past whom they believed dead. His exact earlier imprisonment is referenced but not retold in detail; that omission does not obstruct this sequence.
- Haydée is Ali Tebelen's daughter. Fernand's betrayal caused her father's death and her own enslavement; the Count later bought her freedom and has protected her for four years.
- Fernand Mondego is now the Comte de Morcerf, Mercédès's husband and Albert's father. He built his title and reputation on the concealed betrayal at Janina.
- Mercédès is Edmond's former beloved and Fernand's wife. She knows Fernand's guilt and recognizes the Count as Edmond.
- Albert is Mercédès and Fernand's twenty-two-year-old son. He begins as the Count's admiring social sponsor and ends as his would-be opponent.
- Danglars is the banker holding the Count's money and one of the enemies under observation. Villefort is the King's Attorney and another enemy. Beauchamp is Albert's journalist friend, whose investigation establishes the accusation independently.

There was no consequential identity confusion. The Count's shift from the title “Monte Cristo” to Mercédès calling him “Edmond” reads as a revelation, not as a second character. The older men in the political and financial scenes remain distinguishable by role, dress, and context.

## 3. Causality, attribution, text, and order

The sequence did not require stopping or backtracking. The revenge chain is legible: Haydée supplies the motive and proof; the Count gives Danglars an incentive to investigate; the newspaper allegation forces Albert and Beauchamp to seek confirmation; the confirmed accusation reaches the Chamber; Haydée's testimony destroys Fernand; Albert traces her to the Count and issues the challenge.

Speech attribution remained clear through staging and tails, including the quieter exchanges between the Count and Mercédès and between the Count and Haydée. All story-bearing text was comfortably readable at the supplied proof size.

Page 32 compresses the transition from Fernand's demand in a crowded Chamber to Haydée's entrance, briefly showing the hall without the assembled peers before the hearing resumes on Page 33. This reads as a dramatic transition rather than a break in page order, and Page 33 immediately establishes who entered and why.

## 4. What changed

- **The Count:** He moves from remote planner to exposed participant. His revenge against Fernand succeeds, but Albert's challenge and Mercédès's recognition force him to choose between completing vengeance and sparing an innocent son. He chooses not to kill Albert and prepares for his own possible death.
- **Haydée:** She moves from privately demanding action to publicly naming herself, presenting the receipt, and becoming the decisive witness against Fernand. After victory, she sees that the Count has engineered a danger to himself and openly fears losing him.
- **Fernand:** He moves from decorated, socially secure Comte de Morcerf to a publicly proven traitor and trafficker whose name is removed from the Chamber. His attempted demand for proof brings forward the witness who ruins him.
- **Mercédès:** She moves from guarded recognition at the party to openly naming the Count as Edmond. She admits that she has understood his revenge and asks him to spare Albert, while also acknowledging why he acted.
- **Albert:** He moves from enthusiastic admirer and host to defender of his father's honor. Once Fernand falls, Albert concludes that the Count orchestrated the disgrace and challenges him, without yet knowing the full shared history revealed in the Mercédès scene.

## 5. Page 40 continuation turn

The reason to continue is clear and urgent. The duel is set for the next morning; the Count has promised Mercédès that he will not raise his hand; Albert does not know this; and the Count has completed a will-like settlement for Haydée. Page 40 therefore turns a successful revenge plot into a question of whether the Count will let an innocent young man kill him, whether Albert will learn the truth in time, and what Haydée and Mercédès will do next.
