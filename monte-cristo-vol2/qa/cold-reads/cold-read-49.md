## Story retell

Nine years after an imprisoned Marseille sailor was believed dead, a fabulously wealthy stranger buys a Paris house chosen for its view of three roofs. He is the Count of Monte Cristo, secretly Edmond Dantès. With him is Haydée, the daughter of Ali Tebelen, Pasha of Janina, whom he rescued from slavery. The Count has come to destroy three men: Baron Danglars, the banker; Villefort, the King's Attorney; and Fernand Mondego, now the celebrated Comte de Morcerf.

Haydée wants Fernand first because he betrayed her father. As a French officer trusted with Janina's eastern gate, Fernand opened it to the enemy for money. Ali was killed; Fernand then sold Haydée and her mother into slavery and signed the receipt. Haydée's mother died soon afterward. The Count has preserved that receipt and plans to make Fernand demand a public hearing rather than merely suffer an accusation in print.

Albert de Morcerf, Fernand's son, eagerly introduces the Count to Paris society and his parents. At the Morcerf party the Count needles Fernand about Greece, meets Danglars and Villefort, and recognizes Albert's mother, Mercédès, as the woman he once loved. Mercédès recognizes Edmond despite his new identity. Her offer of fruit and questions about his birthplace confirm it between them, but neither says his name. The Count goes home dwelling on every detail of his enemies while deliberately refusing to dwell on Mercédès.

The Count uses Danglars's vanity and suspicion to start the mechanism. He suggests investing money behind Fernand, hints that the fortune came from Greece, and lets Danglars decide to request evidence. A reply identifying Fernand as the paid betrayer of Janina is passed anonymously to the newspaper. Albert confronts the journalist Beauchamp and demands a retraction. Beauchamp refuses to withdraw an allegation before checking it, travels to Janina, confirms the surrender and payment from records and witnesses, and returns with evidence strong enough to print.

The Chamber of Peers agrees to hear Fernand. Fernand expects to defeat a newspaper charge and challenges the accusation's author to stand and name himself. Instead Haydée enters. She identifies herself and her father, describes Fernand's trusted position, and produces his signed receipt for selling her and her mother. The Chamber strips Fernand's name from its door. His title, public honor, and political standing vanish within hours.

Albert traces Haydée to the Count's Champs-Élysées house and understands that the Count arranged his father's fall. He challenges the Count to a pistol duel. Haydée sees that the revenge she longed for has put an innocent twenty-two-year-old in danger, mirroring her own loss at eleven. Mercédès comes to the Count, calls him Edmond, and reveals that she has always known what Fernand and Danglars did in 1815: they framed Edmond at her betrothal dinner so Fernand could marry her. She asks Edmond not to kill her son. She also tells him plainly that this vengeance was staged so she would witness it. The Count agrees to stand at the duel and not return Albert's fire, privately settling his estate because he expects to die.

Mercédès then tells Albert the truth. At the Bois, Albert publicly withdraws his challenge because the newspaper was right, refuses to fight Edmond Dantès, and rejects Fernand's name and inheritance. The Count, who had prepared himself to kill Albert and then to die for Mercédès, survives but feels no relief.

Fernand comes to the Count's house armed and furious that Albert would not shoot. The Count seats him and recounts the evidence of Fernand's character: Fernand watched him refuse fruit in the Morcerf house and knew what that recognition meant, yet did nothing; now he has entered an enemy's house and accepted its drink. The Count names the Pharaon, the 1815 conspiracy, Danglars's letter, and Fernand's act of posting it. He finally makes Fernand look at him and says, “I am Edmond Dantès.”

Fernand returns to an emptied house. Mercédès and Albert have taken none of the wealth because they know where it came from. Fernand sees the abandoned pistol case and shoots himself. Mercédès and Albert leave together with only what is cleanly theirs. Outside, the Count watches their carriage disappear and counts “One”: Fernand is the first of the three roofs to fall. The final lit window belongs to Villefort, the King's Attorney, still working late. It matters because the revenge has not ended; the second target is awake under the next roof, and the Count is already turning toward him.

## Cast

- The Count of Monte Cristo / Edmond Dantès: the wealthy stranger and hidden former prisoner directing the revenge.
- Haydée: Ali Tebelen's daughter, survivor and witness, whose signed sale receipt destroys Fernand publicly.
- Fernand Mondego / Comte de Morcerf: Mercédès's husband and Albert's father; betrayer of both Edmond and Ali Tebelen.
- Mercédès de Morcerf: Edmond's former fiancée, Fernand's wife, and Albert's mother; she recognizes Edmond and prevents the duel from killing her son.
- Albert de Morcerf: Fernand and Mercédès's son; initially the Count's enthusiastic host, then challenger, then repudiator of Fernand's name and fortune.
- Baron Danglars: banker, author of the 1815 letter, and the Count's unwitting instrument in exposing Fernand.
- Villefort: the King's Attorney and another man marked by the Count; the final lit window identifies him as the next active target.
- Beauchamp: Albert's journalist friend, who verifies the Janina accusation and prints the evidence.
- Ali Tebelen, Pasha of Janina: Haydée's father, betrayed and killed after trusting Fernand.

## Causal chain

Fernand and Danglars frame Edmond in 1815, allowing Fernand to marry Mercédès. Fernand later betrays Ali Tebelen for payment and sells Haydée and her mother. Edmond survives imprisonment, becomes the Count, rescues Haydée, preserves Fernand's receipt, and returns to Paris. He induces Danglars to seek Greek evidence; the evidence reaches the newspaper; Albert's demand for verification sends Beauchamp to Janina; the confirmed report provokes the public hearing Fernand's pride demands; Haydée's testimony and receipt make denial impossible; Fernand loses his title; Albert challenges the Count; Mercédès reveals the old conspiracy and saves both men from the duel; Albert renounces Fernand; Mercédès and Albert abandon Fernand and his tainted wealth; Edmond reveals himself; Fernand kills himself. The Count counts this as only the first completed destruction and turns toward Villefort.

## What Fernand's destruction cost

Fernand loses his title, honor, political place, wife, son, fortune as an inheritance, and finally his life. Mercédès loses the house and security she spent twenty-three years building, while Albert gives up his father's name and wealth. Haydée receives public truth, but only through reliving her father's murder and her enslavement before a hostile room. The Count gets the destruction he designed, but it nearly costs Albert's life and his own, forces Mercédès to reopen the loss of Edmond, and exposes that he prolonged the revenge partly for the pleasure of making his enemies and Mercédès watch. His survival after the duel brings no relief. The ending therefore lands as victory contaminated by collateral harm, with the final window warning that the same machinery is about to continue against Villefort.
