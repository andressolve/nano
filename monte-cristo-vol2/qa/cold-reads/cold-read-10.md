# Blind cold read — promoted Pages 1–10 rerun

## Conditions

I read only the ten named `page-XX-promoted-600.png` proofs, once, in page order. I did not consult scripts, prompts, audits, reports, full-size pages, or other project files.

## Verdict

**APPROVED**

There is no mandatory defect. One grammatical error on Page 1 caused a real but momentary reading stumble, and two later beats made me briefly wait for an antecedent or identity. None prevented me from recovering the intended story on the first read.

## 1. What happened

Nine years after a Marseilles shipowner recovered a ship he had lost, a mysteriously wealthy man arrives in Paris, buys and furnishes number thirty on the Champs-Élysées, and chooses it because its window overlooks three strategically important houses. He and Haydée survey those houses: the banker’s, the King’s Attorney’s, and the socially active house that soon proves to be Morcerf’s.

Haydée wants the banker attacked first. She explains that her father was carried through Janina when she was eleven and that the man who sold him is dining nearby. The Count chooses Morcerf first. Their disagreement reveals both that he rescued or repurchased Haydée and promised to restore what was taken from her, and that she is not merely a passive dependent: she has lived with him for four years, understands the revenge plan, and challenges his refusal to name the woman in the Morcerf house.

The Count explains that Morcerf was once a fisherman’s son, could not write his own name, went east to Greece, and returned as a general with his history concealed. He predicts Morcerf will invite him in. An invitation arrives quickly, and the Count attends Morcerf’s crowded house.

Albert de Morcerf, the general’s son, recognizes and welcomes the Count. A silent dark-haired woman notices him across the party. The Count then meets General de Morcerf, needles him about Spain versus Greece, and lets the general understand that he has studied both the house and the man. He also meets Danglars, the banker holding six million of his money, and Villefort, the Attorney-General, whom he needles about power, prosecution, and forgetting. Villefort appears unsettled and asks whether they have met; the Count says no.

Finally, Albert tells the Count that his father avoids speaking about Greece and admits only that it was long ago and that nobody emerged clean. The Count calls that honest. Albert warmly invites him back, while the Count turns away with the information he wanted.

## 2. Who each person seemed to be

- **The Count of Monte Cristo / the dark-haired man:** The central figure; a formerly maritime man, or at minimum someone tied to the Marseilles shipowner in the opening, now immensely wealthy and operating under a title. He is a controlled, observant revenge planner entering Paris society deliberately.
- **Haydée:** The young woman living in his house. He found her and “bought [her] back.” She is the daughter of a man betrayed and sold in Greece by the man now called Morcerf. She has her own claim to justice and presses the Count to act directly.
- **General / Comte de Morcerf / Mondego:** These names clearly resolve as the same man. He began as a fisherman’s son, went to Greece, returned a general, and now hides or minimizes what happened there. He is Albert’s father and the first target the Count chooses.
- **Albert de Morcerf:** Morcerf’s genial, socially confident son. He appears unaware of the danger in welcoming the Count and supplies candid information about his father.
- **Danglars:** The banker, now a baron, who holds six million belonging to the Count. He is one of the planned targets.
- **Villefort:** The King’s Attorney / Attorney-General. He is another planned target and appears to feel an unrecognized threat or familiarity during the handshake.
- **The silent dark-haired woman at the party:** Not named in these ten pages. Her placement, her attention to the Count, and Haydée’s earlier insistence that there is an unnamed woman in Morcerf’s house made her seem likely to be that woman—probably Morcerf’s wife and someone personally important to the Count’s past. Her lack of a name reads as deliberate suspense.

### Identity confusion

I had one brief visual question on Page 7 about whether the silent dark-haired woman might be Haydée because both women have dark hair and appear in deep red clothing. The older styling, ballroom context, and the earlier dialogue about an unnamed woman in Morcerf’s house quickly made her read as a different person. This was momentary, not consequential confusion.

I did not confuse Mondego, the general, and Morcerf after the Count’s history lesson; they read as one identity. Albert, Danglars, and Villefort were all introduced distinctly.

## 3. Why events happened and what I had to supply

The Count bought this particular house so he could observe three targets before making contact. Haydée wants the banker first because the wrong done to her family remains immediate to her; the Count chooses Morcerf because Morcerf’s hidden rise and household are more complicated and more personally charged. He does not force entry into Morcerf’s life. He makes himself conspicuous enough that Morcerf’s curiosity and social ambition produce the invitation, exactly as planned.

At the party, the Count uses polite conversation as reconnaissance and pressure. Mentioning Greece tests Morcerf. His remarks to Danglars establish financial leverage. His wordplay with Villefort tests the prosecutor’s conscience and memory. Albert’s friendliness gives him continuing access and, immediately, an admission about Greece.

The sequence asked me to supply only a few connecting steps:

- I inferred that the opening’s shipowner and the later Count are the same central man, or that the opening is at least deliberately pointing to the Count’s former life. The pages strongly imply this but do not state it outright.
- I inferred that the invitation on Page 6 is the fulfillment of “He’ll ask me,” and that “Four days he lasted” means Morcerf’s resistance or delay lasted only four days.
- I inferred that the silent woman is the unnamed woman in Morcerf’s household and that she belongs to the Count’s buried personal history.
- I inferred that the Count deliberately pretends ignorance about Spain and Greece to provoke Morcerf, rather than genuinely confusing the campaigns.

Those are satisfying suspense inferences, not missing causal steps.

## 4. What changed for the main character

The Count moves from an unseen observer in an empty, strategically chosen house to an invited presence inside the world of all three targets. By Page 10 he has shaken hands with the banker and prosecutor, stood inside Morcerf’s house, made Morcerf reveal sensitivity about Greece, gained Albert’s trust, and obtained an indirect admission that the Greek past was morally dirty. His revenge changes from surveillance and planning into active social penetration. The silent woman’s appearance also shows that entering this house is emotionally dangerous, not merely tactical.

## 5. Exact stops, rereads, and mental corrections

- **Page 1, opening sentence:** I stopped at “walked down to the harbour to found a ship he had lost sitting at anchor.” I reread the clause and mentally corrected **“to found”** to **“and found.”** The intended event was still unmistakable, but this is a visible grammatical error and the clearest first-read stumble in the batch. It did not create lasting story harm, so I am recording it as nonmandatory under this gate’s rule.
- **Page 3, “The man who sold him”:** I paused very briefly to confirm that “him” meant Haydée’s father, not Haydée. The preceding sentence resolved it without a reread.
- **Page 4, “There is one more person in it”:** I held the identity open until Haydée said the Count never names “her” and calls her “the woman.” This felt intentionally withheld, not malformed.
- **Page 6, “Four days he lasted”:** I stopped for the antecedent because the speaker is shown only as a gloved figure in a carriage. Reading it after the invitation card and “He’ll ask me,” I took “he” to mean Morcerf and the line to mean that Morcerf invited the Count after only four days. Page 7 supported that reading. I did not need to go back.
- **Page 7, silent woman:** I briefly checked whether she was Haydée, then concluded she was a different woman in Morcerf’s household. No reread was required.
- **Pages 8–10:** I did not stop, reread, or mentally correct any visible wording. The Greece exchange, the introductions to Danglars and Villefort, and Albert’s account all read cleanly in sequence.

## 6. Boredom

No boredom. Page 1 is the most expository page, but the three roofs give the exposition a concrete threat and purpose. The Haydée confrontation supplies emotional pressure before the social infiltration begins. Pages 7–10 accelerate through introductions that each advance a different line of danger. The repeated polite conversations do not feel repetitive because each exposes a different vulnerability.

## 7. Would I continue?

Yes. I would continue to learn who the silent woman is, exactly what Morcerf did in Greece, why Villefort seems disturbed by the Count, what crimes connect the banker and prosecutor to the Count’s past, and whether Albert’s openness will make him collateral damage. The ten-page endpoint creates active forward pull.
