# Script-Blind Cold Read — Promoted Pages 1–10

## 1. What happened

Nine years after a Marseille shipowner recovered a ship he had lost, an immensely wealthy stranger bought and furnished a house at number thirty on the Champs-Élysées. He chose it because its window gave him a view of three nearby houses belonging to people he is watching: a banker, the King’s Attorney, and a socially ambitious man who entertains constantly.

The stranger studies those houses with Haydée, the young Greek woman who lives with him. He regards the banker as the easiest target and initially talks about beginning there, but Haydée challenges the emotional distance of that choice. She reminds him that the man who betrayed and sold her father—the betrayal that led to her father’s severed head being paraded through Janina—now lives only three streets away. That man began as an illiterate fisherman’s son, went to Greece, returned as a general, and now calls himself the Comte de Morcerf. The stranger decides that Morcerf will come first. Instead of forcing entry into Morcerf’s house, he predicts that Morcerf will invite him.

Four days later, the invitation arrives. At Morcerf’s crowded Thursday gathering, the stranger is openly identified as the Count of Monte Cristo. Morcerf’s son Albert welcomes him, recognizes him as someone he had previously seen walking in Paris, and jokes that his father has been seeking the Count’s company while pretending not to care.

Inside, Monte Cristo meets General Morcerf. The Count says he walked past the house four times because he likes to understand what a man built before shaking his hand, then deliberately steers the conversation toward where Morcerf earned his rank. Morcerf first challenges him and then admits it was Greece. Monte Cristo feigns an innocent geographical mistake while making the general visibly uneasy.

Monte Cristo also meets Danglars, the banker, and reveals that Danglars already controls six million of his money. Then he meets Villefort, the Attorney-General, and needles him with remarks about the power to decide what gets forgotten. When Villefort asks whether they have met, Monte Cristo says no. He has therefore entered the social world of all three targets, gained ready access to Morcerf’s family, and concealed the fact that these introductions are reconnaissance.

Albert later speaks privately and admiringly about his father. He says Morcerf has disclosed the story of Greece only reluctantly and in fragments over twenty years, always dismissing it as old history that nobody came out of clean. Albert takes that reserve as honesty. He invites Monte Cristo to return without the crowd and expects that his mother will like him. Monte Cristo has thus been personally welcomed farther into the Morcerf household by a son who does not suspect his purpose.

## 2. Who these people are and how I distinguished them

- **The stranger / the Count of Monte Cristo:** The tall, severe, dark-haired man in black. Pages 1–6 withhold his title, and Page 7 explicitly names him. His black coat, angular face, controlled manner, and habit of standing apart make him easy to track.
- **Haydée:** The young, dark-haired Greek woman in a red-and-gold robe. She lives with Monte Cristo, was bought back or rescued by him, and lost her father through Morcerf’s betrayal. Her age at that trauma was eleven. Her clothing, younger face, and direct emotional challenge distinguish her clearly from everyone at the party.
- **General / Comte de Morcerf:** The older, broad, moustached military man in a decorated uniform. He is the former fisherman’s son and the man tied to the betrayal in Greece. The uniform and moustache make him immediately distinct.
- **Albert de Morcerf:** Morcerf’s young, curly-haired, sociable son. He welcomes Monte Cristo at the party and later talks to him privately about his father. His youth, curls, bright expression, and light waistcoat distinguish him clearly. His outer clothing changes from the entrance sequence to the later conversation, but his face and manner made the identity continuous on first read.
- **Danglars:** The heavyset, older banker with a round face and ornate waistcoat. He is visually and temperamentally distinct from the military Morcerf and gaunt Villefort.
- **Villefort:** The lean, grey-haired Attorney-General with a long, worried face. He is distinguished by his severe dark clothes, gaunt build, and visibly unsettled reaction to Monte Cristo’s remarks.
- **The dark-haired woman watching from the party on Page 7:** She is presented as significant but remains unnamed in these pages. I could distinguish her from Haydée by her Western evening dress, arranged hair, setting, and older appearance. I did not know whether she was Morcerf’s wife, the unnamed “woman” from Monte Cristo’s earlier list, or both. That uncertainty reads as a deliberate reveal being held for later rather than confusion about the current action.

I did not confuse any of the speaking men. The closest possible identity risk was Albert’s clothing shift by Page 10, but his face, hair, age, and continuous social role carried him without a reread.

## 3. Why things happened, and where I supplied a missing step

- Monte Cristo bought this particular house so he could observe the roofs and routines of people he intends to approach or attack.
- He is targeting Morcerf because Morcerf betrayed and sold Haydée’s father in Greece, enabling the father’s murder and Haydée’s enslavement or sale. Monte Cristo’s promise to give Haydée back everything taken from her turns that revenge into a personal obligation, even though he says he originally meant only money.
- Haydée pushes him to begin with Morcerf because starting with the banker would be emotionally safer and more abstract. Her story forces him to confront the human victim who is already in the room.
- Monte Cristo waits for an invitation because entering as a desired, mysterious guest gives him access without exposing pursuit. His sudden wealth and Paris reputation have made Morcerf curious enough to approach him.
- He questions Morcerf about the house, military rank, Spain, and Greece to test the general’s reactions and signal knowledge without making an accusation.
- He places money with Danglars before meeting him so that the banker already has a financial relationship with him and a reason to engage.
- He needles Villefort about forgotten cases and prosecution to test whether the Attorney-General recognizes him or feels threatened by the past.
- Albert welcomes him because he is fascinated by the mysterious Count, has been primed by his father’s month-long curiosity, and appears genuinely friendly and socially open.

The small steps I supplied were these:

- On Page 6, I inferred that the envelope bearing Morcerf’s title was the predicted invitation and that “Four days he lasted” meant Morcerf resisted his curiosity for four days before sending it. Page 7 immediately confirmed the destination.
- I inferred from Pages 1–2 that the three roofs corresponded to the banker, the Attorney, and Morcerf’s entertaining household, although the third owner’s name was disclosed only later.
- I inferred that Monte Cristo engineered his Parisian mystique partly to make Morcerf invite him. The pages show the reputation, prediction, and result, but do not spell out every social mechanism.
- I inferred that the unnamed woman on Page 7 is probably Morcerf’s wife and likely personally connected to Monte Cristo’s larger plan. Nothing in these ten pages requires that inference to understand the actions already occurring.
- The exact old offenses connecting Monte Cristo himself to Danglars and Villefort are not restated here. Their dialogue makes clear that he knows and is quietly testing them, but I assumed those histories belong to what preceded this volume. That withheld history did not make any present action or choice incomprehensible.

## 4. What changed for the main character

Monte Cristo changes from a hidden observer arranging targets at a window into an accepted guest operating inside the Morcerf household. He begins the sequence talking as if revenge can be selected strategically, with the banker as the simplest first move. Haydée makes the cost personal, and he commits to Morcerf first. By the end, he has shaken hands with Morcerf, Danglars, and Villefort, unsettled two of them, established leverage with the banker, and gained Albert’s trust and invitation. The most important practical change is access; the most important emotional change is that Haydée has forced Morcerf’s betrayal to become the first live act of the plan rather than an item on a list.

## 5. Where I stopped and worked

I did not stop to decode the plot, reread text, or look backward on any page.

The only moment that required an extra beat at normal reading speed was **Page 7**, when the page moves from Albert and Monte Cristo at the entrance to the silent woman watching from across the party. I paused long enough to register that she was a new, intentionally unidentified person of importance and not Haydée. The visual differences resolved that within the panel; I did not need to return to an earlier page.

On **Page 8**, the Spain/Greece exchange demanded attention because Monte Cristo is performing ignorance while Morcerf is guarding his past. The alternating close-ups and the Count’s final apology made the tactic clear on the first pass.

## 6. Where I was bored

Nowhere. Pages 1–5 sustain tension through the contrast between Monte Cristo’s cold planning and Haydée’s lived grievance. Pages 6–10 pay that tension off quickly by putting him inside the target’s party, where ordinary introductions carry double meanings. The gathering never became a parade of names because each introduction changed Monte Cristo’s position or tested a different target.

## 7. Would I keep reading?

Yes. I want to know whether the watching woman recognizes Monte Cristo, what Morcerf did in Greece in full, what Danglars and Villefort did to become targets, and whether Albert’s genuine warmth will complicate the revenge. The ending creates useful dramatic irony: Albert believes he is inviting an interesting new friend deeper into the family while the reader knows Monte Cristo came specifically to dismantle it.

There was no consequential first-read defect in these ten pages. The unnamed woman, the unrepeated pre-volume histories, and Monte Cristo’s feigned mistakes are active mysteries or dramatic irony, not missing information needed to follow the immediate story. No identity, causal link, or scene transition required backtracking or became clear only after repair.
