# Blind cold-read gate — Pages 1–30 rerun after Page 29 replacement

## Scope

I read only the canonical promoted 600 × 900 page proofs for Pages 1–30, once, in order, at reading speed. I did not open any script, outline, prompt, audit, critic report, manifest, ledger, batch report, owner-tolerance note, reference, production document, QA plan, or earlier cold-read report. This report was written from memory after the read.

## 1. What happened

A rich stranger calling himself the Count of Monte Cristo buys a mostly empty Paris house chosen because its window overlooks three targets: a banker, the King's Attorney, and a man who entertains constantly because he wants to be liked. Haydée, the young Greek woman living with him, challenges his plan to begin with the banker. Her father was betrayed and killed at Janina by the man now living nearby as the Comte de Morcerf. The Count knew that man when he was the poor fisherman's son Mondego, before he went to Greece and returned as a general. Haydée pushes the Count to admit that the Morcerf house contains another person he is avoiding—the unnamed woman—and the Count chooses Morcerf first.

The Count predicts Morcerf will invite him. Four days later he enters the Morcerf house and meets Albert, the general's son. He quietly provokes General Morcerf into saying he earned his rank in Greece. He also meets the banker Danglars, who already holds six million of his money, and the Attorney-General Villefort. Both men treat him as a stranger even though his later thoughts show that they helped destroy his old life.

Albert is friendly and honest. He says his father will not speak plainly about Greece and that no one came out of the old affair clean. Mercédès, Morcerf's wife, probes the Count much more personally. She offers him fruit from her garden, asks where he was born, and says he reminds her of someone. He refuses the fruit, claims birth at sea, and stays guarded. Alone afterward, Mercédès remembers Edmond's arrest and reported death, Fernand's inability to hear Edmond's name, and her own decision to marry Fernand. The fruit refusal confirms or deepens her suspicion that the Count is Edmond under another identity.

Haydée asks the Count about Mercédès and catches him lying when he calls her merely the woman who runs the house. The Count later relishes remembering his encounters with Mondego, Villefort, and Danglars, but deliberately refuses to revisit the part involving Mercédès. In the Morcerf house, Fernand notices Mercédès withdrew early; she nearly confronts him and then stops.

Haydée offers to go publicly to the newspapers under her father's name. The Count says she never needs to enter the Morcerf house again, then admits that he personally wants to be present when Morcerf's standing collapses and wants it to take a long time. Haydée realizes he is not primarily acting for her father. She produces a receipt proving that Mondego sold her and her mother in Constantinople. The Count asks to hear everything from the beginning.

Haydée's account moves to Janina in 1822. Her father trusted a French officer with the eastern gate of his lakeside fortress. The officer negotiated under a white flag, returned with money, opened the gate at two in the morning, and enabled the fortress's destruction. Haydée's father was killed and beheaded. The officer returned for Haydée and her mother, sold them in a market, and took payment recorded in a ledger. Her mother died that afternoon, when Haydée was eleven.

Haydée refuses simply to hand the receipt over. She insists that the Count hold it but that she herself read it aloud when the time comes. She has waited sixteen years and will not stand behind a Frenchman while he speaks her father's name for her. The Count accepts this and says he will build her a public room containing three hundred people, with Mondego walking in because he demands to be heard.

The Count then activates that public mechanism. He tells Danglars he is considering putting money behind Morcerf and casually raises the question of money from Greece. Danglars's suspicion leads him to write to Greece in his own name. The answer states that Janina's fortress was surrendered by a paid French officer named Fernand Mondego. Angry that Morcerf has spent twenty-three years speaking of Spain at his table, Danglars sends the information anonymously to *The Impartial*.

The paper prints the accusation. Albert confronts the editor, Beauchamp, and demands a retraction. Beauchamp refuses to retract something he has not checked. He offers a face-saving withdrawal under his own name, but Albert rejects it because that would admit newspaper carelessness rather than establish his father's innocence. He tells Beauchamp to investigate even after Beauchamp warns that he may dislike the answer.

Fernand asks Mercédès to call the story a lie before he carries it to the Chamber. She refuses and reveals that she has never asked what he did before their marriage. Beauchamp spends eleven unhappy days in Janina and finds the surrender register, the clerk who counted the money, men who stood on the walls, and a town giving the same account. An old man asks whether the officer has a son. Beauchamp decides to print.

The Count reads the Janina paper and notes that Beauchamp never wondered who sent him. After eight days of publication, the Chamber of Peers agrees to hear the Comte de Morcerf. Outside the Chamber, the Count explains to Haydée that a petition in her name was anonymously carried to the president by a lawyer who has never heard of him, allowing the president to think the hearing was his own idea. Haydée understands that the Count could have exposed Morcerf months earlier but waited so Morcerf would enter a room of three hundred people and believe he had won. She tells the Count he is enjoying the process more than she is. He sends her inside.

## 2. Who the people are, how I distinguished them, and identity confusion

- **The Count of Monte Cristo:** the tall, dark-haired man dressed almost entirely in black. He is the strategist and is strongly identifiable as the supposedly dead Edmond under a constructed identity. The book does not give a direct “the Count is Edmond” declaration in these pages, but Mercédès's fishing-village probe, the fruit refusal, her memory, and the Count's language about being buried make the equation clear.
- **Haydée:** the young dark-haired Greek woman in red and gold. She is the daughter of the ruler betrayed at Janina, was sold with her mother, and has lived in the Count's house for four years after he bought her back. Her age, costume, speech, and setting kept her fully distinct from Mercédès.
- **Fernand Mondego / General Morcerf / Comte de Morcerf:** the broad, moustached, decorated older soldier. Page 5 explicitly connects the poor fisherman's son, the officer who went east, the returning general, and the Paris title. He is Mercédès's husband, Albert's father, Haydée's betrayer, and the Count's first target.
- **Mercédès de Morcerf:** the older dark-haired woman in the black-burgundy gown. She was deeply connected to Edmond, married Fernand after Edmond's reported death, recognizes the Count, and has deliberately avoided learning the details of Fernand's rise.
- **Albert de Morcerf:** the young, clean-shaven man with curled brown hair, blue coat, and pale waistcoat. He is Fernand and Mercédès's son. He is socially open toward the Count and honest enough to prefer an investigation over a convenient retraction.
- **Danglars:** the heavy, round-faced banker in ornate waistcoats. He holds the Count's money and becomes the Count's unwitting route to Greece and the newspaper.
- **Villefort:** the gaunt, pale, grey-haired Attorney-General. His office, severity, and distressed reaction during the handshake separate him from the other older men.
- **Beauchamp:** the lean, dishevelled blond newspaper editor who often holds spectacles. He is Albert's friend but verifies and prints the truth rather than protecting the Morcerf name.
- **Haydée's father and mother:** her father is the ruler betrayed and beheaded at Janina. Her mother is sold with her and dies that afternoon. Their roles are clear through Haydée's narration and the flashback images even though neither is maintained as a recurring facial identity.

There was no sustained visual identity confusion. On corrected Page 29, I assigned the last panel to the Count immediately: the black sleeves and older hands match him, the dark table and lamp return to his visual world, and “he never once wondered who sent him” can only be the architect observing Beauchamp's investigation. I did not need Page 30 or a backward look to determine the speaker.

## 3. Why events happened, and what I had to infer

The Count is taking revenge on Morcerf, Danglars, and Villefort for the destruction of his earlier life as Edmond. The exact original act committed by each of the latter two is not retold in this run, but the Count's memory of a man who helped bury him and the reactions at the party establish their shared guilt sufficiently for the current movement. He chooses Morcerf first because Haydée calls out the victims and emotions he has reduced to a “house,” and because Morcerf's vanity makes him easy to draw into public judgment.

Mercédès tests the Count because she recognizes Edmond beneath the new identity. He can enjoy the guilty men's failure to recognize him, but not his encounter with her, because she belongs to the life that was taken rather than simply to the revenge target list.

Haydée supplies the Janina account because the crime belongs to her directly and she refuses to be used as silent proof. The Count's plan changes enough to give her a public voice. He then exploits character rather than inventing evidence: Danglars's financial suspicion obtains the letter; Danglars's humiliation sends it to a newspaper; Albert's loyalty forces Beauchamp to verify it; Beauchamp's professional integrity sustains publication; Morcerf's vanity makes him demand a hearing; and the anonymous petition places Haydée inside it.

The steps I had to supply were:

1. The Count is Edmond. The connection is strongly supported but implicit.
2. Danglars and Villefort participated in Edmond's destruction. Their precise historical acts are outside these pages.
3. The recovered ship in the Page 1 Marseille time marker belongs to prior story context. Its connection is not explained through Page 30, but it is separable from the active plot.
4. “The one part” the Count refuses to revisit is Mercédès.
5. “When it goes” means Morcerf's rank, wealth, household, and public standing.
6. The faceless French officer in the Janina flashback is Mondego/Morcerf.
7. The Count expected the accusation to make Albert demand verification and send Beauchamp to Janina. Page 29 confirms the Count regards Beauchamp as successfully “sent,” while Page 30 makes the broader trap explicit.

None of these required returning to an earlier page. Items 1–2 and the opening ship reference assume or point toward prior-volume knowledge, while Items 4–7 are resolved prospectively by the sequence.

## 4. What changed for the main character

The Count begins as an aloof planner at a window, describing people as roofs, roles, and houses. Haydée forces him to confront the human material he has abstracted. At Morcerf's party he gains proximity to all three enemies and enjoys their ignorance, but Mercédès's recognition exposes an emotional limit: she is the one encounter he cannot turn into appetite.

He then says plainly that the revenge is his and that he wants it to be slow. Haydée's receipt and insistence also change the execution. He stops treating her merely as protected evidence, agrees that she will speak her own words, and builds the public “room” around her testimony.

By Page 30, the Count has transformed private reconnaissance into a public trap in which every participant believes he is acting from his own motive. That makes his intelligence and control impressive, but it also makes him morally more alarming. His admission that he delayed so Morcerf could think he had won, followed by Haydée's observation that he is enjoying it more than she is, reveals revenge as pleasure rather than simple justice.

## 5. Where I stopped or had to work, and why

Under the instructed standard, these quick or eventually resolved moments still count as defects:

- **Page 1:** The opening reference to a Marseille shipowner finding a lost ship functions as a time marker but is not connected to the active story by Page 30. I assigned it to prior-volume context and continued; the unresolved reference did not obscure the stranger buying the Paris house.
- **Page 3:** “The man who sold him” first reads idiomatically as the man who sold out or betrayed Haydée's father. Later pages reveal that the same man also literally sold Haydée and her mother. The double use is coherent, but its exact scope is not yet knowable on Page 3.
- **Page 6:** “Four days he lasted” required a quick referent check. The invitation and the preceding “He'll ask me” make “he” Morcerf and “lasted” the four days before he gave in and invited the Count.
- **Page 13:** “Mercédès de Morcerf had known about her husband since she was nineteen years old” briefly makes “known about” sound like acquaintance rather than guilty knowledge. The following sentences supply the intended sense.
- **Page 15:** “The four seconds Villefort had held on” made me supply the handshake as the omitted object. I also mentally heard quotation marks around “old friends” in the line about Danglars. The compressed interior prose remained understandable, but it required grammatical completion.
- **Page 17:** “When it goes” lacks a literal noun. I supplied Morcerf's status, household, fortune, and reputation; the later newspaper and Chamber mechanism confirms that broad reading.
- **Page 24:** “Putting money behind Morcerf” took a short interpretive beat before reading as financing or extending credit, used as bait to make Danglars examine Morcerf's wealth.
- **Page 26:** The opening crops the Count to his lower face, hands, and newspaper, while “he” in “he has not had his breakfast yet” is identified visually only when Albert appears below. Clothing, voice, and the same-page payoff made both referents clear, but the panel asks for a brief prospective inference.

I did not stop, reread, or look ahead on corrected Page 29. The final speaker and the meaning of “sent him” were immediate. I also did not lose a speaker elsewhere, reread a full page, or mentally repair illegible lettering.

## 6. Where I was bored

Nowhere. The opening reconnaissance quickly becomes social infiltration; Mercédès changes the emotional stakes; Haydée changes the moral ownership of the revenge; and the newspaper investigation gives the later pages strong procedural momentum. The quiet Janina images and the investigative compression on Page 29 each deliver consequential information rather than pausing the story.

## 7. Whether I would keep reading

Yes. Page 30 ends at the threshold of the public hearing with the accusation verified, Morcerf committed to defend himself, Haydée ready to speak, and the Count's enjoyment exposed. I want to see whether Haydée is admitted and believed, how Fernand answers, what Albert and Mercédès do with the truth, and whether the Count can still distinguish justice from appetite once the consequences become personal.
