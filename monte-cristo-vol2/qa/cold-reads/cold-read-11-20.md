# Blind cold read — Pages 11–20

## Scope and verdict

I read only the ten promoted 600px proofs for Pages 11–20, once, in page order. I did not consult scripts, prompts, audits, prior reports, full-size art, or other project files.

**APPROVED**

There is no mandatory first-read defect. The sequence asks the reader to make several inferences, but each is either supported in the run or is clearly being held for the continuing reveal. I could transcribe and attribute the dialogue comfortably at proof size.

## 1. What happened

- At a formal dinner, a woman offers the black-clad Count fruit. He refuses and says he never eats fruit. She probes: the fruit came from her garden, and she asks about his origin and family. He gives guarded answers about dining late, being born at sea, and his father moving often.
- The woman mentions that her son likes him. Her questions become unmistakably personal when she says he reminds her of someone. He says he hopes that person was a friend; she says he was.
- The narration identifies her as Mercédès de Morcerf. She has lived for years with the knowledge that something was wrong around Edmond's arrest and supposed death: Fernand could not bear Edmond's name, yet she married Fernand and built a life with him. The Count's refusal to eat fruit confirms or sharply strengthens her suspicion that this man is connected to the lost Edmond.
- Back at the Count's house, Haydée questions him about the dinner, especially the wife. He calls the wife merely the woman who runs the house. Haydée recognizes that answer as a lie, and he ends the conversation.
- Alone, the Count deliberately relives the evening: contact with Mondego, Villefort's lingering hold, and Danglars calling him an old friend. He savors being recognized by men implicated in his figurative burial, but deliberately refuses to revisit one part of the evening—the encounter with Mercédès.
- At the Morcerf house, Mercédès and Fernand discuss whether the Count will return. Fernand senses that she disliked the guest and notices that she withdrew early. She almost says something consequential to Fernand, then stops and asks for the lamps to be put out.
- Haydée confronts the sleepless Count. She offers to expose herself publicly under her father's name if his plan is costing him. He says she never needs to enter that house again, then admits that he wants to be present when the man's standing collapses and wants the process to take a long time. Haydée realizes he is not primarily doing this for her father; he says no.
- Haydée produces a receipt she has kept for four years. It records that the same man sold her and her mother in Constantinople and signed for the money. The Count asks her to tell the whole story from the beginning.
- The story moves to Janina in 1822. Haydée's father held a lakeside fortress and trusted a French officer with the eastern gate. The officer went out under a white flag, returned with money after making terms, and opened the gate at two in the morning. The fortress burned, Haydée's father was killed and beheaded, and the officer returned for Haydée and her mother—an ominous return that connects directly to the receipt and sale already revealed.

## 2. Who each person seems to be, and identity confusion

- **The black-clad man / the Count:** a rich, disciplined man who is almost certainly the supposedly dead Edmond under a new identity. Mercédès's fishing-village probe, his fruit refusal, and the burial language make that inference strong without requiring an explicit declaration in these pages.
- **Mercédès de Morcerf:** Fernand's wife, mother of the son who likes the Count, and someone who was once deeply attached to Edmond. She suspects the Count's true identity and also appears to have long suspected Fernand's role in Edmond's disappearance.
- **Fernand / Morcerf / Mondego:** these appear to be three names or forms of address for the same man: Mercédès's husband, the decorated older man in her house, one of the Count's targets, and likely the French officer in the Janina account. This equivalence is not stated outright inside these ten pages. It caused brief name-matching work, but the domestic setting, Mercédès's use of “Fernand,” the Count's target, and Haydée's evidence make the intended identity readable.
- **Haydée:** the young woman in red, under the Count's protection for four years. She is the daughter of the Janina ruler, an eyewitness and victim of the betrayal, and a potential public witness against the target. She seems to care about the Count while retaining strong agency of her own.
- **Mercédès and Fernand's son:** unseen here. He likes the Count and seems to be an emotional complication, but he is not named in this run.
- **Villefort and Danglars:** unseen men from the dinner whom the Count associates with his former destruction. Their exact offices or individual acts are not explained here, but their role as old enemies is sufficient for this sequence.
- **The French officer in Janina:** shown only from behind and not named in the flashback. I read him as the seller named by Haydée and, by extension, as Fernand/Mondego. The sequence appears to be in the act of confirming that identification rather than accidentally obscuring it.

No two visible characters were visually confusable. Mercédès and Haydée remained distinct across setting and costume, and the Count and Fernand were immediately separable.

## 3. Why events happened, and steps I had to supply

Mercédès keeps pressing the Count because she suspects he is Edmond and is testing details that might penetrate his disguise. The Count attended the dinner to observe the men he holds responsible for destroying his earlier life. He can relish their unknowing contact with him, but Mercédès is emotionally different, so he avoids replaying that part.

Haydée asks about the wife because she notices that Mercédès affected him and that he is lying about it. She offers her own name and face because she believes the plan concerns her father's betrayal and wants to bear the cost herself. The Count resists not because the evidence is weak, but because he wants a slow, personal reckoning. Her receipt then makes her family's case independent and concrete, forcing him to hear the story on her terms.

The supplied steps were:

1. The Count is Edmond.
2. Fernand, Morcerf, and Mondego are the same man.
3. “The one part” the Count refuses to revisit is Mercédès.
4. “When it goes” means the collapse of Fernand's house, rank, wealth, or public standing, rather than a literal object.
5. The French officer in Janina is the same man who later sold Haydée and her mother, and likely Fernand/Mondego.

All five inferences were supported strongly enough to keep moving. None required a corrective reread of the sequence.

## 4. What changed for the main character

The Count begins as controlled and nearly unreadable, allowing Mercédès to probe while giving away very little. The private narration then reveals that his control is selective: he enjoys the proximity of his enemies, but Mercédès still reaches a part of him he will not examine. With Haydée, he is forced to say plainly that the revenge is his own, not an altruistic act for her father. Finally, the receipt and her testimony widen the reckoning beyond his private injury. By Page 20 he is no longer only rehearsing what was done to him; he is listening to what the same target did to Haydée and her family.

## 5. Stops, rereads, and mental corrections

- **Page 13:** I paused at “had known about her husband since she was nineteen years old.” “Known about” signals guilty knowledge rather than simple acquaintance, and the following sentences supplied what she knew. I did not need to reread the panel.
- **Page 15:** I briefly supplied the missing object in “The four seconds Villefort had held on,” reading it as holding on during a handshake or greeting. In “Danglars saying old friends to a man he had helped bury,” I mentally put quotation marks around “old friends.” Both read as compressed interior narration, not broken prose.
- **Page 16:** I automatically voiced “Did he.” as a very flat question—effectively “Did he?”—because it answers “He'll come again.” The printed period can also be read as deliberate emotional flatness, so it did not impede the scene.
- **Page 17:** I stopped briefly on “when it goes” to decide what “it” meant. I supplied Fernand's house/status/fortune/reputation. Haydée's next line—“Then you are not doing this for my father”—confirmed the emotional meaning even though the noun remains deliberately broad.
- **Page 20:** I paused on the final “He did.” The first beat of “came back for us” can suggest rescue; the already-seen sale receipt immediately turns it into threat and betrayal. That reversal was clear and effective.

I did not reread any full page, lose a speaker, or mentally repair garbled lettering. No visible word was illegible at 600px.

## 6. Boredom

None. Pages 11–13 use restrained conversation as an identity test; Pages 14–18 steadily convert subtext into motive and evidence; Pages 19–20 open the historical betrayal at exactly the point the present-day conflict needs it. The silent/low-text images provide tension rather than dead space.

## 7. Would I continue?

Yes. Page 20 ends at the right point: I understand the betrayal's outline and the officer's likely identity, but I want Haydée's full account of what happened after he returned, how the sale occurred, and what the Count will do with proof that now belongs to both of them.

**Final verdict: APPROVED**
