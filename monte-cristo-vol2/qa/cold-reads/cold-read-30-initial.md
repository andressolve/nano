# Blind cold-read gate — canonical Pages 1–30

## Scope

I read only the canonical promoted 600 × 900 page proofs for Pages 1–30, once, in page order, at reading speed. I did not open a script, outline, prompt, audit, critic report, manifest, ledger, reference plan, production document, or the Page 30 QA plan. This report is from memory after the uninterrupted read.

## What happened

A fabulously wealthy stranger calling himself the Count of Monte Cristo buys a nearly empty Paris house chosen because its window overlooks the homes of three targets: a banker, the King's Attorney, and a socially hungry man who entertains. Haydée, the young Greek woman living with him, pushes him to begin with the Comte de Morcerf rather than the banker. She knows Morcerf as Mondego, the man who betrayed her father in Janina and later sold her and her mother. The Count knew Mondego before he became a general and wants access to his house. He predicts Morcerf will invite him; four days later, he does.

At Morcerf's party, the Count meets the general's son Albert, then General Morcerf himself. He quietly needles the general about the source of his rank and forces him to say that he earned it in Greece. He also meets Danglars, the banker who already holds six million of the Count's money, and Villefort, the Attorney-General. Both behave as strangers even though the Count's private narration later makes clear that they helped destroy his former life. Albert, friendly and fundamentally honest, admits that his father will not talk plainly about Greece.

Mercédès, Morcerf's wife, tests the Count more personally. She offers him fruit from her own garden, asks where he was born, and tells him he reminds her of someone. He refuses fruit, claims birth at sea, and remains guarded. In private narration, Mercédès remembers Edmond's arrest and supposed death, Fernand's inability to hear Edmond's name, and her decision to marry Fernand anyway. The Count's refusal of her fruit confirms or intensifies her recognition that he is the lost Edmond under another identity.

Back home, Haydée asks about the wife and catches the Count lying when he reduces Mercédès to merely the woman who runs the house. Alone, the Count relishes his encounters with Mondego, Villefort, and Danglars, but deliberately refuses to replay the part involving Mercédès. In the Morcerf house, Fernand notices that Mercédès withdrew early. She nearly confronts him, then stops.

Haydée offers to expose Morcerf publicly under her father's name. The Count says she need never enter the house again, but then admits that he personally wants to be present when Morcerf's standing collapses and wants the collapse to take a long time. Haydée understands that his campaign is not primarily for her father. She produces a receipt showing that Mondego sold her and her mother in Constantinople. The Count asks for the full story.

In the Janina account, Haydée's father trusted a French officer with the eastern gate of his lakeside fortress. The officer negotiated under a white flag, returned with money, opened the gate at two in the morning, and enabled the fortress's destruction. Haydée's father was killed and beheaded. The officer returned for Haydée and her mother, took them to market, and sold them. Her mother died that afternoon, when Haydée was eleven.

Haydée refuses to surrender the receipt as though she were only evidence to be handled by the Count. She insists that he hold it but that she herself read it aloud in the eventual room. The Count accepts this and describes a public hearing before three hundred people, engineered so that Mondego will demand admission and defend himself.

The Count visits Danglars and casually suggests putting money behind Morcerf. By mentioning that Morcerf's wealth came from Greece, he activates the banker's suspicion. Danglars writes to Greece in his own name. The reply says that the fortress at Janina was sold by a paid French officer named Fernand Mondego. Furious that Morcerf has spoken of Spain at his table for twenty-three years, Danglars anonymously sends the information to the newspaper *The Impartial*.

Albert reads the accusation before breakfast and confronts the editor, Beauchamp. Beauchamp refuses to retract an assertion he has not yet checked. Albert refuses Beauchamp's offered face-saving withdrawal because it would admit only newspaper carelessness rather than prove his father innocent. He tells Beauchamp to investigate even after being warned that he may dislike the result.

Fernand asks Mercédès to call the charge a lie before he carries it to the Chamber. She refuses and reveals that she has deliberately never asked him what he did before their marriage. Beauchamp spends eleven unhappy days in Janina. He finds the surrender register, the clerk who counted the money, and a town full of consistent witnesses. He decides to print the truth. The Count privately notes that Beauchamp never wondered who had set him on the trail.

After eight days of publication, the Chamber of Peers agrees to hear Morcerf. The Count has also arranged a petition in Haydée's name, carried by a lawyer who does not know him, so that the president believes the hearing is his own idea. In the carriage outside, Haydée realizes that the Count could have released the accusation months earlier but delayed until he could place Morcerf in front of three hundred peers and let him believe he had won first. She tells the Count he is enjoying the process more than she is. He sends her inside for the hearing.

## Who the people are, how I distinguished them, and identity confusion

- **The Count of Monte Cristo:** the tall, dark-haired man in severe black. He is the central planner and, by the Mercédès material and references to being figuratively buried, almost certainly Edmond under a constructed identity. The sequence never gives a single declarative “the Count is Edmond” line, but the fruit, fishing-village history, Mercédès's recognition, and his past with the three targets establish it without a backward search.
- **Haydée:** the young dark-haired Greek woman in red and gold. She was eleven when her father was killed, was sold with her mother, and has lived in the Count's house for four years after he bought her back. Her youth, red costume, directness, and position beside the Count kept her distinct from Mercédès.
- **Fernand Mondego / General Morcerf / Comte de Morcerf:** the broad, moustached, decorated older soldier. Page 5 explicitly bridges the poor fisherman's son Mondego, the general returned from Greece, and the Paris title Morcerf. He is Mercédès's husband, Albert's father, the Janina officer, and the Count's first public target.
- **Mercédès de Morcerf:** the older dark-haired woman in the black-burgundy gown. She was close to Edmond, married Fernand after Edmond's reported death, and has long avoided asking how Fernand obtained his life. Her age, formal dress, guarded stillness, and Morcerf-house setting made her unmistakable.
- **Albert de Morcerf:** the young, clean-shaven, curly brown-haired man in the blue coat and pale waistcoat. He is Fernand and Mercédès's son, socially open toward the Count and morally serious enough to demand verification rather than a convenient retraction.
- **Danglars:** the heavy, round-faced banker in ornate waistcoats. He holds six million for the Count and becomes the unwitting financial and newspaper lever against Morcerf.
- **Villefort:** the gaunt, pale, grey-haired Attorney-General. His office, severe black clothing, and haunted reaction to the Count's handshake separate him from the other older men.
- **Beauchamp:** the lean, dishevelled blond newspaper editor, often holding spectacles. He is Albert's friend but chooses verification and publication over protection.
- **Haydée's father and mother:** her father is the ruler betrayed at Janina; her mother is the other captive sold with her and dies on the market day. They are primarily understood through Haydée's words and flashback silhouettes rather than sustained facial identities.

There was no sustained visual identity confusion. The one transitional identity that required work was the unseen reader in the final panel of Page 29: black sleeves, the familiar dark room, and the thought about who “sent” Beauchamp made me assign the hands and voice to the Count. Page 30's continued explanation agreed with that reading, but because the face was withheld, this counts as an eventually-confirmed first-read defect rather than no defect.

## Why events happened, and what I had to infer

The Count is seeking revenge against Morcerf, Danglars, and Villefort for the destruction of his life as Edmond. He chooses Morcerf first partly because Haydée forces him to confront the living victims inside that “house,” and partly because the Morcerf household contains Mercédès, his unresolved emotional vulnerability. He enters through Albert because Morcerf's curiosity and need for social status make an invitation predictable.

At the party, the Count gathers admissions and watches whether the old men recognize him. Mercédès tests him because she does recognize patterns from Edmond: his origin, his old life, and the refusal of fruit. Her recognition affects him more than the enemies' failure to recognize him, which is why he can savor the handshakes but not replay her conversation.

Haydée supplies the Janina case because she wants agency in a reckoning that directly belongs to her. The Count initially treats the revenge as his own slow design; her receipt and insistence make him create a place where she can speak publicly herself.

The public plan works by exploiting character rather than fabricating proof. Danglars's suspicion sends a query to Greece. His humiliation sends the answer to a newspaper. Albert's loyalty sends Beauchamp to verify it. Beauchamp's professional pride and the consistent evidence sustain publication. Morcerf's vanity will make him demand a public hearing instead of hiding. The Count's anonymous petition ensures Haydée has entry and standing without exposing his hand.

Steps I had to supply were:

1. **The Count is Edmond.** Strongly supported but not stated as a direct equation in these pages.
2. **The Count's three targets caused Edmond's earlier destruction.** The “helped bury” language and their physical reactions establish guilt, but the specific original acts of Danglars and Villefort are not retold here.
3. **The Page 1 Marseille shipowner and recovered ship are a prior-story time marker.** Their identity and connection to the present plot remain unexplained through Page 30. I could proceed without that knowledge, but the opening sentence briefly promises a connection this run does not supply.
4. **“The one part” the Count refuses to revisit is Mercédès.** The preceding pages make this clear.
5. **“When it goes” means Morcerf's house, rank, wealth, and public standing.** The noun remains broad until the newspaper and Chamber plot make the mechanism concrete.
6. **The faceless French officer at Janina is Mondego/Morcerf.** The receipt, Page 5 history, and later printed name connect him.
7. **The Count expected Albert to force verification.** Page 29 and Page 30 reveal the breadth of the trap, but the Count never narrates that exact prediction step. The causal chain still reads as intentional because every stage turns the next person's pride or loyalty into propulsion.

## What changed for the main character

The Count begins as an aloof strategist looking down from a window and reducing people to targets and houses. Haydée immediately challenges that abstraction. At Morcerf's party he achieves physical proximity to all three enemies and enjoys their ignorance, but Mercédès's recognition exposes an emotional limit: she is the one part of the evening he cannot make into appetite.

He then admits to Haydée that the campaign is personal and that he wants prolonged satisfaction, not merely justice for her father. At the same time, her refusal to be used silently changes his plan. He accepts that she will speak the receipt herself and builds the “room” around her testimony.

By Page 30, he has moved from private reconnaissance to a fully public mechanism in which Morcerf, Danglars, Albert, Beauchamp, and the Chamber all believe they are acting from their own motives. That demonstrates his power but also darkens him. His admission that he delayed so Morcerf could think he had won, followed by Haydée's observation that he is enjoying it more than she is, exposes revenge as pleasure rather than duty. The main character is therefore both more emotionally legible and more morally alarming at Page 30 than at Page 1.

## Where I stopped or had to work, and why

These are defects under the instructed standard even where they resolved quickly:

- **Page 1:** I stopped in the opening sentence at the Marseille shipowner finding a lost ship at anchor. I expected the shipowner or ship to connect to the stranger, but no connection is supplied through Page 30. I treated it as a prior-volume time marker and moved on.
- **Page 6:** “Four days he lasted” required a quick referent check. The invitation card and Page 5's “He'll ask me” made “he” Morcerf and “lasted” the four days before giving in and inviting the Count.
- **Page 7:** In Albert's introduction, “Albert de Morcerf. The general's son. He's been asking after you...” made me briefly test whether “he” meant Albert or the general. I settled on the general because Albert is speaking and has just named his father. The scene meaning survived, but the pronoun cost a beat.
- **Page 13:** “Mercédès de Morcerf had known about her husband since she was nineteen years old” briefly made “known about” sound like acquaintance rather than guilty knowledge. The following sentences corrected the sense without a full-page reread.
- **Page 15:** “The four seconds Villefort had held on” made me supply the handshake as the missing object. I also mentally placed quotation marks around “old friends” in “Danglars saying old friends to a man he had helped bury.” The compressed interior prose remained understandable but required grammatical repair.
- **Page 17:** I stopped on “when it goes” because “it” has no literal noun. I supplied Morcerf's social house—rank, wealth, family standing, and reputation—and the later public plan confirmed that reading.
- **Page 24:** “Putting money behind Morcerf” took a short interpretive beat: I read it as financing or extending credit to him, used as bait to make Danglars examine the source of Morcerf's wealth.
- **Page 29:** The final reader is shown only by hands. I identified him as the Count from clothing, setting, and the thought “he never once wondered who sent him.” Page 30's continuation supported that assignment. Because identification depended partly on the next page, this is an eventually-clear defect.

I did not stop because of illegible lettering, lose the order of a conversation, or have to reread a whole page. The Page 7 pronoun and Page 29 faceless transition were the only identity/attribution work; neither changed my understanding of the event chain.

## Where I was bored

Nowhere. The quiet house reconnaissance turns into social infiltration, the Mercédès exchange changes the emotional stakes, Haydée's testimony changes the moral ownership of the revenge, and the newspaper investigation gives the second half a strong procedural drive. Pages 19 and 21 are visually quieter, but each lands a major historical beat rather than pausing the story. The eleven-day verification on Page 29 is compressed enough to feel consequential rather than repetitive.

## Whether I would keep reading

Yes. Page 30 ends at a genuine threshold: the public accusation is verified, Morcerf has demanded a hearing, Haydée has secured the right to speak for herself, and the Count's enjoyment has become unsettlingly explicit. I want to see whether Haydée is admitted and believed, how Fernand answers, what Albert and Mercédès do with the truth, and whether the Count can still distinguish justice from appetite once the consequences become personal.

## Mandatory comprehension defects and verdict

The first-read friction above is real and has been counted as defect rather than excused because it eventually resolved. None, however, caused mandatory story, identity, attribution, or cause-and-effect harm across the thirty-page run. The opening ship reference remains unexplained, but it is separable from the active plot. The Page 7 pronoun and Page 29 faceless transition cost brief beats without changing who acted or why.
