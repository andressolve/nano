# Cold Read — Promoted Pages 1–10

## 1. What happened

Nine years after a Marseille shipowner recovered a ship he had lost, an immensely wealthy stranger bought a Paris house at number thirty, Champs-Élysées. He chose it because its windows overlook three relevant houses: those of a banker, the King's Attorney, and an entertainer. The stranger and Haydée discuss which enemy he should approach first. He initially chooses the banker, but Haydée pushes him toward the Morcerf household: the man who betrayed her father in Greece is nearby, and the stranger is also avoiding an unnamed woman in that house.

The stranger explains that he knew Morcerf when Morcerf was an illiterate fisherman's son. Morcerf went to Greece and returned a general, and Paris now accepts him as the Comte de Morcerf without asking how he rose. The stranger decides to begin with Morcerf and predicts that Morcerf will invite him in. Four days later, the invitation arrives.

At the Morcerf gathering, Albert de Morcerf recognizes the visitor as the Count of Monte Cristo and introduces him to the household. Monte Cristo needles General Morcerf about having earned his rank in Greece, pretending to confuse Greece with Spain. He then meets Baron Danglars, the banker who already holds six million of his money, and Villefort, the Attorney-General. Both encounters play as meetings with men from Monte Cristo's past who do not recognize him. Finally, Albert says his father has avoided giving a full account of Greece for twenty years, defends him as an honest man, and warmly invites Monte Cristo back to meet his mother.

## 2. Who the people are, distinctions, and confusion

- The Count of Monte Cristo is the stern, dark-haired man dressed in black. Page 7 supplies his title; the framing and the later failures of recognition make him the transformed Marseille shipowner from Page 1.
- Haydée is the young, dark-haired woman in the red patterned dress. She is Greek, her father was killed, and Monte Cristo found and bought her back. She is his intimate confidante and has her own direct claim against Morcerf.
- General/Comte de Morcerf is the older, moustached man in decorated military uniform. Monte Cristo knew him as Mondego, a fisherman's son; Haydée's account identifies him as the man who sold her father.
- Albert de Morcerf is the cheerful young man with curled brown hair and a pale waistcoat. He is the general's son and is openly fascinated by Monte Cristo.
- Baron Danglars is the stout, ruddy banker in the ornate waistcoat.
- Villefort is the thin, pale, grey-haired Attorney-General dressed in black.
- The dark-haired woman in burgundy at the bottom of Page 7 appears to be Albert's mother and the unnamed “woman” Monte Cristo cannot bring himself to name.

There was no visual identity confusion. The delayed naming of Monte Cristo and the withholding of Albert's mother's name felt intentional. I could distinguish her from Haydée immediately, although her exact history with Monte Cristo remains unstated.

## 3. Why things happened and steps I supplied

Monte Cristo bought the house for strategic proximity and surveillance. He is arranging revenge against the people connected to the destruction of his former life. Haydée redirects him to Morcerf because Morcerf's Greek past includes the betrayal of her father, and because she sees that Monte Cristo's reluctance is really about the woman inside Morcerf's house. Monte Cristo walks past Morcerf's house repeatedly to provoke curiosity and gain an invitation without asking for one. Depositing six million with Danglars gives him immediate importance and access. His apparently polite mistakes about Greece, forgotten acts, and French meaning are deliberate tests and threats disguised as social conversation. Albert's admiration gives Monte Cristo an innocent route deeper into the family.

The sequence required me to supply that the shipowner and Monte Cristo are the same man, that his enemies knew him under an earlier identity, and that Albert's mother is the unnamed woman with whom he has painful personal history. I also supplied that Danglars and Villefort harmed his former self, although these ten pages do not yet state their particular offenses. Those inferences were strongly signalled by the juxtaposition, the targets' failure to recognize him, and Monte Cristo's controlled verbal attacks.

## 4. What changed for the main character

Monte Cristo moves from detached observation and an abstract choice of targets to entering the Morcerf house and making direct contact with all three principal men. Haydée forces him to admit, through his reaction rather than a confession, that this is not only a calculated campaign: the Morcerf household contains a woman he fears facing. By Page 10 he has successfully penetrated the family through Albert, but the invitation to meet Albert's mother exposes the emotional cost of doing so.

## 5. Where I stopped or had to work

I stopped on the first sentence of Page 1 at “to found a ship.” I had to replace “found” with “find” mentally before the sentence parsed.

I briefly worked on Page 4 to connect “one more person,” “the woman,” and the Morcerf house, but the emotional avoidance was legible in the exchange and Page 7's silent sightline supported it. On Page 9 I also made the intended inference that Danglars and Villefort had known Monte Cristo's former identity. Neither required rereading.

## 6. Where I was bored

Nowhere. The quiet opening quickly became a target map, Haydée's challenge added personal pressure, and the party sequence paid that setup off through increasingly dangerous conversations.

## 7. Would I keep reading?

Yes. I want to learn exactly what Morcerf did in Greece, why Danglars and Villefort are targets, who Albert's mother is to Monte Cristo, and whether Albert's trust will survive the revenge.

## Mandatory defect

1. **Page 1:** The opening sentence says the shipowner walked to the harbour “to found a ship he had lost.” The required infinitive is “to find.” Because this occurs in the first line and produces a grammatically and semantically wrong phrase, it causes an immediate first-read stop and mental correction.
