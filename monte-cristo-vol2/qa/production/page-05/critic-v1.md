# Page 5 independent critic — v1

## Step 1 — blind transcription from the 600 × 900 proof

Before opening the script, source candidate, prompt, promoted predecessor, references, or builder audit, I read the following from the desktop proof alone, in reading order:

1. “When I found you and bought you back, I told you I would give you everything he took. I meant the money.”
2. “I know what you meant.”
3. “I knew him when he mended nets. He could not write his own name; another man wrote it for him.”
4. “He went east to Greece a fisherman’s son, and came home a general.”
5. “Paris calls him the Comte de Morcerf now, and nobody has ever asked him what he did there.”
6. “You are right, and I am going to enjoy being told so more than I should. Morcerf first.”
7. “How will you get into his house?”
8. “I won’t. He’ll ask me.”

After fixing that transcription, I compared it with Page 5 of the script. All eight strings are exact. Nothing is unreadable, misread, missing, duplicated, replaced, or added. The desktop proof is comfortable to read, so the complete lettering-size and reader-comfort gate passes.

## Mandatory defect

1. **Page architecture — there is no unmistakable single dominant panel.** The page reads as three near-equal horizontal stages: the combined top tier, the central Count portrait, and the closing two-shot. The intended panel 3 is only marginally larger than panel 4, so the confrontation and the closing exchange compete for dominance. This is a substantial hierarchy change, not a de minimis one-to-three-point construction variance.

- **No glass and no decanter:** Pass. Neither object appears anywhere on the page.
- **Eight strings and reader comfort:** Pass. The blind desktop transcription captured all eight strings exactly and comfortably. The Count owns the first, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, and eighth; Haydée owns the second and seventh.

## Reader response

The page reads clearly. The defeated flat hand opens in silence, Haydée’s return gives the Count permission to retarget his appetite, and the history of Mondego descends cleanly through the central exchange. The final “He’ll ask me” is crisp and makes the promised invitation an effective page turn. The problem is not comprehension but hierarchy: the central target-selection beat and the bottom invitation exchange occupy comparable visual stages, so the declared dominant turn is not singular. The page needs one unmistakable field to carry its centre of gravity.
