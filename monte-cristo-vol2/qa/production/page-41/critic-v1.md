## Blind read
At the other end of Paris that night, a young man sits in his room cleaning a disassembled pistol when his mother enters in black. She takes control of the encounter, tells him to put the weapon down, and reveals that Edmond Dantès was arrested at nineteen before he could marry her, that Danglars wrote the accusing letter, and that the young man's father carried it to the post. The son moves from occupied silence to confronting the fact that she knew; the mother confirms that she always did and ends by telling him that the man he will shoot at eight o'clock is Edmond. The mother owns the page, and the change is the son's discovery of both his father's part in Edmond's ruin and Edmond's identity as his appointed opponent.

## Findings

### Finding C1
- Observation: Although every string is legible and textually exact, multiple balloon tails visibly reverse or confuse ownership. In the opening, "You've been out" points toward the mother while "Yes" points toward the son. The tails for "There was a boy in Marseille..." and "Danglars wrote the letter..." point toward the son, and "Where is he now?" points left toward the mother rather than the son.
- Material reader harm: The page's central event is a mother disclosing her own buried history to her son, but the visual grammar repeatedly assigns her first-person account to him and also reverses the opening exchange and final question. Readers must override explicit tail attribution using context to reconstruct who is speaking, so mother and son's lines cannot be assigned confidently from the page itself.
- Redraw justification: The ownership failures affect the opening, two essential lines of the 1815 revelation, and the final exchange; they materially destabilize the dominant mother-son dramatic relationship and exact attribution across the complete page, making replacement risk warranted.
