# Intent-first page intents — Pages 33–49

These are reader-facing goals, not image-generation specifications. They are
shown to both builder and critic. Exact text and staging remain in the protected
script and in the role-specific packets.

## PAGE 33

Haydée calmly identifies Fernand, supplies the facts of his betrayal, and lets
his own signed sale finish him. The whole Chamber lies between accuser and
accused. Above Fernand, the Count leans out of the gallery with unmistakable
appetite: Haydée speaks, but he built the killing ground.

The page is about testimony becoming public fact. It is not a test of whether a
tiny wax seal or handwriting can be inspected; the President reads the evidence
aloud.

## PAGE 34

The public machine has finished with Fernand. The Chamber empties around him
while he remains upright in all his decorations, abandoned because nobody has
given him a next move. The final empty doorway carries the consequence outward:
his name is gone, and Albert learns it from the street.

The page should feel vacant and procedural, not melodramatic. Fernand does not
collapse, weep, or perform remorse.

## PAGE 35

Albert forces Beauchamp to trace Haydée back to the person who placed her in the
Chamber. Beauchamp resists hurting his friend, but his final answer makes Albert
understand that the Count—the man he liked—engineered his father's destruction.

The page belongs to the instant of comprehension, not to newspaper-office
decoration or detective props.

## PAGE 36

Albert accuses the Count in a crowded public foyer and challenges him. The Count
answers with a cruel, exact distinction, accepts the glove too readily, and then
shows the smallest involuntary flicker after his pleasure.

The crowd establishes public stakes; anonymous faces and costume details are
not the event. Albert's anger and the Count's dangerous stillness are.

## PAGE 37

Haydée returns triumphant from Fernand's humiliation and discovers that the
Count cannot enjoy the victory he wanted. She is warm, moving, and certain; he
is motionless before an untouched meal, looking at a wall because Albert is
twenty-two and Haydée was eleven.

The reader should feel the moral collision without either character delivering
a philosophical speech.

## PAGE 38

Mercédès comes alone at night, strips away the Count's title with one word, and
speaks the name the whole volume has withheld: Edmond. In the vast black room,
their final close view collapses twenty-three years of distance.

The page is about recognition and history becoming intimate. It is not about
furnishing, etiquette, or decorative accuracy.

## PAGE 39

Mercédès asks Edmond not to kill Albert, defeats each practical objection, and
names the private motive beneath his revenge: he needed her to see it. Once she
says she has seen it, his closed hand opens and he agrees to stand still and
die rather than fire on her son.

The dominant relationship is two wrecked people looking directly at one
another; everything else serves that surrender.

## PAGE 40

At three in the morning, the Count methodically arranges his estate because he
expects Albert to kill him. The inventory of his nine constructed years ends at
a closed pistol case and the arithmetic he has avoided: what revenge cost and
what it bought.

The page is quiet preparation for death, not a display of legal documents or
weapon detail.

## PAGE 41

At the other end of Paris, Mercédès tells Albert the buried story of 1815 with a
disassembled pistol between them. She names Edmond Dantès, explains Danglars'
and Fernand's actions, admits she always knew, and makes Albert understand whom
he is due to shoot in four hours.

This is a mother giving her son the truth without softening it.

## PAGE 42

At dawn before witnesses, Albert publicly withdraws the duel he demanded. The
Count came prepared to stand still; instead Albert removes his hat and takes
responsibility out loud. The open pistol case and startled seconds establish
what everyone expected, but no shot is fired.

The reader turns for the reason Albert will give, not for duelling ritual.

## PAGE 43

Albert publicly states what Fernand did to Edmond Dantès, gives up his quarrel,
and refuses the Count's prepared response. Then he leaves the field with
Mercédès, takes her name, and removes both of them from the Count's frame.

The page belongs to Albert's moral choice. The Count's sudden lack of a line is
important, but the departing mother and son own the final image.

## PAGE 44

Nobody shoots the Count. Left alone on the wet field, he discovers that what he
feels is not relief. During the carriage ride home he decides Albert's mercy
changes nothing, and by the time he reaches the black room he has already set
out one glass for Fernand.

The page turns survival into a colder continuation, not a redemption.

## PAGE 45

Fernand arrives ruined and armed, demanding a duel. In his own house the Count
refuses, makes him sit, pours a glass, and drinks it empty—the first thing he has
swallowed in the volume. He explains that he never eats or drinks beneath an
enemy's roof, then states the decisive fact: this roof is his.

The empty glass is consequential because of the action, not because the reader
can inspect its exact design.

## PAGE 46

The Count reconstructs the 1815 betrayal until Fernand recognizes the trap. He
then abandons every persona and says, full-faced, “I am Edmond Dantès.” In the
narrow aftermath Fernand is gone, and so is the pistol case.

The unmasking is the page. The missing case creates the turn toward the next
page; tiny hardware or decorative detail does not.

## PAGE 47

Fernand climbs his own empty staircase carrying the case and finds a bedroom
stripped only of Mercédès and Albert: their valuables remain because they took
nothing of his. Outside, the dead house has one white upstairs window, then a
single crack.

The suicide is conveyed through architecture, absence, and sound. No body,
impact, gore, muzzle flash, or interior shooting image is needed.

## PAGE 48

Mercédès and Albert stand beside a hired carriage with one small bag, having
refused Fernand's possessions. They hear the shot, turn toward the now-dark
window, and understand. Mercédès holds Albert where he is and makes them leave
without going back inside.

The page is about her final refusal of the house and her protection of her son.

## PAGE 49

From the far pavement, the Count watches their carriage lamp recede—the last
warm thing in the volume—and counts one revenge completed. Before it has left
sight, he looks beyond the Morcerf roof toward a different house where
Villefort's lit window announces the next target.

The ending must feel chillingly continuous: Fernand's destruction cost the
Count something real, and it did not stop him.
