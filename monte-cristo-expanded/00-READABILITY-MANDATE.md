# The Count of Monte Cristo — Expanded Edition Readability Mandate

## Status

**Locked as the working direction by Andres on 2026-07-26.**

The published 32-page first edition remains intact in
[`../monte-cristo/`](../monte-cristo/). This expanded edition is a separate
adaptation pass prompted by a direct reader response: the first edition is a
great story with strong art and dramatic moments, but its pace sometimes asks
the reader to fill gaps, guess at relationships, or infer cause and effect.

This mandate governs the expanded Volume I architecture, prototype, script,
page design, editorial testing, and eventual reader.

## The Central Correction

The expanded edition will not merely add more words to the existing pages. It
will give the story enough dramatic and narrative time to become effortless to
follow.

> The reader may wonder what a secret means. The reader must never wonder
> whether a necessary piece of the story was omitted.

Readability means that a first-time reader can always understand:

1. where and when the scene is happening;
2. who the important people are;
3. what each person currently wants;
4. what information each person knows or conceals;
5. why a decision is made;
6. what changed because of that decision;
7. why the next scene follows.

## Adaptation Form

The expanded edition is a **cinematic illustrated novel**: a deliberate hybrid
of graphic novel, illustrated prose, and nearly silent visual spectacle.

Working length: **52 portrait story pages**, plus cover, end interstitial, and
quiz.

Production canvas: **1024 × 1536, 2:3 portrait**.

The portrait direction was approved by Andres on 2026-07-26 after comparison
with the strongest page architecture in the Heike project. Three monochrome
layout proofs demonstrated that it can handle opposed dialogue, illustrated
prose across time, and large-scale spectacle. The production format is now
locked. See [`04-FORMAT-PROOF-NOTES.md`](04-FORMAT-PROOF-NOTES.md).

The three principal page modes are:

### 1. Dramatic scene

- Usually three to five panels.
- Dialogue, gesture, opposition, discovery, and decision carry the page.
- Each visible speaker is staged clearly.
- Dialogue does not carry information that would sound unnatural merely
  because the reader needs it.

### 2. Illustrated prose

- One dominant illustration or a small sequence of quiet images.
- Usually 50–120 words, with 140 words as an exceptional ceiling.
- Used for time, travel, interior experience, consequence, reorientation, and
  transitions between major scenes.
- Prose occupies a designed reading field with generous margins and line
  length; it is not placed over visually busy art.
- These pages are designed as illustrated prose from the beginning. They are
  not blank comic captions created as a lettering workaround.

### 3. Spectacle or silence

- A full-width image, full-page image, or sparse cinematic sequence.
- Little or no text.
- Used for arrival, dread, discovery, escape, wonder, grief, and aftermath.
- Silence is earned by clarity in the pages before it.

The intended balance is approximately:

- 32–34 pages led by live dramatic scenes;
- 10–12 pages led by illustrated prose or prose-image combinations;
- 6–8 pages led by spectacle, silence, or visual aftermath.

Individual pages may combine modes, but each page needs one dominant reading
experience.

## Narrative Voice

The prose voice is:

- third-person and close to Edmond whenever possible;
- emotionally exact without explaining the moral in advance;
- literary but immediately comprehensible when read aloud;
- concrete about bodies, places, objects, time, and consequences;
- restrained around secrets that the drama has not earned the right to reveal.

The voice may move briefly toward Mercédès, Villefort, Morrel, Julie, or Faria
when the reader needs to experience a consequence Edmond cannot witness. Such
changes must be unmistakable and purposeful.

Narration should carry the reader **between rooms**. Dialogue should let the
reader **live inside them**.

Narration may:

- establish time and place;
- bridge travel or elapsed time;
- identify what changed between scenes;
- reveal a private belief that cannot be spoken naturally;
- remind the reader of a returning person or significant object;
- show the accumulation of prison time or education;
- make the consequence of an earlier choice explicit.

Narration may not:

- summarize a confrontation that should be dramatized;
- lecture on Restoration politics or the novel's historical importance;
- repeat what the picture and dialogue already make clear;
- announce what the reader should feel;
- turn Edmond's transformation into a list of acquired abilities;
- conceal a missing causal step beneath elegant language.

## Anti-Terse Dialogue Rule

Dialogue may be brief, but it may not omit the noun, action, relationship, or
intention the reader needs to understand the exchange.

- Consequential questions name what is being asked.
- A fragment is permitted only when the immediately preceding line and visible
  action give it one unmistakable meaning.
- Pronouns fail when more than one visible person, object, promise, or action
  could be the referent.
- Lyrical language may deepen a concrete decision; it may not replace the
  decision.
- Every balloon of five words or fewer receives an explicit ambiguity audit.
- If a cold listener asks “What?” “Who?” or “Tomorrow for what?”, the line is a
  blocker even when the writer can explain it from notes.
- Dialogue is not subject to an artificial scarcity rule. Use enough natural
  language to make motive, action, relationship, and consequence effortless;
  restage or split material before cutting necessary text or shrinking type.

The complete binding tests and Page 5 case study are in
[`19-ANTI-TERSE-DIALOGUE-MANDATE.md`](19-ANTI-TERSE-DIALOGUE-MANDATE.md).

The stricter complete-script standard is in
[`20-EFFORTLESS-STORY-CRITIC-MANDATE.md`](20-EFFORTLESS-STORY-CRITIC-MANDATE.md).
“Recoverable,” “eventually clear,” and “understandable after looking back” are
revision flags, not passing grades. A cold reader must be able to paraphrase a
page's action, motive, and change after one normal read.

## Pacing Rules

1. **Enter clearly.** The first page of a scene establishes place, important
   people, and the immediate desire or danger.
2. **Let the turn happen.** Important decisions receive enough panels and
   dialogue to feel chosen rather than reported.
3. **Stay for the consequence.** Betrayal, death, revelation, and rescue receive
   reaction or aftermath before the plot moves on.
4. **Do not confuse compression with momentum.** A quick succession of major
   events can feel slower than a clearly motivated sequence because the reader
   must stop and reconstruct it.
5. **One dominant turn per page.** A page may contain several beats, but the
   reader should know what changed.
6. **Reintroduce naturally.** A returning character is identified through name,
   relationship, behavior, or context before the plot depends on recognition.
7. **Make plans legible.** When Edmond uses a disguise, document, debt, or
   intermediary, show what he wants, what step he is taking, and the visible
   result.
8. **Protect emotional duration.** Do not place grief, ingenious mechanics, and
   the next major revelation on the same page.

## Portrait Page Grammar

- Use one dominant image or panel receiving roughly 45–70% of the page's visual
  attention.
- Use no more than two small reaction insets unless causality requires a
  sequential strip.
- Build a clear top-to-bottom reading path.
- Wide internal panels remain available for ships, harbors, offices, tables,
  prison cells, islands, and opposed speakers.
- Use vertical accumulation for time, consequence, descent, isolation, and
  emotional aftermath.
- Do not mix portrait and landscape story canvases inside the expanded reader.
- The landscape first edition remains a separate completed work.

## Character-Clarity Rules

The conspiracy must never collapse into “three jealous men.”

- **Danglars** believes Edmond has been given the command and future that should
  have been his.
- **Fernand** wants Mercédès and treats her choice as an obstacle he can remove.
- **Caderousse** recognizes the wrong, but repeatedly chooses the comfort of not
  being the person who stops it.
- **Villefort** knows Edmond is innocent and sacrifices him to protect his name,
  marriage, and career from the fact that Noirtier is his father.

Faria must not arrive as a solution machine. The reader needs to experience:

- his wit and impatience;
- the failed tunnel that creates the partnership;
- the ordinary work through which trust grows;
- teaching as pleasure before it becomes power;
- disagreement over anger, knowledge, judgment, and escape;
- his physical decline without loss of dignity;
- Edmond becoming his son before being named his heir.

After the treasure, every disguise must have a clear dramatic function:

- **Abbé Busoni** asks questions that Edmond cannot ask as himself.
- **Lord Wilmore** moves money and institutions while concealing gratitude.
- The reader should understand that each identity is Edmond's deliberate tool,
  not a newly introduced character.

## Adaptation Freedom

Readability and emotional truth outrank chapter-by-chapter fidelity.

The expanded edition may:

- combine minor characters;
- reorder discoveries;
- invent bridge dialogue and private moments;
- let the reader understand a mechanism earlier than the source does;
- repeat names and relationships naturally;
- simplify legal, political, maritime, or financial processes;
- enlarge an emotional beat while compressing a transaction;
- preserve a character's dramatic function without preserving every incident.

The edition must preserve:

- the distinct moral responsibility of Danglars, Fernand, Caderousse, and
  Villefort;
- Edmond's honorable innocence;
- Mercédès as an active chooser;
- Faria as friend, teacher, and surrogate father;
- death and escape as emotionally inseparable;
- the treasure as Faria's final act of trust;
- Morrel's rescue as Edmond's first great use of power;
- the restored *Pharaon* as the volume's final image and moral fork.

## Text and Page Production

- Every expanded-edition finished canvas is **1024 × 1536, 2:3 portrait**.
- Never generate or approve a landscape or square expanded-edition story page.
- The reader remains navigation and quiz infrastructure, not a story-lettering
  layer.
- Every delivered page is a single finished, flattened image.
- Dialogue pages retain native, integrated speech balloons and are rejected for
  wrong wording, speaker ownership, or ambiguous tails.
- Illustrated-prose pages use intentional editorial typography in a stable
  prose field. They do not imitate speech balloons or retrofit captions onto an
  already composed comic page.
- Velvet Cinema remains the graphic direction.
- Existing first-edition images are references and possible reuse candidates,
  not constraints on the new pacing.

## Acceptance Test

After every movement, a cold reader should be able to answer without coaching:

- Who acted?
- What did that person want?
- What choice was made?
- Why was it made?
- Who was affected?
- What does Edmond now believe?
- Why are we going to the next scene?

A page or movement fails if the correct response depends on information present
only in planning notes, source knowledge, or the reader's guess.

## Prototype Gate

No expanded-edition full page run begins until a continuous prototype proves
the hybrid form across Pages 12–18 of the working architecture:

> Villefort's world → Edmond's examination → Noirtier's name → Villefort's
> choice → the night transfer → Château d'If.

The prototype must demonstrate:

- effortless political and relational clarity;
- natural alternation between prose, dialogue, and spectacle;
- accurate, comfortable text at normal reader size;
- visible cause and effect across page turns;
- correct character identity and speech attribution;
- 1024 × 1536 portrait output on every page;
- a final arrival at Château d'If that feels inevitable and terrifying.

Only after that sequence passes should the complete script and image run
proceed.
