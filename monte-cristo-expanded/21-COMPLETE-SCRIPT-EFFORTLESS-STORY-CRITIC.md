# The Count of Monte Cristo — Complete Script Effortless-Story Critic

## Round 1 verdict

**NOT APPROVED.**

The complete script has a strong, coherent story spine. A first-time reader can
follow Edmond's homecoming, the distinct responsibilities of the four
betrayers, Villefort's choice, the Faria relationship, the escape, the two
disguises, and Morrel's rescue. The new Page 8 is a major improvement: it uses
one accusation, preserves the letter from Elba, and makes all three men's
choices visible.

Approval is withheld because several passages still ask the reader to decode
compressed language, accept information that a character could not yet know,
or absorb multiple major emotional turns without enough duration. Those are
binding failures under the anti-terse and effortless-story mandates, not a
request for decorative polish.

The central Round 1 diagnosis is:

1. **The plot is generally clear; individual lines sometimes are not.** The
   weakest dialogue still sounds like an outline, a thesis, or an elegant
   slogan instead of a person completing a thought.
2. **Most pages have good page intelligence.** The principal structural
   exceptions are Page 40 and Pages 44–45, which compress too much lived story
   into too little emotional time.
3. **One knowledge leak is a hard causal error.** On Page 32, Edmond identifies
   Noirtier as Villefort's father even though no scene has given Edmond that
   information.
4. **The prose/dialogue division is not always being used to best effect.**
   Page 38 makes Edmond recite escape mechanics in fragments; Pages 46 and 48
   make characters explain institutional procedure that narration could carry
   more naturally.

The clarity benchmark is *Olympians* by George O'Connor only in the sense
specified by the mandate: a reader should move forward on the first read, not
stop to repair an antecedent, decode an aphorism, or reconstruct a mechanism.

## Approval-blocking findings

### B1 — Page 4 does not identify Mercédès promptly, and its final exchange hides the question

**Quoted text**

> **Fernand:** “He has been gone for months. The sea may keep him for years.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Must I ask the question too?”
>
> **Mercédès:** “Only if you have forgotten the answer.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The woman who becomes the story's central love is not named in the exact page
text. The page begins with an unsupported `He`, and its concluding “the
question” could mean “Do you still choose me?”, “Will you marry me?”, or “Have
you waited for me?” The planning notes know the answer; the reader should not
need them. Page 5 then asks the explicit marriage question, making Page 4's
unnamed question even harder to assign.

**Prescriptive repair**

- Name both absent people in the spoken confrontation. For example: “Mercédès,
  Edmond has been gone for months. The sea may keep him for years.”
- Make Edmond's returning question concrete and distinct from Page 5's timing
  question. For example: “Do you still want the life we planned?” / “I waited
  because I still want it.”
- Keep Mercédès's refusal of Fernand intact; it is the page's strongest beat.

### B2 — Page 5 retains outline-like fragments in the scene meant to make the future tangible

**Quoted text**

> **Edmond:** “Captain's wages. A home with room for Father. No more empty
> cupboard.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The nouns are identifiable, but the line sounds like three planning bullets.
This is the exact kind of technically understandable terseness the new mandate
rejects. The page's emotional job is to let the reader inhabit a shared future;
Edmond should sound as though he is telling Mercédès what they can now do.

**Prescriptive repair**

Use a complete spoken thought, such as:

> “With a captain's wages, we can find a home with room for Father. He will
> never face an empty cupboard again.”

The approved proposal exchange — “Will you marry me tomorrow?” / “Tomorrow—and
every day after.” — should remain.

### B3 — Page 6 gives three characters expository soliloquies instead of dramatizing or narrating their convergence

**Quoted text**

> **Danglars:** “Nineteen—and Morrel gives him the ship.”
>
> **Fernand:** “Tomorrow, she marries him.”
>
> **Caderousse:** “One drink. Then none of this concerns me.”

**First-time-reader failure**

All three men appear alone and state their motive aloud for the reader. The
information is clear, but the speech does not sound like live conversation.
Caderousse's line is especially authorial: he announces the exact moral failure
the adaptation wants us to assign him.

**Prescriptive repair**

Make this an illustrated-prose convergence page and reserve speech for the
table. A workable orientation field would be:

> “Danglars had expected the captaincy now promised to Edmond. Fernand knew
> Mercédès would marry Edmond tomorrow. Caderousse saw what their anger was
> becoming and chose a chair, a cup, and the comforting lie that none of it
> concerned him.”

The images can then silently bring all three to one table. If dialogue is
retained, give each man a real interlocutor rather than three parallel
self-explanations.

### B4 — Page 7 explains the conspiracy as compressed procedure

**Quoted text**

> **Caderousse:** “Stop. That sentence already has a crime in it.”
>
> **Danglars:** “A sailor visits Elba. A dead captain gives him a letter. In
> times like these, paper can open a prison.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The second line is a three-sentence synopsis disguised as speech. “Paper can
open a prison” is clever but indirect: a cold reader has to translate it into
“a false accusation could make the police imprison Edmond.” Caderousse's line
also sounds like the author labeling the exchange rather than a frightened man
trying to stop it.

**Prescriptive repair**

Let Danglars state the proposed action and consequence in ordinary language:

> “Edmond stopped at Elba and carried a letter for the dead captain. If the
> police believe he serves Napoleon, they will imprison him without Fernand
> laying a hand on him.”

Caderousse can answer from fear and conscience:

> “That would send an innocent man to prison. Put the pen down.”

Page 8 can then dramatize whether anyone actually stops the accusation.

### B5 — The Page 8 → Page 9 transition calls a pre-wedding celebration a wedding feast

**Quoted text**

> **Page 8 narration:** “Before Edmond's wedding feast had ended, Fernand
> placed the accusation in official hands.”
>
> **Page 9 guest:** “A captain and a wedding in two days!”
>
> **Edmond:** “One day. The wedding first.”

**First-time-reader failure**

Page 8 says the wedding feast is already occurring; Page 9 says the wedding is
tomorrow. A reader cannot tell whether Edmond is arrested before, during, or
after the wedding. This is a factual continuity error, not a subtlety.

**Prescriptive repair**

Change the Page 8 narration to “Before Edmond's betrothal feast had ended...”
or “Before that evening's celebration had ended...” Keep Page 9's explicit
one-day timing.

### B6 — Page 11 ends Edmond's trusting walk on another compressed mantra

**Quoted text**

> **Edmond:** “An answer. Then home.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The prose already explains what Edmond believes, but the spoken fragment makes
him sound like a production outline. It omits who will answer what and what
must happen before he goes home.

**Prescriptive repair**

Use the complete belief:

> “I will answer his questions, and then I will go home.”

### B7 — Page 12 transmits Villefort's political backstory through dialogue that has no immediate human reason to occur

**Quoted text**

> **Marquise:** “Your father still serves Napoleon.”
>
> **Villefort:** “Noirtier chose his side. I chose the King—and your
> daughter.”
>
> **Official:** “A sailor arrested at his betrothal. Accused of carrying a
> Bonapartist letter from Elba.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The prose field already orients the reader. The Marquise then repeats a known
family fact without a present demand, and the official includes the
prisoner's betrothal because the adaptation wants to parallel Edmond and
Villefort, not because it belongs in a case briefing. The information is clear,
but the people momentarily become narrators of the outline.

**Prescriptive repair**

- Give the Marquise an immediate stake: before Renée bears Villefort's name,
  she wants his word that Noirtier's politics will not enter their home.
- Let Villefort answer that demand: “My father chose Napoleon. I chose the
  King—and Renée. His politics will not touch us.”
- Have the official brief the actual case: “A prisoner is waiting in your
  office. Edmond Dantès, a sailor accused of carrying a Bonapartist letter from
  Elba.”

This preserves every necessary fact while making the exchange a live test of
Villefort's desired future.

### B8 — Pages 19–20 make institutions speak in epigrams and make Villefort dictate a file note to himself

**Quoted text**

> **Governor:** “The register does not need to meet you. A prosecutor signed
> it.”
>
> **Morrel:** “This red purse can do both. Eat today. I will petition again
> tomorrow.”
>
> **Villefort, alone:** “Dangerous. Hold without contact.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The governor's metaphor delays the simple fact that he will not reopen a
prosecutor's order. Morrel speaks the motif aloud instead of directly offering
food and help. Most importantly, Villefort appears to say a clipped file
annotation to himself; the exact page text does not establish whether it is
speech, dictation, or document text.

**Prescriptive repair**

- Governor: “I will not reopen a prosecutor's order because a prisoner denies
  it.”
- Morrel: “Take the purse, Monsieur Dantès. You must eat while I petition again
  tomorrow.”
- Restage Page 20 Panel 4 with a clerk so Villefort can say, “Mark the file:
  dangerous Bonapartist. No visitors or correspondence.” Alternatively, put
  the instruction in a protected typeset document field and do not assign it a
  speech tail.

### B9 — Pages 21–22 obscure Edmond's decision and his rejection of “tomorrow”

**Quoted text**

> “If the prison meant to take his life, he would not help it take one more
> obedient day.”
>
> **Jailer:** “Tomorrow you will beg.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Tomorrow is your word.”

**First-time-reader failure**

Refusing food does help the prison take Edmond's life, so the narration's logic
has to be unpacked: Edmond means to reclaim the choice of when he dies rather
than submit indefinitely. “Tomorrow is your word” then asks the reader to
decode a motif when Edmond's despair should be immediate.

**Prescriptive repair**

Make both ideas explicit:

> “At last, he pushed away his food. If the prison intended to keep him until
> he died, he would choose the day himself.”

and

> “I stopped believing in tomorrow years ago.”

The scrape can then interrupt a fully understood decision.

### B10 — Page 26 conceals the object of Edmond's most important emotional statement

**Quoted text**

> **Faria:** “You learn patience quickly.”
>
> **Edmond:** “No. I learned what I refuse to lose.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The reader must choose whether Edmond refuses to lose time, the tunnel, hope,
freedom, or Faria. The page record says the intended answer is their earned
trust, but the exact line withholds that object for effect.

**Prescriptive repair**

Name the relationship:

> “No. I learned that I refuse to lose you.”

If that feels too intimate this early, use “I learned that your life matters
more than one hour of digging.” Either version lets the bond be felt without a
riddle.

### B11 — Page 30 opens and closes on missing objects

**Quoted text**

> **Edmond:** “Tell me who did it.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Now we ask the dangerous question.”

**First-time-reader failure**

At the page opening, `it` could mean his arrest, the accusation, the detention
order, or the loss of his entire life. At the close, the “dangerous question”
is not named even though the next page depends on it. Both lines make the
reader supply consequential language.

**Prescriptive repair**

Use:

> “Tell me who sent me to prison.”

and end on the actual next inquiry:

> “Now we ask which of them profited when I vanished.”

### B12 — Page 32 gives Edmond knowledge he never acquired

**Quoted text**

> **Faria:** “Noirtier served Napoleon.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Villefort's father.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The reader learned on Page 12 that Noirtier is Villefort's father, but Edmond
was not present. On Page 15 Villefort denied knowing Noirtier and never revealed
the relationship. Page 32 therefore leaks reader-only information into
Edmond's mouth. The page notes call this a fact Edmond “personally witnessed,”
but the script does not support that claim.

**Prescriptive repair**

Give the discovery to Faria, whose learning plausibly includes the public
family connection:

> **Faria:** “Noirtier? I know that name. He serves Napoleon—and he is
> Villefort's father.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Then the letter could expose the family Villefort was hiding.”

Faria can then connect that exposure to Villefort's royalist marriage and
career. This turns the page into a genuine revelation for Edmond rather than a
continuity error.

### B13 — Page 33's revenge language is too abstract to understand without interpretation

**Quoted text**

> **Edmond:** “Fernand wanted my love. I will leave him none.”
>
> **Faria:** “And if an innocent person stands beside them?”
>
> **Edmond:** “I strike around them.”

**First-time-reader failure**

“Leave him none” could mean destroy Fernand's marriage, kill Mercédès, make
Fernand unloved, or take back Mercédès. “I strike around them” does not say how
Edmond would protect an innocent person. The page's moral boundary is crucial,
but its language asks the reader to translate metaphor into intention.

**Prescriptive repair**

Let Edmond name what he feels before Faria tests it. For example:

> “Danglars stole my command and my future. I want him to lose everything he
> built from that theft.”
>
> “Fernand took my place beside Mercédès. I want him to feel what he did to
> me.”
>
> “Villefort buried me to protect his own name. I want the truth to expose
> him.”

Then make the innocent-person answer honest rather than magically controlled:

> **Faria:** “And if punishing them hurts people who had no part in your
> imprisonment?”
>
> **Edmond:** “Then I must find another way.”
>
> **Faria:** “You cannot always control where pain spreads.”

The existing final principle — protect the innocent — can follow.

### B14 — Page 36 compresses Faria's governing moral lesson into a slogan

**Quoted text**

> **Faria:** “Knowledge first. Treasure second. Judgment last.”
>
> **Faria:** “Then do not remain.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The page record interprets these fragments as “use knowledge before wealth”
and “delay judgment until anger does not choose it,” but those meanings are not
fully present in the exact words. “Do not remain” also omits “in this prison” at
the moment it becomes Faria's final instruction to escape. Page 42 later repeats
`Judgment last`, so the underwritten slogan becomes a book-level motif.

**Prescriptive repair**

Let Faria finish the lesson:

> “Use what you know before you use the treasure. And do not pass judgment
> while anger is choosing for you.”

and

> “Then do not remain in this prison. Live, my son.”

Update Page 42's remembered line to a short but complete excerpt, such as:
“Do not judge while anger chooses for you.”

### B15 — Pages 38–39 make a crucial escape mechanism depend on clipped self-instructions

**Quoted text**

> **Edmond:** “Your bed. My cell. They will find you where they expect me.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Knife. Seam. Rope. Air.”
>
> **Edmond:** “Not yet.”

**First-time-reader failure**

“Your bed. My cell.” is especially difficult: Edmond means that he will carry
Faria into Edmond's cell and lay him in Edmond's bed, but the words can be read
as placing Faria's bed in Edmond's cell. The checklist requires the reader to
infer the entire plan. On Page 39, “Not yet” does not name the remaining danger
— the weight still tied to Edmond. This is the book's most intricate physical
sequence and should be its clearest.

**Prescriptive repair**

Use narration for procedure and Edmond's voice for grief and choice. For
example:

> “Edmond carried Faria through the tunnel into his own cell and laid him in
> the bed. Then he returned to Faria's cell and climbed into the empty burial
> sack, keeping the knife in his hand.”

Edmond's live lines can be:

> “Forgive me. This is the only door left.”

and, once in the sack:

> “I cut the seam, then the rope, and swim for air.”

On Page 39 replace “Not yet” with “The weight is still tied to me” or “Cut the
rope before it pulls me under.” The visual sequence can remain spectacular,
but the causal chain will no longer depend on tiny rope detail or shorthand.

### B16 — Page 40 compresses rescue, concealment, recovery, friendship, proof of skill, and acceptance into one page

**Quoted text**

> “For weeks Edmond hauled sail, read weather, shared watches, and earned the
> place his false name could not buy.”
>
> **Lookout:** “Customs sail!”
>
> **Jacopo:** “Welcome aboard, friend.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The page is intelligible, but it is only “recoverable” emotionally. Jacopo
saves Edmond, the captain suspects him, weeks pass, Edmond proves himself in a
chase, and Jacopo names him friend within six panels. The reader receives the
fact of a bond rather than enough ordinary time to feel it. This is exactly the
rushed quality the expanded edition is meant to correct.

**Prescriptive repair**

Split the material into two pages, even if the book grows beyond 52 pages:

1. **Rescue and recovery:** Jacopo pulls Edmond from the water; the captain
   challenges his story; Jacopo gives him clothes, food, and repeated care;
   Edmond earns a provisional berth through ordinary seamanship. End on the
   first voluntary exchange of names or trust.
2. **A place aboard:** Let weeks of shared work lead to the customs danger.
   Edmond's maneuver saves the ship; “Welcome aboard, friend” becomes the
   consequence rather than the summary.

At the start of the following island page, add explicit orientation:

> “Weeks later, the *Jeune-Amélie* anchored at Monte Cristo.”

Without that line, Page 41 never names the island in exact image text and asks
the reader to infer that the goats and eastern shore belong to Faria's map.

### B17 — Page 43 contains a literal logic error in its explanation of disguise

**Quoted text**

> “Guilty men might lie to someone they believed dead, and officials might
> refuse to reopen his case.”

**First-time-reader failure**

A person believed dead cannot stand before the guilty men asking questions.
The intended idea is that appearing as Edmond would reveal his survival and
make them hide what they know.

**Prescriptive repair**

Use:

> “If he returned as Edmond Dantès, guilty men would hide what they knew, and
> officials might refuse to reopen his case.”

The rest of the page clearly distinguishes Busoni's confessional purpose from
Wilmore's institutional purpose.

### B18 — Pages 44–45 rush Louis's death, Morrel's kindness, the full table confession, Mercédès's survival, and two men's rise

**Quoted text**

> **Caderousse:** “Morrel found him too late. He brought food in the red purse,
> but Louis was already dying.”
>
> **Busoni:** “Louis died?”
>
> **Caderousse:** “Calling for Edmond.”
>
> **Busoni:** “And you knew why Edmond had vanished.”
>
> **Busoni, next page:** “Mercédès?”

**First-time-reader failure**

The revelation of Edmond's father's death receives one reaction balloon before
the interrogation resumes and Caderousse confesses all three conspirators'
roles. The next page immediately covers Mercédès's years of waiting, her
marriage, Danglars's banking fortune, Fernand's military rise, Caderousse's
excuse, and the diamond. The facts are clear, but the reader is not allowed to
live through the two losses. Page 44 alone has three dominant turns: Louis is
dead, Morrel remained loyal, and Caderousse confirms the conspiracy.

The one-word queries “Louis died?”, “Mercédès?”, and “Danglars?” are not equal.
“Louis died?” directly answers an explicit statement and is valid. “Mercédès?”
and “Danglars?” open consequential inquiries without saying what Busoni asks.

**Prescriptive repair**

Expand this interview rather than packing more text into the existing pages:

1. End the Louis/Morrel page on “Calling for Edmond” and a sustained silent
   Busoni reaction. Do not move immediately to accusation.
2. Give the table confession its own dominant turn: Caderousse states that
   Danglars wrote the accusation, Fernand delivered it, and he chose not to
   stop them; Busoni makes him face that choice.
3. Give Mercédès's survival its own clearly elapsed time. Open with “What
   became of Mercédès?” and state that she waited for years and married only
   after everyone believed Edmond dead. “Surviving is not betrayal” can remain
   as Edmond's humane conclusion.
4. Ask “What became of Danglars and Fernand?” before summarizing their rise.

This likely requires one or two added pages. Added pages are preferable to
shrinking Louis, Mercédès, and Edmond's reactions into plot delivery.

### B19 — Pages 46 and 48 explain institutional mechanics through dialogue between people who already understand them

**Quoted text**

> **Clerk:** “The English gentleman seeks a prisoner's record?”
>
> **Wilmore:** “Closed is not the same as unread.”
>
> **Banker:** “Then Morrel will owe your firm.”
>
> **Wilmore:** “And the owner of a debt may demand payment—or mark it paid.”

**First-time-reader failure**

The archive exchange sounds like a caricatured official encounter. In the bank
scene, Wilmore explains debt ownership to a banker solely so the reader can
hear it. This is precisely the “exposition disguised as speech” test in the
mandate. The financial mechanism matters, but the human choice is simply that
Edmond buys every debt so he can cancel it.

**Prescriptive repair**

- Archive: “You want Edmond Dantès's prison record?” / “Yes. The accusation
  and every order attached to it.” / “The file is closed.” / “I have authority
  to inspect it.” Let prose or the visible card carry the simplified access
  mechanism.
- Bank: move the legal explanation into concise narration. Let the actual
  transaction be direct: “Buy every note Morrel owes and transfer them to
  Thomson & French.” / “Then your firm controls the debt.” / “At eleven,
  cancel it all.”
- Replace Edmond's “Memory did not invent them” with the concrete conclusion:
  “I remembered correctly. Danglars wrote the accusation, and Villefort signed
  the order.”

### B20 — Page 50 creates unresolved diamond continuity

**Quoted text**

> **Page 44:** “A dying prisoner left this diamond for Edmond Dantès's
> friends.”
>
> **Page 45:** “The diamond waits until the truth is finished.”
>
> **Page 50 note:** “The diamond is for Julie.”

**First-time-reader failure**

Only one diamond has been established. The script never shows whether
Caderousse receives it after finishing the truth, but Page 50 then puts “the
diamond” in Julie's purse. A reader can reasonably think the same object was
silently taken from Caderousse and reassigned, or that a second diamond appeared
without introduction. The object is not needed to understand the debt rescue,
but the definite wording makes it a continuity question.

**Prescriptive repair**

Choose and state one version:

- simplest: remove the diamond from Julie's note; or
- resolve Busoni's diamond on the confession page, then call Julie's object “a
  separate diamond for Julie's future.”

Do not leave the object to be reconciled from source-novel knowledge.

## Remaining blocker R2-B2 — The diamond's stated purpose contradicts its recipient

**Quoted text**

> **Busoni, Page 45:** “A dying prisoner left this diamond for the people who
> were faithful to Edmond Dantès.”
>
> **Busoni, Page 46:** “Fear explains why you stayed silent. It does not make
> you innocent.”
>

**First-time-reader failure**

The physical chain now passes: one Busoni diamond is shown throughout and is
visibly transferred to Caderousse; the red purse contains no diamond. But the
reason for the transfer does not pass. Busoni introduces the jewel as an
inheritance for people who were faithful, proves that Caderousse knowingly

for fidelity. For example:

> **Busoni:** “A dying prisoner left this diamond for whoever could tell me

>
> **Caderousse:** “Then ask your questions.”

> **Busoni:** “You have told me the truth. Take it.”
