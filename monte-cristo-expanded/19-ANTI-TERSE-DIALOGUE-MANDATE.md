# The Count of Monte Cristo — Anti-Terse Dialogue Mandate

## Status

**Locked by Andres on 2026-08-01.** This mandate governs every future script
revision, page prompt, generated page, and critic pass.

The reader should never have to supply the missing noun, action, relationship,
or intention merely because a fragment sounds cinematic.

> Brevity is welcome. Incompleteness is not.

There is no requirement to minimize dialogue. The adaptation may use more
words, more balloons, a larger prose field, a restaged panel, or an additional
page when that is what effortless comprehension requires. Never cut the words
that make motive, action, relationship, or consequence clear merely to make a
page look economical.

## The problem

Terse dialogue becomes bad dialogue when compression removes the grammatical
or dramatic information that tells the reader what a line means.

The rejected Page 5 exchange exposed the failure:

> **Edmond:** “Tomorrow?”
> **Mercédès:** “For the wedding—and everything after.”

`Tomorrow?` has no stated object. It could mean the wedding, the house, moving
in, the captaincy, or the beginning of their life together. The reply is
lyrical, but it asks the reader to reconstruct the actual question.

The approved replacement is:

> **Edmond:** “Will you marry me tomorrow?”
> **Mercédès:** “Tomorrow—and every day after.”

The new version keeps the tenderness and rhythm while making the action and
commitment explicit.

## Binding rules

1. **Give important questions an object.** A question that causes a choice,
   plan, promise, revelation, or page turn must name what is being asked.
2. **Do not float fragments.** A one- or two-word line may not carry necessary
   plot logic unless its meaning is uniquely determined by the immediately
   preceding line and visible action.
3. **Name the action.** Prefer `Will you marry me tomorrow?` to `Tomorrow?`,
   `Did Fernand send it?` to `Him?`, and `Do you want me to stay?` to `Stay?`
   when the action matters.
4. **Protect antecedents.** Pronouns such as `it`, `him`, `her`, `this`, and
   `that` fail when more than one visible person, object, promise, or action
   could be their referent.
5. **Do not use poetry to conceal logistics.** A lyrical reply may deepen a
   concrete decision; it may not replace the decision.
6. **Do not confuse speed with omission.** Adding three or four clarifying
   words is better than making the reader pause and reconstruct the scene.
7. **Let characters finish consequential thoughts.** Invitations, accusations,
   bargains, refusals, plans, and promises should normally contain a subject,
   meaningful verb, and object or complement.
8. **Read the exchange aloud without notes.** If a listener asks “Tomorrow for
   what?” or “Who is ‘he’?”, the dialogue fails even if the writer can explain
   it.
9. **Do not ration useful text.** A complete natural sentence outranks a terse
   fragment. If the complete exchange no longer fits comfortably, restage or
   split the material; never shrink type or remove causal language.

## Short lines that remain welcome

Short dialogue is powerful when the setup makes its meaning singular:

- `Father!` works because Edmond is visibly embracing Louis.
- `Never.` works because it directly answers a complete question in the same
  panel.
- `I am.` works because the officer has just asked which man is Edmond Dantès.
- `Tomorrow.` works as an answer to `Will you marry me tomorrow?`

The same words fail when the reader must invent the missing question or choose
among several possible referents.

## Page and script test

For every balloon of five words or fewer, the writer and both critics must ask:

- Is this a complete thought, or an intentional answer to a complete thought?
- Can it refer to only one visible person, object, action, time, or promise?
- Would a first-time listener understand it without seeing the script notes?
- Is the brevity emotionally forceful, or merely compressed?

Any necessary line that fails one of these tests is a **BLOCKER** and must be
rewritten before page generation or approval.

## Complete-script critic gate

Do not begin this gate until Andres explicitly says his Pages 1–10 reader QA is
complete.

After that signal, the script critic must audit every exact-image line on Pages
1–55 before further production. Earlier approval does not exempt Pages 1–10
because the reader's Page 8 finding established a stricter standard. The critic
must:

- flag every consequential fragment with a missing action, object, antecedent,
  relationship, or intention;
- inspect every balloon of five words or fewer, while also catching longer
  lines that remain evasive or underwritten;
- read exchanges aloud as dialogue rather than judging isolated literary
  phrases;
- assume the reader has never read the novel and has no access to production
  notes;
- recommend fully written natural dialogue without treating word count as a
  virtue;
- allow added words, balloons, panels, prose, or pages when clarity and
  emotional duration require them;
- withhold approval until no necessary exchange asks the reader to reconstruct
  omitted meaning.

The critic must also enforce
[`20-EFFORTLESS-STORY-CRITIC-MANDATE.md`](20-EFFORTLESS-STORY-CRITIC-MANDATE.md).
It must produce a report, the script must be revised from that report, and the
same critic must re-audit the revision until the complete script is approved.
